# Chapter Summaries

Rolling window of the most recent twenty to thirty chapters, in full detail, for voice and continuity. Older entries are archived by volume.

## Volume 01 — *The Bellweather Forecast* (Chapters 1–48)

### Chapter 0001 — The Shuffled Plate

Day four, 21:40, the Weatherglass maintenance shop. Marek renders the Ninth Ward tower's cracked feed plate (serial 4471, from Pell's four-year-old T-4 bundle) and finds the Six Movements arriving out of order: Gather and Break where Weight belongs, a doubled Route, Release unfinished, Witness in the last third. He ruins one plate, is floored by the bench, gets a bent needle, and works out that three air masses are arriving inside one cycle, which is why the ridge's instruments disagree by six hours. He reads the cracked plate a second time cold, then cuts and edges a sound nine-inch replacement out of Office stock, sets it under the oilcloth for Malle's inspection, and notes that it is an ordinary repair that corrects no bias and will be nine minutes of praise in three days. He does not write the shuffle into the chart-room register. He writes time, plate number, tower and the movement order in pencil on the inside of his left cuff — *21:40, cut 22:05, G · Wt B · R R · Rel— · Wt, Rel began and did not finish*, which decodes directly against the trace he read and promises himself, twice, that if he is wrong he will say it out loud to a person. He clears the backlog — a fatigued aneroid, a slug-eaten rain-gauge float, Feth Raal's barometer for free — and finds two faint ghost needles standing off her true needle. Ends with Feth, at her stall, reporting men in good coats asking after narrow forecasts and a second version of any figure. **Read only; nothing spent, and no metallic taste — that arrives with the Chapter 5 binding and is new there. The cuff note is the first thing Marek has ever kept off the record.**

### Chapter 0002 — Two Versions of One Notice

Day five, 06:00, Kestrel Yards, Tarin's POV. Ivo Slate of the Cloud Exchange offers a sealed, licensed, insured Office window for the same passage at half her rate, backed by a Bellweather figure the Office sold four days ago under a standard secrecy clause to four carriers, a grain factor and two insurers — and the Office does not publish. Ilva Sorn makes the true arithmetic: the Line's published uncertainty is its only currency and the yard cannot keep spending it. Nessa Tarrow, the night watch, copies the Office window and Tarin's counter-reading onto one sheet with both hours. Tarin declines the window, flies the *Petrel* on her own nine-year-old box and her own reading, gets the passage right four hours early, and loses a month of the *Petrel*'s overhead because the Nacre factor took the Exchange window at 06:40 for its insurance. She posts all three versions on the Line's board. Slate closes his little book and thanks her for publishing both, because his agency report will cite the yard board as evidence that the Office's window was understated at the time of sale.

### Chapter 0003 — What a Ward Cannot Read

Day six, 10:00 to 18:00, the Weatherglass and Pell's glassworks. The Ninth Ward feed plate Marek **cut on the Monday night and fitted on the Wednesday afternoon** reads clean, so the shuffle is in the air and only cracked glass had been positioned to show it. He has no standing and Malle's day is a solid block of watch assignments; Malle's only requirement is "bring me something with an instrument behind it." The only good glass in the district is the Ninth Ward's paid-for replacement pane, serial 4486, quoted at the end of last year, paid for, never collected, and sitting in Pell's shop ever since — a pane the ward paid for and has never had, and cutting it does not darken the frame, which has held a bubbled temporary for a fortnight. What it destroys is the only good glass that district was ever going to be sent. **He did not take a working public notice; he destroyed a paid-for one. Do not conflate the two.** Pell refuses, then names the price: Marek does every piece of glass work that tower has coming for two years, at his own hand, no charge to the ward, and Pell keeps the balance. The tower crew come at half past midday and Marek tells them in the doorway what he has done and why; the crewman enters it on a docket as glasswork owed to the Ninth Ward. On the stairs, Malle asks the first question nobody has ever asked him — *if you are right, what would I see that would prove it* — and Marek answers truthfully and reveals nothing: Release before Witness in the second turn, visible on the ward's own glass, which is why he needs to be on the tower. At six he reaches the ward ledger bench, and Tovan Iles tells him to come at nine, and not with a tube.

### Chapter 0004 — The Bell and the Pencil

Day seven, 09:15, the ward ledger bench, Tovan's POV. Fourteen unattached observations on the rack and the watch bell an hour off. Marek brings an order of movements with no place, time or effect, so the bench has no drawer for it. Tovan teaches the rule as a job: an observation is not a promise; an observation nobody acted on cannot later be compelled to mean anything. She names the emergency-ledger rule — a public claim touching a route, water system, hospital, school or market must be entered inside one cycle — and reveals the ledger has one line in it from before her time, about a ferry. She tells Marek about a claim on her own bench **two winters ago**: a good one, place and time and effect and a bell and a sponsor, which **the district read off the bell and refused** in favour of its own published plan, so that nothing happened and no record anywhere says so. (This is *not* Neris Kade's Chapter 8 story, and the two must never be merged: Tovan's is a district refusing a claim; Neris's is a shelter rota that changed after she acted on an unsigned number.) She stamps his plate as an unattached observation and writes his name on the source tab, which nobody ever wants. Off the record she asks whether a reading nobody acted on counts as having predicted anything; he answers that a reading never acted on is a reading never tested, and she hears the evasion and lets it stand. The bell goes at ten and the ninth ward's head does nothing at all. Anwen Sarr, forty-six, arrives with three others at five past ten, and the chapter ends with Tovan seeing that only a claim will get them anything.

### Chapter 0005 — The Third Watch

Day seven, 10:05 to 00:20, the Weatherglass office, the public forecast hall, and the ward ledger bench. Marek's structural turn. An unconditional claim needs an instrument chain and he has none on Hearthward, so the only shape available is a conditional one, which puts the decision with the ward. He states the claim's whole reach, including the part he has never seen: the lower row drains clear. The water steward tells him the rain-garden crew clear the drains Tuesdays and Thursdays, and says she has been telling Ferren for three years without being asked. Ferren is standing in the room and Marek does not turn round, does not ask where he went, and does not ask what the last time looked like; he tells Ferren to clear them tonight and Ferren says he will. The alternative is written into the sheet (open the lower outfall in the second hour and take the market's ground floor), the expiry is the third watch, the sponsor is Marek. Malle allows it and says the name on it will be Marek's and not the ward's. The single sequence-glass panel appears at the bench, immediately before the entry, quoting the claim's own effect and warning that one possible turn will close. **On the bench, with his hand flat on the glass, Marek also sees the plant for Chapter 7: a dead stretch in the middle of the moisture trace with nothing in it at all, the moisture returning on a shallow slope and late. He cannot make his eyes leave it, and he puts it down to the lamp.** This is the batch's only look at the gap before the moral climax, and it is closed-limited POV — it must not pre-empt Chapter 6, and the earlier flash-forward about six rooms under water has been cut. Tovan enters it, strikes the bell twice — once for the yards' ward head, because the terraces' upper tanks feed the Kestrel freshwater header, once for Hearthward — and writes the first entry in the emergency ledger in four years. Two ward heads come up and stay up, clear, for Hearthward and the yards; the ninth ward's head does nothing at all, and has not for a fortnight, because its pane is a bubbled temporary — nothing is coming down the wire from that tower. Marek reads the claim out twice to a full hall and then fails to go and check his own arithmetic, and feels an unfamiliar relief at being relieved of it. The chapter closes at 00:20 on the stair, with the plate lying on Tovan's bench under a cloth in the dark. First binding of the batch: inner-ear pressure, a metallic taste **new here and not present before it**, and the beginning of an inability to look away from a movement that has already stopped.

### Chapter 0006 — The Lowest Row

Day seven 23:00 to day eight 08:20, Hearthward terraces, Tarin's POV. The leading rain arrives with no wind in front of it and Tarin moors the *Wren*, *Sparrow* and the barge *Penny* on the grass above the market in a crosswind coming off the reservoir. The claim is true and visible: the gate holds, the upper rows are dry. At 23:40 the lower row's drains fill and the surcharge line climbs the retaining wall, and nine hours later the row drains in half an hour, which proves there was nothing behind the pipe. Six ground-floor rooms flood; **the vault holds ninety drawers and it is the lower six, eighteen inches off the market floor, that are ruined** — not ninety ruined drawers, and the distinction is now stated once only; nobody dies. **Tarin is given the spend without being told it:** during the mooring she stands over the row's four catch pits and remembers watching Ferren's crew clean them out seven days earlier, on the previous Thursday, in the dry, from the grass above, with Ferren shouting up that the row would take anything now; clean pits on Thursday last were due again on Thursday this, and a row with a clear outfall on the third night of rain is a Thursday problem that has quietly moved itself to next Tuesday. (Not the immediately preceding Tuesday — on that Tuesday she was at the Nacre apron in Ch 2.) Ovett counts sixty-one people out of the market. Marek has been on the terrace with the ward glass since eight the previous evening, and **at ten past six he goes down onto the reach with a lamp and puts his hand in the outfall throat — which is the same minute the tower keeper is lifting both plates out of the Ninth Ward tower seven miles away, and Marek is not there for that.** Their exchange at eight runs on exact figures: Tarin came up over the ridge at half past ten and has been there nine and a half hours, Marek came up at eight and has been there twelve, and two hundred and twenty people moved on those terraces in the night. Tarin asks him one question — who stood the garden crew down, and when — and he gives a list of the questions he did not ask, and says there is no more, and does not say the sentence that would have explained it. **Marek's report of the rota is now accurate: Ferren's lot tells them when to come, and the steward has been telling him for three years, not nine.** The Anwen Sarr meeting is at **twenty to eleven** in Chapters 6, 7 and 9, not eleven o'clock, and it must stay there: Anwen arrives at five past ten (Ch 4) and the public hall is already filling by eleven. At eight, Ferren tells a Kestrel glazier that he has no memory of the night he did not work, that nobody stood him down, that he remembers the whole night clearly and the drains being clear, and that what he cannot get past is that there was nothing wrong and that he was glad. He is not lying.

### Chapter 0007 — The Gap in Release

Day eight, 08:30, the Weatherglass, Marek's POV. **Marek has not been near the tower since Thursday.** He arranged it on Thursday evening on the second landing, and at 06:10 the tower keeper lifted both plates out, wrapped them in one sailcloth, set the bundle at the foot of the ladder, and asked nothing — the docket is the cover, since a keeper carrying two plates down a hill is doing glasswork the tower's account already says Marek is doing at his own hand and no charge to the ward. **Marek is seven miles away at that minute, at Hearthward, with his hand in the outfall throat.** The tower's feed is on a dead seat with no spare; the plate that came down is nine inches square and carries just under two days. His private plate — the nine-inch square cut off the crown of the ward's paid-for pane on the Wednesday — was in the same sailcloth, and is the only other glass in the world showing anything. The feed plate is instrumented and dull everywhere except the second turn, where Release thins to nothing for two and a half hours (23:58 to 02:26) and returns forty minutes late. He compares it against a logged March dropout in the same feed: that one dropped hard and left a crescent. This one is not a fault. His private plate shows the same absence — **and the physical reason both were in that tower is now on the page: he ground it to the feed's thickness, which costs an afternoon, and such a plate seats *behind* the holder, face to face with the ward's own plate with a finger of air between them, so two plates in a holder take the same trace off the same feed. He put it in himself on the Wednesday afternoon, carried it down to Tovan at the bench on Thursday morning, and put it back at seven o'clock Thursday evening.** Two plates, one feed, one holder, one tower, one man, no independent witness — the second entry was "stays in the tube" and it contradicted the first, and is gone. He reconstructs it physically: the row drained in half an hour, so the pipes were already empty, so the crew's Thursday clearing cannot have happened, and the crew cannot be sent, because the branch in which they went is the branch in which the row needed them. He states plainly in his own reasoning that looking cost him nothing and that the water in those rooms was the cost of making. He puts the four times in pencil on his cuff, keeps the bench sheet in a drawer, and decides not to file. Malle asks what the claim displaced; Marek gives him the mechanism and then a false rainfall answer; Malle lets it go because he has a front to staff, tells him to bring somebody else's plate, and orders him to put the tower's feed plate back that night and say nothing. **He does it at ten that night** — up the ladder, plate into the seat, no line in the tower's book, feed back up on the Office's glass in twenty minutes — which gives back the only instrument that could have shown the gap to somebody with standing to ask about it, and is the second thing in three days he has decided not to do. Marek minuted the sequence fault and asked for it on the record. The fine hearing in his left ear is thin and unexplained.

### Chapter 0008 — What the Notice Did Not Say

Day eight, 16:00, Low Spindle, Neris's POV. The published Bellweather notice is technically accurate and useless: Sergeant Ampow says it is the truest notice he has read in the district and it does not tell him when to start the pumps, whether to close the school, or what to do with the ninety people on the ground floors. Pet the runner returns from the ridge with a routing slip: the ninth ward's pane is out for replacement, replacement delayed, and a tower clerk told him that somebody cut a working plate out of it and told the crew himself. Neris writes the name and the question *did he know* in her own notebook and gives it no answer. In the drying room above the Ninth Baths, Sable Orin releases an unsigned number with an hour on it — *second belt, low ground from the west, 01:20 to 04:40* — on the ground that the Office is withholding the shape of the danger. **The card names a corridor and a time and no place at all, which is the chapter's own point and what makes the numbers uncompellable: translating a corridor into a street is Neris's work, done on the page when she writes the pump order off it.** She refuses to have sent it to Neris in particular; she put it out where anyone might read it. Neris names the shelter rota that changed underneath her last winter and that nobody can now reconstruct, mentions it once, and moves on. She takes the number, and offers to have it publicly attributed. She empties the Sump Lane school at 18:00 on her own warden authority, costs the ward a morning of wages, and spends nothing at all. **Forty-one children are counted outside the doors: the forty who walk up from the low blocks, plus one carried out of a house with no downstairs and no cart — the discrepancy is now accounted for in the text, not only in this file.** She starts a ruled page: *What the notice did not say.* Four lines. She declines to write a fifth, which would be about her own rota.

### Chapter 0009 — The Arithmetic of a Correct Claim

Day nine, 10:30, the Weatherglass, Marek's POV. Second low point. **The published figure reads nine hours, and the paragraph gives its own history — fifty on Thursday, twenty at ten o'clock on Friday night, nine now — which is the only clock the batch quotes; it slides downward throughout and every value must stay consistent with a collision at about 18:00 tonight. The six hours Marek is thinking about are the ridge's instrument disagreement from Chapter 1, not an hours-to-arrival, and the chapter now says so.** Neris corners him in a corridor and asks for a time for the low blocks; he has the private plate that would give her one and cannot give it to her because it has no chain, and when she presses he describes his whole career back to her as the reason. **The plate's location is now stated correctly: it ran in the ninth ward's own feed seat from seven o'clock on Thursday evening — the last time it was ever in that seat — until the keeper took it out at first light, and the tower's own plate is back up on Tannery Walk in its seat.** Juba Tren has come up the hill to see the Deputy, is told to wait, and gets on his knees to clear a blocked step on the Weatherglass front, saying almost nothing, which is the only mercy Marek gets all morning. Anwen Sarr brings the ledger clerk and Ferren into the public hall and reads five items: the claim said the row drains clear and it did not drain clear for nine hours, and she wants somebody to explain in a year whether *drains clear* and *stops being under water eventually* are the same phrase, because her assembly agreed to the first and got the second; the claim was true and a hundred and sixty people went up the upper terraces before midnight (two hundred and forty is the Sump Lane school roll, a different number and not interchangeable); the drains were cleared on Tuesday and not on Thursday and Marek knew the rota and never asked; a surcharged outfall does not drain in half an hour, so either the crew went out and two witnesses are wrong or they could not; and what did the claim require the district to give up. Marek answers with a sentence about records procedure, and she walks out carrying it, having told him she will hold him to publishing the cost in writing. The low point is not shame: it is recognising that his father's prediction about him was right and that he had been counting on not finding out. Malle summons him, shows him the joint request — one claim to cover both the Kestrel corridor and the low wards, which are the same air and cannot both be had — and tells him the only reading of the collision's order in the building belongs to a maintenance hand who has already given him a true answer that was not an account. The collision is confirmed at six hours. Marek takes the Bellweather watch: low ground, ninth ward, **the collision through to the end of it**, one reader, and his own name. The second belt has already been and gone in the small hours; this is not a second-belt watch.

### Chapter 0010 — A Steady Knife

Day nine, 17:00, the Weatherglass, Marek's POV. The watch roster is up with his name on it as *reader* — **low ground, ninth ward, the collision through to the end of it**; the second belt is already behind them — and the joint request pinned beneath it states in two correct lines that the western draw can either hold the Kestrel corridor open or ease the lower basin and not both, with an Office note that the Exchange's Bellweather window is *admissible* in the corridor decision because four licensed carriers have paid against it. The front has gone quiet and is now an hour out instead of six. The Line's board carries one sheet: a new instruction sheet, in Nessa Tarrow's careful hand, *Office window only, per instruction, 17:00*, and under the note, in **a different pencil, an Office hand** (not Nessa's), the two hours of Tarin's counter-reading have been rubbed out — not torn off, carefully, with the flat of a thumb over the paper so it could be reused. **Marek deliberately does not write Nessa's name in his own note about it**, and that restraint is a named beat. At the records counter, Sable Orin's broadside — *verified, the yards must be under sail by four o'clock* — has been reclassified at 14:10 under Rule 6 of the Charter of Public Notice as an unattached observation: not lodged, not compellable, not producible, with a handwritten line from Deputy for Records and Public Notice Lior Venn stating that the Exchange is not a party to any district's preparations and is not required to price them. Marek checks his own plate against it and it agrees exactly, and he feels relief, because the same machinery that buries her number has buried his. In the chart room, reglazing the Ninth Ward tower's framed chart, he finds that the chart's upper margin has been trimmed off with a knife: the cut edge is brown with age while the paper around it is still clean, so the trim is old and the sheet is old and the two were not contemporaneous. What survives of the provenance line reads *SEQ/N9/ — lodged by — A. Vale*, with a violet filed mark of a bell struck across it and a watch number, and then the paper ends, because the citation and the date were taken off. **The line is in his mother's hand; the mark is stamped onto it, not written by her — never write that the violet mark is hers.** He writes the time, plate reference and filing mark in pencil on the inside of his left cuff — **two readings and a filing mark, in three blocks of space** — and tells nobody. **The clock closes: 17:20 at the margin, ten minutes of the gum work, coat on at half past five, and half an hour out.** The yards' four o'clock goes on a torn notebook page in the coat pocket — the yard board and the broadside both — and stays there. The passage that used to state the series thesis in narration has been cut; the batch now leaves it to the trim, the mother's hand, and the cuff. He goes down the hill to the low ground with a claim he has not written, a collision nobody else can see the order of, and a piece of paper upstairs with a hole cut in it.

## Volume 01, Batch 0002 (Chapters 11–21)

Day ten = Sunday, eleven = Monday, twelve = Tuesday, thirteen = Wednesday, fourteen = Thursday, fifteen = Friday, sixteen = Saturday, seventeen = Sunday, eighteen = Monday, nineteen = Tuesday. **The collision reached the basin at about 18:00 on day nine and is never re-derived.** No chapter states an hours-to-arrival.

### Chapter 0011 — Two Truths in the Rain

Day nine from ten past six, Low Spindle, the ninth ward's stone floor (an old rope works four feet below the street, chosen because a man on it can see the lane and a man on the street cannot see his feet). Nineteen people, not the nine he expected: four crews, two pump houses, and eleven who came because a runner boy told a corner shop. Neris Kade has had the floor for two hours with nothing to say on it. Marek has a plate in a tube, a broom, and no chain. He says the whole of his standing out loud before he starts — *nothing I say tonight costs anything, no claim, no entry, nobody has to write this down, the only thing spent if I am wrong is my standing* — and then gives the order as numbers: **Gather alone and early; Weight does not finish and Break comes in on top of it early and hard; Route twice, a short hook under a long one; Release starts and its hour cannot be timed; Witness arrives out of order, on the Break, not on the Release.** He says *I am not sure* twice, the second time at Neris's demand, and tells the floor the uncertainty is his and not the weather's. He puts the plate down flat on the stone where anyone can look at it. **Ott Belm, the ninth ward's district copy, nineteen, writes it all in a wet notebook.** Ampow repeats it back to the second pump house crew, gets Break wrong, and Marek corrects him in front of everyone rather than letting it stand; Ampow then takes the corrected version to the pump house rather than his own. The collision comes at about six: the plate firms under his hand, his ear comes up half a tone, the taste of a coin arrives, and for four seconds he has the pure professional pleasure of an instrument doing what he said in front of a witness — and he notices, in the middle of it, that if this is the good part then he has been living for nine days in a job with no good part in it. Ampow's crews work the night: second pump house out at twenty past six, Sump Lane grate crew to the terrace and the school undercroft by half past seven, the first pump house overwhelmed and saying so. The Release runs eleven minutes and stops all at once at nine. The Witness comes on top of the Break. Nine inches at the corner of the fourth block and holding. **In the small hours he understands that the district was moved by somebody else's number on the Friday and that his nineteen minutes of being right has changed nothing about how the room is organised.** He opens his left cuff at twenty past five, reads the three blocks of pencil, finds no room, writes nothing, buttoned it back. **The Office's bulletin arrives at about eleven: every sentence in it is true, and it says nothing whatever about the low ground, and does not use the word *reader*.** It gives the collision hour correctly and says the turn behind the collision passed the northern wicks at ten o'clock. **A read. Nothing is spent.** Cost: his voice, his sleep, and the order, which is now in a district's heads and one boy's notebook and cannot be got back.

### Chapter 0012 — The Fifth Line

Day ten from eight — **a Sunday, and the prose now says so** — the Low Spindle district office. Two sprains, one stove, an eighty-year-old who would not leave his ground floor and is alive and furious. **Ott Belm's wet notebook, with the order and the crew times in it, is copied out by hand — deliberately untidy, because a clean document is not the same as a true one.** Sergeant Ampow wants the school opened and the Friday wages back; the block captains want to know who is paying; Neris says the ward is paying and gives the account exactly — **shut on the Friday morning at ten on her own authority with no notice and no claim, emptied again on the Friday evening at six, kept shut all night, opened again at seven on the Saturday morning because the water had not come** — and the district's cost is one morning of wages, which is the cheapest thing the ward owns, and the book already says so. Then Ott reads out the Office's bulletin, and the captains discover the thing the chapter turns on: **the Office's published hour was right, and it must be written down in those words, because in a year somebody will try to say the warning failed, and it did not fail, and the reason it did not fail is that the district did not use it.** Ott Belm, dryly: *then the Office is the only thing in this that anybody can be shown to be wrong about.* That is the fifth line. She writes it on the ruled page: the ninth ward's public glass has been unreadable for a fortnight, so nothing the district did can be compared with anything the Office published — we cannot be shown to have been wrong and we cannot be shown to have been right, and a district that cannot read its own notice cannot be argued with, which is not the same as being right. **It is not the line about her own shelter rota, which she still cannot write, and she notices with total clarity that she has got away with something.** **Neris sends the list nowhere.** What she sends, by hand with Pet, is a letter to the Kestrel Line asking not for a window but for a *turn*: the western turns over the low ground, with the hours on them, on a board a warden is welcome to read, free, and she will not pass it to anybody who has to pay for it. **The only document produced in Veyra that week that anybody acted on.** No spend. What Neris spends is standing.

### Chapter 0013 — What the Hulls Are Worth

Day eleven, Kestrel Yards. The mooring lift finally has its pin. **Maro Hesk appears in prose for the first time**: forty-one, weather-window broker and insurer, introduces himself as being in the wrong order on purpose because the wrong order is the useful one. He wants to buy the yard board, because Slate's agency report cites it and proves the Office's Bellweather corridor window was short by an hour and a half at the time of sale. His case is exact and entirely true: the Office did not lie, the Office sold a number it believed, and the number was short, and four carriers, a Nacre Holds grain factor and two insurers all paid against it, and *the next one has to be a number an underwriter in Red Sill can stand behind*. He does not ask for anything. He lays out the ask that is coming in three days — that somebody is going to call the Kestrel Line a *resource*, and that four businesses are quietly hoping a man in a good coat asks them to put their hulls somewhere in particular — and that if Tarin holds the lane, the Exchange gets its cover back and the yard gets a market that believes a yard can be inside a certified corridor without being swallowed by it. **Tarin prices it before it is asked.** On the boards, in chalk, in front of a carrier's man, she writes four conditions: the Line's reading on the board with the hours whatever the Office's window says; the reading up *before* the claim; the claim names the Line in the sponsor's own line; and both figures up with the difference in a column. Slate refuses to agree as a licensee and then agrees as a man standing in a yard and writes the four lines on the Exchange board, and says he will read them out to anybody who asks. Then Tarin goes down to the south bank and finds the **Lower Sill culvert**: a Bellweather-era concrete landmark with the city's oldest surviving dedication stone, a headwall cracked and shored twice and failing again, a banded timber stack, a replacement dewatering pump on a pallet, a repainted board, and **nine crew, loaded**. Nobody has cancelled it. Nobody has stood the crew down. **The window is a reach-level window, not a weather window** — the low flat takes a loaded flatbed about a fortnight a year — and because the work is classified as maintenance, it never becomes anybody's weather, so nobody ever has to decide anything and it simply does not come. **The convoy is loaded and does not move. No spend.**

### Chapter 0014 — A Number With No Place On It

Day twelve, the drying room above the Ninth Baths, and a records room. Sable Orin was on the ninth ward's stone floor on Saturday with a strip of glass and an aneroid, and the number it gave her is right, and when she puts it up nobody will act on it. The sheet is the most honest she has ever published: the turn, the corridor, the four o'clock, the **basis** printed for the first time (her aneroid, her wet cell, one strip of sequence glass bought rather than cut, Feth Raal's barometer read in public and not copied) and the sentence *I cannot say why it is right*. **She still gives a corridor and a time and no place, and says so on the sheet.** Four refusals, all correct: a Ternway secretary who cannot act without naming a field and will not translate it; a Kestrel low ward clerk who thanks her and files it; an assembly that will not run a shelter rota a third time in nine days on an uncertified number; and **Neris Kade, in person**, who declines because she translated a corridor into four blocks once on the Friday night and got a habit out of it, and because she now has a better instrument: a request outstanding to a yard for a turn she can name a district to, which is the worst number in the world and at least has two names on it. Then a woman comes and asks for the number. **Sable says no**, because giving her a direction and an hour without a street makes the asker the instrument, and if it goes wrong the woman is the one who acted. Sable catches herself telling a stranger *I know* about a mother-in-law on the Quay Steps she has never met. **Nothing is spent, and the chapter makes the not-spending visible as a failure rather than a near miss.** She counts: eleven people have asked her for something and she can remember asking five of them anything. She opens a **fourth notebook** — *Declined. Who. Why, in their words. Date.* — and does not take the sheet down. She is not sorry. She is reorganising.

### Chapter 0015 — Tannery Walk

Day thirteen from nine, the Ninth Ward tower, Tannery Walk. **Gant Ollis**, keeper eleven years, and the arrangement: he has never asked about the plates and Marek has never offered, and both of them know the other is being careful. The work is the week's unpaid docket. **A brass surround, bedded properly, around a temporary that should never have been temporary** — the rebate shows two sets of screw holes and a hundred and forty years of counter-sunk iron, and the reason the frame is empty is money: the replacement was quoted and paid for and never collected, and the district's glasswork allocation does not cover a man and a crate sent forty miles. ### Chapter 0016 — A Pipe That Is Already Full

**Day fourteen** — the Thursday — late afternoon and evening, Low Spindle. **Batch midpoint.** Chapter 15 ends with Marek walking down Tannery Walk on the Wednesday the thirteenth, and he now goes to the bottom of it, turns round, and comes back the next day, so the outfall visit is marked as the day after. ### Chapter 0017 — Nothing Interesting

Days fourteen and fifteen, Ferrygate district, the Nacre Gate, the low side. **Nell Cray**, clerk nineteen years, whose stated ambition is a week in which nothing interesting happens, and who has one. ### Chapter 0018 — Twelve Years Back

Day sixteen from eight, the Ninth Ward tower, the feed seat, the lamp, the plate Marek fitted. He takes the spare plate's box up with no reason at all, which is a luxury he has not had since he was nineteen. His document is in the second drawer downstairs: four hundred and thirty words, all procedure, three non-procedural sentences in a different ink, and he cannot get past the third. He reads for two hours, properly — down once, up movement by movement out loud, once with the lamp cold, once tilted. **The collision is legible and durable and no instrument in the world has published it.** And the Release is clean: no smear, no hard edge, no crescent, no step, moisture coming up at an angle and closing over in the ordinary way. **The Ninth Ward feed dropped nothing in the biggest collision in nine years**, which means there was nothing wrong with the tower, which means the two and a half hours of nothing in the second turn was not the plate, not the wet cell, not the lamp, and the only innocent explanation has just been removed by the Office's own instrument. Then he goes back further than the feed keeps, lighting the plate obliquely with his eyes soft, and on the sixth night of the run there is a **complete, instrumented Witness movement with the tower's own date stamp in the margin, dated twelve years back, arriving in the glass from below rather than out of the air.** He cannot account for it: no instrument in the manual will tell him how a working plate holds a twelve-year-old registration and there is no index in the building he may read. **It is a date and nothing else.** No name, no document, no order, no institution, no antagonist. **He does not take the plate, does not copy the trace, and does not write it in the tower's glass book** — three refusals, each with a different cost. He leaves at six, stops at the corner of Sump Lane, wants to go back up, does not, and knows he watched himself decide it on purpose. **A read. Nothing is spent.** The knowledge is now solely his, and he is on a roster, and a man on a roster who knows something is a man who can be asked.

### Chapter 0019 — Hours on the Board

Day seventeen, the Weatherglass and Kestrel Yards, Sunday. ### Chapter 0020 — The Signature

Day eighteen, the Hearthward rain-garden assembly rooms, above a market shut since the Friday before last with a season's seed stock in the hall. Anwen Sarr called no meeting: she sent for the clerk and for Marek and the clerk sent for the chair and the chair sent for Ferren, because the standing order is Ferren's. **The Council's request is the best one-page document she has received from that building in six years** and she says so in front of her own clerk. **Sabin Orle**, assessor for water scheduling, has driven up on a Sunday evening on a telephone call from a district clerk, with her folder of four certified trial claims, all of them true, three of them true where an uncertified forecast would have been wrong; she wants the Hearthward seat and says so, and says she has no controlled test on a prepared district and cannot get one, and that the one assembly which read the request is the only one worth having. Anwen refuses in two reasons, and puts the second first in her own head and second on the paper. **First: sequence.** The Council cannot test the method's effect on prepared districts while the cost of the only prepared district's claim is unpublished. **Second: Marek.** She will not put the district's name under a trial of the method, in front of a Council, in front of the market factor and in this assembly's minutes for eleven years, while the district owed the truth about it is waiting on a man in the room. Sabin asks him the right question — *what would this assembly have to see in order to concur?* — and gets the answer that is not ready, and tells him he has until the Council sits, and that the Council sits tomorrow, and that she is sorry about the method and not about the seat. **Kell Bray writes four clauses as she reads them aloud**, including that the refusal is not a criticism of the method, and the assembly **will** concur with any future claim that carries a published cost at entry, and the standing requirement that no certified claim be entered without its cost published at the point of entry **or the sponsor recording in writing that the cost cannot be made public and why.** Anwen says out loud that clause four is drafted so a Council can satisfy the first sentence and fail the second. **The trial proceeds with the Nacre Holds seat instead.** In the rain at the bottom of her own steps she tells Marek, and asks him to hold to it, that **she did not refuse for him** — the experiment was in the wrong order, and if his document had been finished this would have been a signature on a Monday afternoon. **No spend. No claim of hers. The refusal is a correct institutional act and looks, from inside the building, like a defeat.**

### Chapter 0021 — The Minutes

Day nineteen, the Council's technical cell in the basin house, morning certification, afternoon session. Marek is there because Sabin Orle telephoned on the Monday and asked him: he is the only reader in the basin who has read the order in a collision, and a claim about a gate release is a claim about order, and it turns on *what is standing behind it*. **He is being used, properly, on the record, in a room, for the thing he is for, and he has wanted it since he was nineteen.** The claim as entered: the Nacre Gate releases at 04:40 on the twenty-first; the release does not exceed the band; the Ferrygate low side drains clear to the second watch; the market road takes loaded carts from first light; listed alternative, the low side floods to the low marker and the autumn market closes for the season; expiry the second watch of the twenty-first, in watch terms and deliberately unpriced, because no chapter fixes a watch to a clock hour; sponsor S. Orle; concurring seat Nacre Holds. **The certification bell is rung once at ten o'clock, which makes the trigger's arithmetic checkable: 04:40 on the twenty-first is just under forty-three hours out.** He attests the **order** in eleven minutes, as a reader and not a sponsor. The certification bell is rung once by the assessor, and the plate stands the claim up, and the single System panel of the batch appears: *CLAIM: … ONE POSSIBLE TURN WILL CLOSE.* **It does not say which.** The Release in the gate's record is now the shallowest he has seen in a certified run. **It is not a dropout** — a dropout drops hard and leaves a hard edge and a crescent, and this feed has one in March with the cause written on a tag in a drawer, and he filed the cause — **it thinned, and came back on a shallow slope, late, with nothing standing behind it.** Beneath the fine trace, three preparations are still legible and going out: a rota, a ferry's night, and **two small marks side by side where a person wrote something down near an instrument that was recording, which a sequence plate does not hold**, and he could not tell a soul what they were for. **He cannot name a district, a person, or a purpose, and he could not produce a document if one were asked for.** Sabin Orle asks him to say nothing in the afternoon and explains exactly why, in two reasons, and adds that she would like to use him again. Malle comes up on the Sunday coach, out of hours, with a sandwich, to clap. He gives Marek the price of the thing he is about to do: a man who says a true thing he cannot prove costs the Office more than a man who says a false thing he can, he is right about the cost, and if Marek does it today he will not do it again. **The afternoon session is the method being congratulated.** A market factor books eleven carts and two barrows for first light on a claim entered four hours ago. A Nacre Holds grain convoy's agent takes the 04:40 to 08:00 window with four chartered barges and lodges the booking with a time on it. And the Ferrygate district acknowledges the claim, **withdraws its rain-garden clearing rota, cancels the ferry's extended night hours, and thanks the Council** — a courteous, completely correct four-line letter, with nothing in it anyone could object to. The sixth minute: the method may be reported to the Council as having cost the district nothing that the district was willing to record. **Marek stands up in the second row from the back and says it.** The claim is true. It spent a branch. What it displaced is not weather. He describes the thinning, the three preparations, the two marks, and the fact that he can tell the shape of three and not the name of one; that the evidence is a smear on a Council plate read by a reader with no chain, in a shuttered room, on the morning it happened, and will not survive the week. **So the call was made correctly, cheaply and invisibly, and the method's success is measured in options removed, and nobody in the room is doing it on purpose, and the good method is what is doing it.** The room calls it a mood and not a challenge, correctly. Sabin Orle asks him four questions, gets three *I don't know*s, and delivers the sentence the volume turns on: **it is not accidental that a claim's cost cannot be documented; it is the design** — a cost you can document is a cost somebody can be compensated for and argued about, and a Council that had to compensate districts for every displaced preparation would not certify anything, because you cannot displace a preparation and put a price on it, and if you can put a price on it then it was never a preparation, it was a plan with a budget, and a plan with a budget is not the thing they are trying to keep. The certification stands; his statement goes in an annex, bound separately, as a statement unattributed to any district and **not** as an objection. The room stands, and the applause starts again inside ten seconds, and it is for the method, and it is entirely sincere. **Then he signs the three-line minute without reading it, and reads it afterwards, and his own attestation is on the first line and a footnote carries the Hearthward refusal and its clause four** — so that every assembly in the basin now reads his name in the same breath as the word *cost*, not because he did anything wrong but because he was right out loud, could not produce the paper, and the room went on clapping, and the minute recorded all four of those facts in the ordinary order of business. **The Office will stop using him. No assembly will ask him anything again.** Malle puts his cap on without applauding and leaves by the side door before the room does. **One binding, certified, entirely accurate. Marek is still a Reader. Strain is unchanged. The hearing loss is not here.**

### Chapter 0025 — Nine Days in a Full Pipe

Day twenty-five, Low Spindle, Neris's POV. ### Chapter 0026 — A Roster With a Hole In It

Day twenty-six, the Weatherglass, Marek's POV. The districts **re-file the joint request** and the Council returns an answer nobody can argue with: *the method is sound. The office is unable to certify a claim covering two goods which require the same air, and this incompatibility is not a matter of degree.* **The eleven words in the middle are the trap, and they are perfectly correct: a district prepared to keep water off its own ground is a district whose people are in their own basements, and that district cannot then be returned.** **Marek is not sent for, and the long Bellweather watch — a second roster, six days, covering the body and the peak — goes up on the board at 16:00 with Aldis Prynne on the reader's line and no version of the sentence in which Marek is on it.** Malle tells him the plain truth about the nineteenth: the Office cannot use a man shown to be unprovable in public, and he put the collision's order in the annex himself so that nine seats would not read the report with a hole in it, and that is a different act by the same man and neither is a favour. Marek asks the one question left to him — what does the order of the movements do to a western corridor — and Malle answers it in a tone that says *this is a tool I am not supposed to hand you*: **a corridor is not a road, it is an hour you can put a hull in; the order changes where the hour is; and the draw that opens the corridor is the same draw that lifts water off a low reach, so the choice is not a choice, it is a decision about who is standing where at the end of it.** A read is offered and refused on both sides: Malle will not take a reading off a man with no chain, and Marek will not give one, and both of them know it is not a coincidence. **No spend, no claim written, nothing entered.**

### Chapter 0027 — The Demand

Day twenty-seven, the Council chamber, Marek's POV. **Cassian Rook appears in prose for the first time.** Fifty-two, Director for six years, emergency warden of Low Spindle for nine before that. Day forty (Tuesday), the basin house, Corrin Malle's POV — **the batch midpoint, and the reader's first look inside the Deputy.** The shut room before: an empty folder carried ninety minutes to a hearing; **a sandwich and an out-of-hours coach three weeks earlier, on the Sunday evening before the Council sat, because he wanted to watch a method he staffs succeed** — day 3 to day 40 is five weeks, not six, and Malle says so four times in the chapter. The shut room before: an empty folder carried ninety minutes to a hearing, two children, a sandwich carried up a hill on a Sunday, thirty years in the building, and the discovery that **the thing he reached for on the third of the month was a rule and the rule worked.** He is asked *why does the entry for the thirtieth say maintenance failure* and gives both sentences in the same words he gave Tovan Iles: **a true statement about the culvert and not a true statement about the thirtieth.** Then he is asked **what the classification did**, which nobody has ever asked him, and he does not know at first, and then he says the true thing inside it: **a classification removes a thing from the categories that can be complained about, and that this is the design, and the design is older than him and he is its custodian and not its author, and he has been in the building for thirty years and cannot find the author.** He is wrong about one thing and the chapter knows it and the reader is not told: **a classification is not a name, and a word is a better hiding place than a name, and he thought putting a word on it was the same as putting his name on it.** He volunteers the finding: **this office has never in its history put a line in a return for a thing that was not damaged, and he does not know whether that is a rule or a shape, and he has looked in four drawers and it is not concealed, it is simply not written anywhere.** Rook rules that there is no finding against him, no censure, no reclassification, no move, no form — **and Malle says in one flat sentence in the corridor that that is a good outcome for him, and then works out that he will have to say it again in a month to somebody who cares, and that there will not be anybody who cared, and that that is the correct arrangement.** He is not redeemed and is not villainised. He reads nothing; he has never read a sequence in his life. **Ilva Sorn's observation that the column is the first rule in this basin that protects a person, by refusing to publish, is the chapter's last institutional beat.** **The assessor then rules, and the ruling is PRINTED IN FULL, in the nineteen words the review is remembered for and the only finding of the review:** *That the effect of a claim on a district's preparations cannot be shown from the record, and so reported.* That wording is canon: Chapter 46 has Nell Cray quote it back as **nineteen words**, and the terms of reference are the sentence it was written around. No spend, no panel.

### Chapter 0043 — The Board at the Freight Apron

Day forty-two (Thursday), the Bellweather freight apron and the south-bank shed, Petrie with Tarin Senn. **The shed is not cleared and nobody has told him to clear it.** The water office sends a clerk on the **Tuesday** with a *disposal of retained stock* form; he refuses, for his own entirely correct reasons: the timber is paid for and stored against the water office's order 114, the pump is on a Union guarantee and cannot be sold by a shed, and a load is not rubbish while there is a wall standing that it was bought for. The district's works clerk comes on the **Wednesday** with a printed enamel sign — **SOUTH BANK DEPOT — LOAD HELD. NO DISPOSAL**, with the works office's name and a serial number — and Petrie refuses that too, because a printed sign has somebody's name on it and can be taken down, and a thing that has been repainted every ninth year for nine years is a thing that is still true. Tarin Senn offers the Line's plank, frame and a man to letter. **He says no on the first day and yes on the second, and paints it himself over the Thursday and the Friday, because a painted board is a date you cannot lose, and if a man is going to paint something on a shed he had better paint something true on it.** He paints the **sixth of the month and not the thirtieth**, because the sixth is when the load went on and the thirtieth is when somebody decided it did not come off, and **there was no such somebody, and a name on that board would send the whole city looking for a man and stop them looking at the load.** The board carries a load, an order number, the sixth, nine crew, *the load is here*, and *painted by the foreman* — **no name, no seal, no accusation.** The term is not used by anybody in the chapter; what is used is *the load that never moved*. **The first civic act in this city in four years, and it costs a pot of white paint and about four pounds of timber, and it is in no return, and no possibility closes.** The culvert is not rebuilt and the timber and the pump are still in the shed. No spend, no panel.

### Chapter 0044 — The Column

Day forty-four (Saturday), the Weatherglass, the records floor, all day, Marek's POV. A drafting committee, and Marek on it by the accident of being the only person in the building who is both a reader and available. **The column is nearly three things**: the strong version that names the sponsor (argued by a man of twenty-two years' standing, and killed by Sabin Orle in ninety seconds: *a column with a name in it is a claim on a person, and this Council keeps records of things and has never kept a record of a person it intended to act against*); the empty version, a rule saying claims shall be published, which Venn supports and then undercuts himself (*a rule is the cheapest thing any building makes*); and the honest version. **Marek writes the rules and argues his own name out of them, out loud, correctly, using Sabin Orle's own sentence from the nineteenth — a cost anybody can name is a cost somebody can be compensated for, and if it can be put a price on it then it was never a preparation — which he agreed with in a shuttered room and has had a month to think about what agreeing with it cost a district.** He volunteers that it cost a district three preparations, two of which went out in a courteous letter and one of which went out in silence, and **names no district, no street, no person, no hour and no mechanism, and says only that there is no column anywhere for a district finding out.** The argument he makes is a defence of himself and nobody in the room asks him a question and every one of them hears what the question would have been. **Anwen Sarr hears it, understands it exactly, says nothing, and uses it four days later.** The six items: place, stated effect, trigger with the hour, expiry with the hour, condition in the words on the sheet, and **turns displaced** — what closed, in the ordinary language of the district affected, and **by no means the name of the person who closed it, nor any word or mark from which the person might be identified.** No other item may be added, and the column may not be amended by a Deputy. **The first entry is the thirtieth: Kestrel corridor, 29th–30th; corridor passable to 04:00; trigger the Line's arrival at the yards' mark, 23:11; expiry 04:00; condition the same; turns displaced: a reach-level window on the morning of the thirtieth, forty minutes, not established in any instrument of this office.** He reads nothing all day, and at ten past four he sets the private plate on a frame in the records room and does not read it, and reads the figure off the return in ink instead, and does not say why, and the reason is his hearing and nobody in the building knows it. **The column is not a dry line and is not called one. The word has not arrived.** No spend, no panel, no advancement.

### Chapter 0045 — Dismissed from the Field

Day forty-five (Sunday), the Council chamber, Marek's POV with Corrin Malle. The review sat through the weekend. The censure is read out at ten past ten and **every clause of it is true**: that the attesting reader made an unproved assertion in a public forum and was unable to support it, and that the assertion was of a cost, and that a cost which cannot be supported cannot be acted upon and cannot be compensated; that he repeated it on the thirty-seventh and again could not support it; and that he has at no time produced a document, an instrument, a witness or a name, and that **no such production was required of him.** Penalty: dismissal from field duty and a censure in the Office's own record. No appeal, there being nothing to appeal. **He has about nine seconds in which three things are available to him — the twelve-year date, the four hundred and eleven words, the chart margin in his mother's hand — and he takes none of them out, and the reader understands that he is choosing not to, and the volume neither rewards nor punishes him for it.** **Nobody in this building had ever once asked him for a piece of paper in six weeks, and that is the single most generous clause in the document and nobody had to fight for it.** He signs, asks one procedural question, and is told that nothing comes off anything and that a censure is not a reclassification and does not touch the attestation. **Tarin Senn is in the room and does not stand and is not asked to. Anwen Sarr is in the room and does not speak and is not asked to, and she is the only person there who knows what she watched him decline. Aldis Prynne is a witness and is asked nothing.** Malle reads the sheet out, says the only sentence he is entitled to say — *if the reader's name comes off, the next one signs it alone* — **and says he said it to a copy clerk on the third of the month and not to Marek, and why, and it is that he is the last man in the basin who can be shown a plate and could not put that name on the form.** Rook puts the sentence that the censure is a correct consequence of the method as authorized on the record himself, while he still can. **His hearing is not mentioned in either document and is not the ground.** The cost of the trial claim remains unprovable after a five-day review that said so in writing on the first day of district evidence. No spend, no panel.

### Chapter 0046 — The Word In The Queue

Day forty-six (Monday), the basin house, the public table, then the hall, Nell Cray briefly. The report is laid at ten and it is good, and the assessor says so out loud twice: **the method is recommended for continued trial; the claim of the twenty-ninth is recorded as held and accurate; the review records that the effect of a claim on a prepared district cannot be demonstrated from the record and recommends one thing, the column, and recommends nothing else; the two reclassifications are set out in full on page fourteen and nobody in the hall objects to either, because both of them are true.** About sixty people in a queue, and they all want to know whether they are insured, and the answer to every one of them is no, and it is no because of a rule and not a box. **The yard's framed column on the wall: two men from a carrier's office in front of it, and the younger one says it is the only honest weather record he has ever held, and the older one does not take his eyes off it.** At about half past eleven **the term arrives, out of the queue, from a woman who has been standing in the wet for an hour: *my side of the river has got a dry line on it.*** Nobody in the building had used the phrase. The Office adopts it inside a week: on the Thursday a clerk writes **DISPLACED TURNS — DRY LINES** at the top of a returned ledger in a fair hand without lifting the pen, in the ordinary course, and nobody costed it, **and the person who coined it is not the person who gets the credit.** The adoption is **on the Thursday, three days later and not one minute sooner** — a clerk writing DISPLACED TURNS — DRY LINES at the head of a returned ledger, and Nell Cray reads the sixth item off the **returned ledger on a trestle**, which is the same object that is on the trestle in the hall in Chapter 48. Nell Cray files the fourth form, with the fourth covering note, four times as long as the last three, and is told in writing that there is no such form. Then she reads the sixth item of the column twice and is given a sentence by a piece of paper for the first time in five weeks, and is told what a dry line is by **Ida Crome, a ratepayer of the ninth ward's fourth block**, and the two of them argue about the thirtieth in front of the column for forty minutes, and about nine people join, and by half past four there are thirty, and there is no minute of it. ### Chapter 0047 — A Fragment Of An Index

Day forty-seven (Tuesday), the chart room and then the archive, Marek's POV with Ione Sere. **The chart room is on the way and there is a frame to do, and the ninth ward's frame is the last one on the end, and the margin is the same sheet he saw on the ninth: a violet square with a bell struck across it, a half-gone sequence reference, an inch and a half cut off the top with a good blade and a steady hand, the cut old and the sheet old and the two not contemporaneous.** He does not go up the ladder. In the archive he asks for a citation and is taught, kindly and precisely, the difference between a citation and a file, and is told in advance that she will not help him and will not stop him, and that she would like that distinction witnessed. **The public catalogue has the N9 tower sequence, class sequence/forecast, lodged: witnessed — and four lines lower, under WITHDRAWN OR MISSING, a Ninth Ward Record lodged as a witnessed forecast sequence with no shelf, no box, no accession and no year of withdrawal.** She declines to say more and says it as a refusal and a kindness, and says there are two rooms in this city where a record of that class can be and neither of them is that room, and that she is not going to say which, as a kindness and not as a gate. **He asks for the routing index, which he is entitled to, and finds a hand-cut gap about an inch long on a rule, and the surviving fragment reads: VASK, O., R-9 — routing desk nine, in a clerk's hand.** **A routing entry cannot name a clerk who was already dead, because a witness is a person who was there; the thing cut out was a date.** He establishes three things and no more: the reference was lodged; it was lodged as a witnessed claim, not an observation, because a bell is struck for a claim and not for an observation; and the citation beside the signature was removed by hand. **Ione Sere tells him, flatly and in a records voice on purpose, that routing entries from the pre-Cut years were renumbered in a ledger migration, and that an old routing entry in an old hand is an old hand and nothing else, and asks him whether he believes it; he says he does, because the alternative is to be a reader who has accused the archive of lying, and there would be a form. She says only *I have said it because it is in the manual*, the narrator gives her a records voice and stops there, and no rule, drawer or signed paper points at a hand.** He takes nothing: not the sheet, not a transcription, not a rubbing, not a note, and not one request in writing, and the chapter states what each refusal cost. **He does not say the name out loud and does not write it; the reader has it and he does not.** He reads no sequence and is not asked to, and the reason is his hearing and nobody in the building knows it. No spend, no panel, no advancement, no tower.

### Chapter 0048 — The Public Dry Line

Day forty-eight (Wednesday), the public forecast hall, from about six in the evening until it is emptied, Marek's POV with the hall. The hall is long, low, barrel-roofed, built for forty, with more than two hundred in it and forty more in the yard and the doors open. **He has written the document. Nine hundred and forty words, his name at the top, and it is not the one he promised on the ninth and refused on the eighteenth: it is the one he could write, and it is worse.** It gives place, trigger, effect, alternative and expiry; how the corridor was timed and how the Line is named; what was displaced; what it cost, being a banded stack, a pump on a pallet, nine crew and forty feet of a low flat; **and then the sentence — this office has no instrument which looks at a reach, and none can be had, and the author cannot supply one because the plate behind the reading has no chain behind it. The claim stands. Its sponsor can produce nothing. Both sentences are true and neither can be got round.** **He read it four times and cut three things out of it, and all three cuts are on the record because he told Anwen Sarr about them and she wrote them down and he initialled it: the twelve-year date, the chart margin, and the sentence about the frame — and the recorded reason for the third is the ward's poverty and the absence of a hand, not a confession, and Anwen Sarr says in the hall that she does not believe it and that he is not sure and neither is she.** He reads it out himself, once, in the ordinary order of business, in about eleven minutes, and stops talking. Anwen Sarr says in the first two minutes that it is not what she asked for, refuses to say the two names or the street, and says the three things: the method is sound; the method's success is measured in options removed and is now printed in a column; and **there is a man at the front of this hall who is the only person who knows what that claim cost, and no document in the world can prove a word of it, and that is not a failure of this hall, that is the hall.** **Before she sits down she walks to the table, picks the document up, and reads the spent-turn line out loud from the front of the room, and says that it is her clause four word for word, and that a man who wanted that line to carry his own name had the easiest argument in the world available to him and argued against himself with it out loud in a records room a fortnight ago and told nobody why, and that he is not hers and she is not going to claim him.** **By eight o'clock the hall is arguing about the column** — the column has been in the public ledger since the **Monday** of the forty-sixth and not since the Wednesday, as the report was — a carrier's underwriter and Ida Crome of the fourth block, neither of them in the reach at half past three that morning, both right, arguing about the reach and the ground and what forty minutes is worth, and she says *forty minutes is a dry line, that is all a dry line is, and they can't price it and they can't say whose it is and they'll not be able to say whose it is in a hundred years.* **The final image, exactly:** the hall late, half the lamps out, the incomplete Bellweather trace pinned to a board — nine inches of sequence glass in a wooden frame with a card listing everything it is not: that it was cut out of an object that is not his, that it carries a day and a half of the second turn from the eighth of the month and the approach to the collision as that stood at half past five on the ninth, and that it carries neither the collision nor the thirtieth, because the author was carrying it in a coat in a maintenance shop on the thirtieth — Tovan Iles at a copy desk two rows back copying an attendance sheet she is not permitted to keep, hand steady, jaw set, with the thing in her box that nobody knows; and through the high windows the ninth ward tower's signal glass, unreadable, a bubbled temporary in a brass surround bedded by his own hands, dark because the ward has no money, with the paid-for pane in a glassblower's shop. **Rain reaches the low end of the city six hours after the front of the ninth of the month went over the point above the Bellweather reach. Six hours is the lag. Six hours is what it has always been, on every rain this basin has ever had, and for nine years that lag was the width of a headwall. The reach takes it because it is a bucket with no wall in it now. The ninth ward stays dark.** The method survives. The record is public and true and incomplete. Nobody is forgiven and nobody asks. No spend, no panel, no branch reopened.
