## Marek Vale

**Stage:** Reader at the end of Volume 01. Unchanged, and no advancement is earned in this volume. He has not followed a sequence across two towers, does not do so in Chapters 36–48, and never will in this volume.

**Where he is at the end of Batch 0004:** in a public forecast hall on the ground floor of the Weatherglass, on the evening of the forty-eighth day, with a nine-inch square of sequence glass pinned to a board beside a card that lists everything it is not, having read out nine hundred and forty words of his own account of a claim he entered and then stopped talking. **He is dismissed from field duty and censured in the Office's own record, permanently, and the ground is correct, and his attestation of the collision's order stays in the file with his name on it, and nothing came off anything.** He has a tube in a coat, a folded true document in the second drawer of his bench that cannot be filed anywhere, about a third of his usable hearing gone from the left side, and a high thin ringing on that side that he has never explained to anybody and is never going to. He has not been on a watch, a roster or a field list since the eleventh of the month before last.

**What he has done:** put a hull inside a claim. He asked a captain for it, in a yard, at the boards, in front of nine people, on the four Kestrel conditions, and she gave him the hull and the hours and made the terms; and then he entered a conditional claim on the ward ledger bench under a Director's authorization, with a bell and a clerk and no certification, with an expiry equal to its own effect and a listed alternative that named what it took; and it held, and it saved the corridor, and it took a quarter of an hour of eased air out of the last evening of a month, which was the ebb, which was the convoy's window, which was forty minutes wide on the morning of the thirtieth. He knew. He went to the south bank to be near it with a docket that was a real errand and a lie about why he was there. He said *it was just the water* to a shed foreman and has not been able to put it down since. And then he went into four minutes of cold moving water after a boy of six and came out with about a third of his fine hearing gone from the left ear for the rest of his life.

**Physical state:** **damaged, permanently, in the body, and not otherwise changed.** A very high thin unbroken ringing in the left ear and roughly a third of the fine range lost on that side — the part that separates a smear from a dropout, the part that is the whole trade. It was four minutes of cold moving water on the thirtieth and it is not a binding and it is not the Volume 12 sequence burn and is never to be written as one, and nothing in the record will ever mention it. The pre-existing strain — the half-tone pressure, the metallic taste, the thin hearing — was **unchanged by the Chapter 29 binding and was not improved by it**; he noticed that he was relieved and that the relief was the wrong thing to notice. He works on the wrong side of a bench now and does not say why, and his father stands on his right without once having to move.

**Flaw, as performed rather than described:** **he went and got the thing instead of the question, and it worked, and the working is the problem — and in the last three chapters of the volume he does it again, twice, and gets it right both times.** He built a permanent public column and argued his own name out of it in a room, correctly, out loud, using the sentence that beat him in Chapter 21, and told nobody why. And in about nine seconds on the Sunday morning of the forty-fifth day he declined to produce the three things on the table that would have made the censure impossible, and he did it to keep a poor ward's tower out of a Council file, and he has never said so to anybody, and the reader is not told either, and the volume neither rewards him nor punishes him. **He is not a martyr and he does not enjoy this. He is a competent man who is still the best reader in the basin, one third deaf in one ear, who has built a machine that will hide him and cannot tell whether that is a defence.** He built a claim that was as honest as he could make it, with an alternative that named what it took, and asked the one person whose free act could make it true, and got a yes in chalk, and entered a true thing in a good hand with a Director's stamp on it, and felt the specific warmth of *this is now somebody else's to do* and identified it correctly and did it anyway. He is not corrupted and he is not broken. He is competent, and the competence is the problem, and he must not become a man who enjoys it.

**What he owes:** Anwen Sarr a document, now fourteen days older and structurally blocked, because the honest version requires an instrument with a chain behind it and there is exactly one and it is the plate in the ninth ward tower that also holds a twelve-year-old Witness; the ninth ward's public glass, which is dark for money and cannot be told about without a confession to a keeper who has never asked; a boy in the fourth block and a woman with a bed made up; a shed foreman the truth, which he cannot give; **the twelve-year-old date in a locked room in a poor ward's tower, which nobody knows he has, and which is now on the same plate as the corridor he spent, and which he cannot write about without burying the first thing under the second; and a plate of hearing that he will not get back and has told nobody about.**

**What he believes now, and should be frightened of:** **that a good claim in a good hand, with a listed alternative and a Director's stamp, is the correct instrument, and that he is the correct man to hold it, and that the fact that it cost a thing nobody can name is a property of the weather and not of him.** The method survived and was recommended for continued trial in a report that is honest and incomplete and correct. The record is now public: a city of a hundred and forty thousand people can see that something was spent at the Lower Sill on the morning of the thirtieth and cannot see the man who spent it, **and the column that lets them see it was drafted by him, and he argued his name out of it, and that is the best thing anybody has ever done for him and the worst thing that has ever happened to him.** And the reader should be more afraid of Cassian Rook at the end of this volume than at the start of it, because Rook has now arranged a column that cannot name anybody, by being scrupulous, in public, for six weeks, and there is not one line in the whole of the review anybody could take hold of.

## Tarin Senn

**Where she is:** Kestrel Yards, on the morning of the thirtieth, on a yard apron in the sun with a launch book under her arm and a nineteen-year-old with his wrist in a splint arguing with a rigger about the price of canvas.

**State:** **has put a hull inside somebody else's claim and has published what it did.** She gave Marek the corridor publicly at the boards, on the four conditions, with the hours on a slate and her own hand under them, and she said out loud, a day before, that the four lines were about the yard's name and that not one of them was about her crew. Then the turn held and the lulls stopped and a mooring hand's wrist came off the coil at 23:11, and she went up the lift that should have been a junior's for four minutes and eleven seconds while Ilva Sorn took the *Marrow* in off the quarter, and she came down and put both of those facts on the board in chalk with the hour on them, under the third line, published as *not the yard's error*, because a column of differences that only reports good news is not a column of differences. **She also flew the reach on the thirtieth in a flat-bottomed lighter and lifted the second pump house's intake screen out of the water by boom from under the freight apron, and it saved a pump house, and it is on the Line's own account as an error of the yard, and she is the one who paid for it.** She has still not asked Marek the second question. The shape of the answer has changed again and she has not begun to construct it.

**What she has of Marek:** a hull, an hour, four minutes of her own hands on a loaded line, a plate in a coat, and the discovery that **a claim under an authorization does not tell you what it has done to the thing you are standing in.** Competence acknowledged on both sides, and a decision about who is the instrument, twice, in opposite directions. **She was in the room on the forty-fifth day when he was censured and did not stand and was not asked to, and said one thing in the corridor afterwards — that it was a bad morning and she was not going to say a single other thing about it in this building, and that in a yard she would tell him what she thought and it would not be this. She also offered the Line's plank, frame and a man to letter a board at a freight apron on the forty-second and was refused, correctly, by a shed foreman, twice, and stood in front of the finished board for four minutes and said *that is not a load list, that is an epitaph*, and was told it is a load list.** Neither of them has said the word partnership and no romance has entered this volume. Volume 03 still owns the first real trust milestone and this is not it.

## Neris Kade

**Where she is:** Low Spindle, on the evening of the thirty-ninth, having come out of a room in which she said out loud, to a clerk, the thing she had known for a year and a half, and having gone home and been unable to write the eighth line of her own page.

**State:** **has moved from a woman who collects omissions to the person holding the only document in the city.** Her ruled page has seven lines, is stamped and dated in her own hand, and its seventh line is the one that carries the volume's argument: the Office has no instrument that looks at a reach, therefore nothing lost on the reach can be shown to have been lost by anybody, therefore a window the city cannot see is not a window the city loses. She sent the list to Malle on the twenty-second, as she said on the ninth day she would, and his reply was correct and moved the document out of her hands and into a building she does not control. She got a second opinion on a line that was already full from a maintenance hand in a hole, for free, in ninety seconds, and it was the answer she already had, and she learned from it that two copies of a true thing in two buildings is still one assertion. Her own shelter rota, the ninth ward's, is still unwritten and is still the line she cannot put her name to. **She has not asked Marek for the plate and has told him so to his face, and he said no, and she accepted it, and she has not stopped asking anybody for anything else.** In Chapter 41 she sent the seven-line page to the Council's review, kept a stamped copy of the copy on purpose, watched the seventh line be read out and ruled not to be evidence of anything, and said *I knew it when I wrote it* and asked for it to be minuted. **She then asked her warden's council for a quarterly sheet of the district's clearing rota, shelter rota and pump order, and it was carried in four minutes, and she said out loud that the reason it is cheap is the reason nobody did it in six years, and the room laughed.** Her own shelter rota, nine names on a card, is still unwritten and is still the line she cannot put her name to, **and she is not going to be able to write it in this volume.**

## Corrin Malle

**State:** **has answered the question nobody had ever asked him, and it was a finding, and he was not punished for it.** He has buried it. He used Marek once in a certifying room on the nineteenth and filed the attestation in a drawer, and he refused to take a reading off him in a corridor on the twenty-sixth, and he has said out loud that a spend on his watch is a spend with his name under it and that a Deputy who cannot explain a thing is entitled to call it the thing whose name he does not have to explain, which is in the Charter, Section 9. He reclassified item (a) of the thirtieth as a maintenance failure, and **he said both sentences out loud to Tovan Iles — that it is a true statement about the culvert and it is not a true statement about the thirtieth** — and he did it without heat and without apology and partly right. He moved Tovan to a copy desk on a form whose ground is true, and told her the whole of his reasoning including the sentence about the reader, and said he had not stopped respecting him for a second and had been the man who took his name off a watch. **In Chapter 42 he is given the reader's interior for the first time and it is not villainy.** An empty folder carried ninety minutes to a hearing. Two children and a sandwich carried up a hill on a Sunday because he wanted to watch a method he staffs succeed. **The discovery that the thing he reached for on the third of the month was a rule, and that the rule worked, and that a word is a better hiding place than a name.** He is asked *what did the classification do*, which nobody has ever asked him, and answers it: **a classification removes a thing from the categories that can be complained about, and that is the design, and the design is older than him, and he has been in the building for thirty years and cannot find the author.** He then volunteers the batch's institutional finding — **this office has never in its history put a line in a return for a thing that was not done, and he does not know whether that is a rule or a shape.** He is right about the culvert and right that a Deputy who cannot explain a thing should not leave the effect unattributed, and he is wrong about one thing and the chapter knows it and the reader is not told. **He is not reclassified, not censured, not moved, not put on a copy desk, and the review recommends nothing against him and says so in a room with a clerk in it — and he says, in one flat sentence in a corridor, *that is a good outcome for me*, and then works out that he will have to say it again in a month to somebody who cared, and that there will not be anybody who cared, and that that is the correct arrangement, and that the correctness of the arrangement is the reason he did it.** **He is not caught. He is not a villain. He has a reader he cannot use, a clerk he has lost, a record he can defend, and a column he caused to exist. His testimony against Rook is Volume 11 and is not anticipated anywhere in this batch.**

## Oren Vale

**State:** **Chapter 34 happened and it was a bracket.** He went down a river path to the south bank with his son, looked at a banded timber stack and a pump on a pallet and a painted date nobody had looked at since the autumn, and gave Marek in about ninety seconds the whole of what nine people carrying shoring timber to a cracked headwall is worth: forty minutes to get the gear onto the flat and a night to do the work, the timber first and the pump second because the pump's bed is the shoring, one man who has stood on a Bellweather wall before and is not on the crew, and a wall that is good for the next front. ## Cassian Rook

**State:** **In prose for the first time in Chapter 27, and the reader should be more afraid of him now than of Malle.** Fifty-two, Director for six years, emergency warden of Low Spindle for nine before that. He sent for Marek at eight in the morning and wrote *bring the ninth of the month; bring the order and not the evidence, because I do not want the evidence and I want the order*, and he is the only man in the building who has treated Marek fairly and usefully at the same time. **He said the true thing at the top of the session so that nobody would spend the morning defending themselves against it.** He read his authorization aloud exactly as written and explained what it required. He took the four licensed carriers' money as a fact in the world rather than a problem. He used Marek for ninety seconds on the order, wrote a slip the size of a stamp docket, put it in a drawer, said *it does not help me at all*, and moved on. He **asked the one question nobody else asked** — how many options does a prepared district have, and accepted the answer *one* with thanks, and had it minuted in the words *that the residual exposure of a prepared district is a single option and is not a compensable loss*, on the day he is the one making the claim, because in four years somebody will say the districts had nothing at stake. He is never wrong about the weather, never lies, never monologues, and his approval stamp is on the Bellweather notice of the twenty-seventh and on the docket that carried the Chapter 29 claim to the ward ledger bench. **He is the reason the volume's method feels reasonable, and his authorization is the vehicle by which a claim with no concurring seat and no positive routing effect became a claim at all.**
