# World

## The city of Veyra

Veyra is a dense city-state built in the shallow basin known as **the Sill**. Lake Orra lies to the west, the Nacre Range closes the northern horizon, and the broad floodplain of the **Low Sill** opens toward the southern farms. The city has grown around nine watersheds rather than a single street plan. Each watershed has its own storm ward, reservoir, market, and local assembly.

Veyra is modern in its civic life but not frictionless. Electric trams run beneath elevated weather rails. Airships dock at the Kestrel Yards. Print presses, radio weather bulletins, pneumatic dispatch tubes, mechanical calculators, and storm-glass instruments sit alongside older communal rain gauges. Most people do not think of the weather as untouched nature; they think of it as a dependency that must be maintained, budgeted, and distrusted.

The skyline is low enough to see weather coming. Copper gutters divide the roofs. Water tanks line the upper terraces. On clear mornings, the nine ward towers show as thin black lines above the haze, each carrying a different color of signal glass.

## Weather in ordinary life

Rain is water, crop insurance, travel delay, and civic ritual. Forecasts determine school closures, airship windows, reservoir releases, planting dates, hospital staffing, and whether a market can safely open. A forecast is posted in public halls and copied into district ledgers. People learn to read the glass marks even when they cannot read the technical notation.

Veyra has several kinds of weather work:

- **Forecasters** observe patterns, certify claims, and maintain the central instruments.
- **Wardens** build and repair towers, clear culverts, run rescues, and enforce safety closures during storms.
- **Rain readers** work at the neighborhood level, interpreting local pressure and the behavior of crops, animals, pipes, and people.
- **Route crews** fly and drive through the weather, using both official windows and local knowledge.
- **Ledger clerks** record what was predicted, what was spent, who relied on the claim, and what compensation remains unpaid.

The official weather office is trusted for its instruments and feared for its omissions. A district can accept a forecast, challenge it, or refuse it if it can show that it has a workable alternative and accepts the consequences. In practice, that right has been narrowed by law, budgets, and the assumption that only experts can calculate the cost of being wrong.

## The Meridian

The **Meridian** is the civic weather engine beneath Crown Basin. It is a vast lattice of storm-glass, copper veins, salt-etched stone, and living maintenance roots. It does not create rain from nothing. It reads, stores, and coordinates atmospheric sequence across the nine watersheds.

The Meridian was built after the **Unweathered Year**, when several atmospheric spells collided and rain stopped for eleven days. Crops failed, reservoirs fell, and the city nearly fractured. The original builders believed that the danger came from uncoordinated weather work: every neighborhood had made a reasonable decision that became catastrophic when combined with the decisions of its neighbors. They built a common instrument to show the order of pressure, charge, moisture, and human action before a spell became irreversible.

The modern Weather Office treats the Meridian as a central forecast engine. That is only its later and incomplete purpose. Its older civic chambers were designed to display conflicting turns and return the choice to the people who would live with the result. A later emergency amendment made one official claim binding across the whole basin. The amendment is legal, not magical; the engine obeyed the new arrangement because the network was designed to obey shared sequence.

The Meridian can be entered through Crown Basin and the nine ward towers. It cannot be understood from above. Every major revelation about its history is tied to a physical repair, a surviving instrument, or a local account.

## History

### The Unweathered Year

Thirty years before the opening, an attempted rainmaking project in the northern wards produced a sequence collision. Rain vanished, riverbeds dried, and several weather wards became dead metal. The city survived through stored water and emergency shelters, but the event left a cultural fear of uncontrolled weather magic.

### The First Compact

Nine watershed crews built the first shared sequence network. Farmers, ferry crews, wardens, glassworkers, and forecasters all contributed readings. The Compact required that a public forecast show its evidence, its limits, and at least one meaningful alternative. It was imperfect and often slow. It also distributed responsibility instead of hiding it.

### The Quiet Years

After a decade of relative stability, the Weather Council centralized the network. Forecasts became official claims; local readings were copied into a single ledger; compensation for a wrong forecast became easier to postpone. The reasons were practical: wars, trade, and population growth made disagreement expensive. The result was institutional drift rather than a single founding lie.

### The Meridian Cut

Twelve years before the opening, a group attempted to disconnect a failing ward without triggering a basin-wide sequence reversal. Forecast researcher **Anja Vale** discovered that the office had been hiding the cost of several “successful” forecasts. During the Meridian Cut, she entered Crown Basin to preserve an unspent evacuation route. The official account says she was lost when the Cut collapsed. In truth, she deliberately closed a branch that would have sent the collapse into Low Spindle, and the route she preserved was later erased from the public record.

The Cut remains a legal scar. The Weather Office calls it a maintenance accident. Families in Low Spindle call it the year the rain learned to lie.

## Regions and locations

### The Weatherglass

The Weather Office campus sits on the eastern ridge above the city. It contains the glassworks, sequence charts, public forecast hall, and the central ledger rooms. The buildings are beautiful and badly heated. Important staff work happens in corridors, kitchens, and maintenance sheds rather than on the observation floor.

### Low Spindle

A low industrial and residential district where several culverts share the lowest flood line. It has the least reliable public weather equipment and the most residents dependent on storm warnings. Low Spindle is where official forecasts become personal: a closed market, a missed repair, or a school kept open can affect a whole neighborhood.

### Kestrel Yards

The airship docks, repair slips, and route offices on the western side of Lake Orra. Independent crews work here alongside licensed carriers. The yards are loud, practical, and skeptical of the Weather Office. Tarin Senn’s Kestrel Line is one of the few routes that publishes its own uncertainty notes.

### Hearthward

The terraced residential and market district built on the eastern slopes. Its rooftop rain gardens and seed vaults make it politically powerful, but its people are not passive: they organize local readings and have refused several official forecasts they consider too broad.

### Crown Basin

The dry central basin above the Meridian’s upper chambers. It contains the old civic observatory, the sealed First Compact chamber, and the core lock. The basin looks empty until a storm moves across it, when the buried network becomes visible through the ground as a pattern of standing water.

### The Nine Ward Towers

Each tower serves a watershed and connects to the Meridian through buried veins. Towers can be repaired locally, but a tower that reconnects without a public warrant can be used to issue a hidden claim. The tower network is both infrastructure and a political map.

## Factions and institutions

### Veyran Weather Office

The official forecasting service. It is staffed by researchers, instrument makers, archivists, and public clerks. Its stated purpose is to protect the basin; its practical purpose is to preserve a single authoritative account. The Office contains sincere public servants as well as people who have learned that inconvenient uncertainty is dangerous to careers.

### Storm Wardens

A citywide service whose field crews respond to actual weather. Wardens answer to the central office for equipment and to local assemblies for emergency judgment. Their crews are the first to see the cost of a forecast after the paper warning has been filed.

### The Cloud Exchange

A private market for airship windows, storm charters, rain credit, and weather insurance. The Exchange does not create weather; it decides who gets access to a reliable window. Some brokers are merely greedy. Others believe that a market is the only honest way to price uncertainty. Their argument becomes dangerous when private certainty is paid for by public loss.

### Local assemblies and watershed councils

Neighborhood bodies that manage schools, water, roads, and emergency shelters. They can issue local weather notices, challenge Office claims, and refuse a binding forecast if they assume responsibility for an alternative plan. The Weather Council has spent decades narrowing this authority.

### The Quiet Ledger

A dispersed network of archivists, instrument makers, former Office clerks, and ward technicians who preserve suppressed forecast records. The name comes from their habit of leaving blank columns in old ledgers. The group is not a uniform cult. Some members want truth published; some believe dangerous knowledge should remain controlled; some are simply preserving evidence of what the Office did.

## Everyday consequences of a spend

A spend is often noticed as an absence rather than a wound. A repair crew arrives to find that no one remembers which material was stored in the relevant shed. A market changes its opening hours because an alternative supplier never made the trip. A family remembers planning to leave by a road that is now closed, but no record explains when the road became unusable. Some people call the feeling **counter-memory**; others call it bad luck.

The absence is real. It is not proof that every person remembers every lost possibility, and it is not a hallucination shared by everyone. It is evidence that a branch which could have supported a particular choice has been closed.

## Author-facing world endpoint

By the ending, Veyra is not a city without weather danger. It is a city with a **Weather Commons**: nine locally governed forecast cells, public records of binding claims, visible compensation, an appeal process, and a rotating council. The Meridian still coordinates atmospheric sequence, but it can no longer merge all local claims into one unquestionable answer. The final safety is shared uncertainty, not perfect control.
