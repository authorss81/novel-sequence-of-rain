# Premise

## One-sentence premise

In the rainward city of Veyra, a weather researcher discovers that atmospheric spells follow a hidden six-movement sequence and that every precise forecast that people act upon closes one still-possible branch of reality, forcing him to choose between saving the city and keeping the future open.

## Core promise

Marek Vale can read what a storm is about to do before it does it. His advantage is also a moral debt: a binding claim becomes dependable by displacing a competing possibility. The reader should feel the appeal of a reliable warning and the danger of treating people as variables in a prediction.

The novel is a weather mystery with a civic-engineering engine. Storms create immediate problems; forecasts affect farms, schools, water, trade, and airships; institutions compete over who may make a binding claim; and every solved crisis reveals a larger history behind the Meridian, the civic weather engine beneath Veyra.

## The canonical rule

A **read** is an observation. It costs only concentration, fatigue, and the risk of a false impression. A **binding** is a precise claim with a place, a time or trigger, an effect, and a witness that is written, spoken to an acting person, entered into an instrument, or used to authorize a route, evacuation, permit, or market order. Only a binding can close a branch.

A successful binding makes its stated route more stable and usually makes the claim true, but it is not an infallible prophecy. A false binding still closes the branch it displaced and rebounds as a **counterturn**. Repeating the same claim does not spend again; contradicting a bound claim does. In public shorthand, people may call a binding a “prediction”; technically, the binding threshold above is what spends. The reader therefore has to ask three separate questions: what can be seen, what can be promised, and who has agreed to live with the cost.

## Starting situation

Marek is a competent but junior weather researcher at the Veyran Weather Office. He repairs instruments, checks storm-cell patterns, and writes forecasts that other people are expected to trust. He does not begin with a command over the weather or with an ability to see the future. He begins with a precise understanding of instruments and a habit of accepting responsibility for warnings that should never have been his alone.

During the Bellweather Front, a storm forms around a rare six-movement collision. Marek sees a turn that can keep an airship corridor open, while the only available alternative seems to put a low district under a wall of rain. He binds a claim: the Kestrel corridor will remain passable through 04:00. The claim displaces the still-available branch in which a late repair convoy can reach the Lower Sill culvert before the front arrives. The airships survive. By dawn the repair convoy has no feasible departure, the culvert remains broken, and a south-bank road is sealed by ordinary storm landslide damage; the missing repair opportunity is the spend, not the landslide itself. The Office files the loss as ordinary maintenance failure.

The incident is small enough to be denied and useful enough to be repeated. It is Marek’s first proof that a forecast does not merely describe a future. It spends one. The cost is not a moral metaphor or a percentage; it is a route, a repair, a message, a harvest, or a choice that can no longer occur in this world.

## The central choice

The series repeatedly asks: who has the right to decide which possibility a community must give up?

Marek wants to eliminate uncertainty because uncertainty has hurt the people he loves. He believes that a sufficiently careful, centralized forecast can save more lives than an open argument. His growth is not from prediction to omnipotence. It is from private certainty toward shared, visible responsibility.

A forecast is not a neutral report. It is a claim made strong enough to bind the sequence. That claim can save a city, but it can also hide the cost from the people who bear it. The final answer is not to stop forecasting. It is to make every binding forecast answerable to the people who live inside its consequences.

## Central mystery

Why does a binding forecast make an alternative impossible, who authorized the Meridian Cut, and why was Anja Vale’s Ninth Ward route erased from the public record?

The reader initially learns that the cost is real, not a metaphor. Later, the story reveals that the Meridian was originally a shared civic instrument for showing competing weather turns. During the Meridian Cut, the then-emergency director Cassian Rook signed the order to disconnect a failing ward; he believed a contained collapse was safer than a basin-wide reversal, and he later ordered the route evidence suppressed when the choice became politically impossible. The central mystery is therefore a mystery of design, ownership, complicity, and responsibility rather than a hidden god or an alternate-world trick. The pre-Meridian origin of atmospheric sequence remains deliberately outside the promised answer.

## Series promise

Each major arc must change what a forecast can mean, who can make one, or who pays for it. The plot alternates field action, laboratory work, political pressure, training, investigation, relationship work, and recovery. A volume resolves an immediate weather or civic crisis while exposing a deeper layer of the Meridian.

## Relationship policy

There is one slow-burn relationship: Marek and Tarin Senn, an airship captain and storm-ward mechanic. Their romance is secondary to the weather mystery and grows through operational trust, disagreement, repair, and shared consequences. There is no harem structure and no instant attraction replacing character development.

## Tone and boundaries

The tone is grounded scientific fantasy: brass instruments, paper ledgers, electric trams, airships, rain on glass, civic budgets, and dangerous weather. The magic should remain physically observable even when its cause is mysterious. The story may be tense, intimate, and occasionally hopeful, but it does not end by pretending that uncertainty has been defeated.
