# Chapter 45 — Dismissed from the Field

The review sat through the weekend, which it had not been scheduled to do, because the Chair had a number of items and a budget of days and had told the clerk on the Thursday that the days were a budget and not a preference, and Mrs Ireson had worked Saturday and Sunday in that chamber twice in nine years and had not minded and had said so.

There were four things on the agenda for the Sunday of the forty-fifth day. Three of them were a formality and the fourth was not, and everybody in the room knew which was which, and the room had a particular air on a Sunday morning that it never has on a weekday, which is the air of a building that has been working at the weekend and is slightly ashamed of it.

Cassian Rook sat at the end of the table and had been at the end of the table for five days and had not once been late and had not once interrupted anybody, and Marek Vale had spent five days watching a Director do nothing, carefully, in public, and had come to the conclusion — which was new, and which he did not enjoy — that doing nothing carefully in public for five days is a heavier kind of work than he had ever done in his life.

The censure was read out at about ten past ten.

---

"Item four," said Rook. "Assessor, the finding is minuted and the recommendation is drafted and I have read both and I have two changes to the wording and I will take them at the end of the afternoon and not now, because I am not going to use a document as a way of getting to a man before the room has had the finding.

"Clerk, will you read the censure."

Mrs Ireson read it. She read it in the flat voice that is used for things that have already been decided in four other rooms, and she read all of it, and there was no part of it she enjoyed and no part of it she altered.

"Censure entered in the record of the Veyran Weather Office against M. Vale, reader, on the ground that — and I am reading the ground, not summarising it —

"*That the attesting reader made an unproved assertion in a public forum, namely the technical cell of the Council on the nineteenth day of the month, and was unable to support it; and that the assertion was of a cost; and that a cost which cannot be supported cannot be acted upon and cannot be compensated.*

"*And that the reader repeated the substance of that assertion before this review on the thirty-seventh day of the month, in general terms, and again was unable to support it, and that the instrument from which the reading was taken is not an instrument of this office and has no registered chain behind it, and that the reader stated this himself when asked.

"*And that the reader has at no time produced a document, an instrument, a witness, or a name, in respect of any cost, at any time, on any occasion, and that no such production was required of him and that the record does not show that it was ever offered.

"*Penalty: dismissal from field duty. Censure entered in the Office's own record against the reader. No other penalty is recommended. No appeal is recommended, there being nothing in the ground of this censure to appeal.*"

---

Every clause of it is true.

And a cost which cannot be supported cannot be acted upon and cannot be compensated. That sentence is a truism, and it is the Charter's own position, and it was said out loud in that chamber five days ago by a Deputy for Field Forecasts, in his own words, and nobody in the room wrote it down at the time because it had not occurred to any of them that it was going to be load-bearing.

And he had not produced a document, an instrument, a witness or a name at any time. That was true as well, and it had been true since the eighth of the month, and it had been true about a district's rain-garden rota and a ferry's night and a two-line sheet, and it had been true about a ward's school and about a shed with a banded timber stack in it, and the sentence says *no such production was required of him*, which is also true, and which is the single most generous clause in the document and which nobody had to fight for.

**Nobody had asked him to produce anything. In six weeks, in a building of a hundred and eighty people in a technical cell and a Director and a Deputy and a records man and an archivist, nobody had ever once asked Marek Vale for a piece of paper.**

The finding was that right out loud is a procedural offence.

He had known it was coming since the Saturday morning, in that chamber, in about four seconds, when the assessor had said out loud that she would put the number in the minute and not the reasons, and he had stood outside afterwards and worked out what the number was for, and had not slept properly for it. He had come in that morning braced for a room and had got five days of correct people and one finding and he had known since the Saturday that this was where the machinery went.

And it had gone exactly where he had known it would go, on the correct day, in the correct words, for the correct reason, and there was not one thing in that document he could have got wrong, and there was nothing in it he could have fought, and nobody had been cruel to him in the writing of it, and three people in that room were about to be asked to sit there while it was read and not one of them had to be a villain to do it.

---

"Mr Vale," said Rook. "Is there anything in the ground you wish to address before it is entered?"

And here is the thing that nobody in that room knew, including Rook, including Malle, including Anwen Sarr and Tarin Senn and Mrs Ireson.

There were three things on the table.

There was a plate in a tube in his coat, in the maintenance shop on the ridge, in a work coat hanging on the back of the door he had not shut, with a Witness movement in its substrate dated twelve years back, arriving in the glass from below, in a tower's own stamped margin, in the ninth ward tower, in a locked room, in the poorest ward in the city, on Office glass in an Office holder. He could not produce it, because producing it means a maintenance report about a plate that carries a dead woman's filing mark, and it would put the ninth ward's feed and the ninth ward's public frame and a poor ward's tower in front of a Council inquiry, and the ninth ward has four hundred and forty people on its low ground and an unreadable notice and no money, and the ward would be the story, and the collision would be a footnote.

There was a folded sheet of four hundred and eleven words in the second drawer of his bench, entirely true, headed with a plate number, containing the order in which the six movements arrived on the ninth of the month, and it cannot be filed anywhere, because the moment it is filed it stops being a maintenance report and becomes a reader's private observation with a plate number that does not exist in any register in the world.

And there was a chart on the north wall of the chart room on this building, eleven inches from the edge of a sheet of paper in a frame, with a provenance line in his mother's hand, a violet square with a bell struck across it, a sequence reference with a half of it gone, and an inch and a half of the upper margin taken off with a good blade and a steady hand.

Any one of those three things would have made this censure impossible. Not by refuting it — by making it *unnecessary*, because a reader who can produce a document does not get censured for producing an unproved assertion.

He had about nine seconds. He did not take any of them out.

That is the whole of it and there is no more to say about it than that. He sat at the end of a bench in a chamber with the windows shut on the Sunday morning of the forty-fifth day and he made a decision in about nine seconds and he did not take a thing out of his coat.

He did not do it to protect himself. He did not do it to protect a district, because there was no district he could name and nobody had asked him for one.

He did it because he is a man who has spent eleven years and a few hundred plates learning that a thing you put on a table becomes a thing with a number on it, and the number is the plate number of nine inches of glass cut out of a pane the ninth ward had paid for and had never been sent, and the moment that number is in a Council file the ninth ward's tower is in a Council file, and there is a keeper in a hut in that tower who has never asked him a question in eleven years, and there are four hundred and forty people on a low ground with a public notice glass that has been unreadable for a fortnight.

And he will pay for that, and he knows the price exactly, and the price is a career and a censure in a room on a Sunday with a clerk in it, and he has worked it out, and the working-out took nine seconds.

He did not say one word of this to anybody, then or ever.

---

"I have nothing to address," said Marek Vale.

"Clerk, will you enter that."

"Entered."

"Then the Chair will ask one question and it is a procedural question and I am asking it as the Chair and I want it answered in the room, and I would like it answered by the reader and not by his officer."

Rook looked down the table.

"Mr Vale. The reader's attestation of the order of the movements on the ninth of the month, entered by name in the day file on the twenty-seventh and read out in this chamber on that morning, is to remain in the file. Does the reader wish it to remain?"

Marek had not expected that.

"I do," he said.

"It will remain," said Cassian Rook. "Clerk, enter it."

They made him sign.

The censure was a sheet, and the sheet was in the ordinary form, and there was a line at the bottom for the reader's signature, and he signed it. It took about four seconds and it was the last piece of paper Marek Vale signed in the Weather Office for anything to do with a forecast, and the pen was a good one and the ink was dry and the clerk squared the tray afterwards, which she did with the flat of her hand, the way a clerk does, and which nobody in that room had any way of knowing was the exact gesture that a bench clerk makes when a thing has gone into the world and cannot be got back.

He signed it, and then he asked his one question.

"The attestation stays with my name on it," he said. "Then I would like to ask a second thing, which is whether the reader's name is to come off anything else in this file as a result of the censure."

"Marek," said Corrin Malle.

"I am asking it as a procedural matter and I am asking it in a room."

Sabin Orle answered it, because it was a records matter and the assessor answered records matters.

"Nothing comes off anything," she said. "That is the ruling and it is the correct ruling and I want the room to understand what has just been prevented, because in a fortnight somebody will say that the censure took the reader's name out of the file, and it did not, and the reason it did not is that the assessor of this review read Rule 6 of the Charter of Public Notice on the Saturday and Rule 6 says that a reclassification does not alter the truth of a record, and a censure is not a reclassification, and a censure is not a finding about a reading.

"Your name is on the attestation. Your name is on the claim of the twenty-ninth. Your name is on the reader's statement of the nineteenth and in the annex, bound separately, and it is going to be read by nine seats in four weeks. And your name is now against a censure in this Office's own record, which is a *different* document and it does not touch any of the others, and I have had it indexed separately this morning with a different reference and I would like you to know that, because in about two years somebody is going to try to argue that the censure attaches to the attestation, and it does not, and the reason it does not is that I thought about it on the Saturday and it cost me four hours."

"Thank you, madam," said Marek Vale.

He signed the second line, which was the acknowledgement, and it took about three seconds, and that was the whole of the procedure, and the total elapsed time of the censure of Marek Vale, from the reading of the ground to the last signature, was about eleven minutes, and he was not dismissed from the Office, and he was not suspended from the glass benches, and nobody had told him that he would not be, and he did not ask.

---

There were three people in that chamber who had come for that item, and not one of them did anything.

**Tarin Senn** was at the back, on the standing side, because a claim built on the Kestrel Line's glass names the Line in the sponsor's own line, and the Line's route officer was on the citation list, and she had come for four days and had not once asked for the floor. She did not stand. She was not asked to. She was in that room for four days as a witness of fact and she was asked four questions and answered all four of them, and one of them was whether the Line had been asked to put a hull in anybody's claim, and she said yes, on the twenty-eighth, at the boards, in front of nine people, and that the Line had asked for four things and had got all four and would take all four again.

And when the censure was read, she sat where she was with her hands on the table in front of her and did not move, and there was a moment — about a second and a half, and Marek did not see it because he was looking at the sheet — where the whole of the yard's four conditions was in the back of her head at once, and the fourth of them, which says *a claim built on the Line's glass names the Line in the sponsor's own line and not in a footnote*, and the line was a wrist, and the wrist was published, and the cost of that wrist is a censure of the man who wrote the words *corridor* on a sheet, and there is not one line on the sheet that could have said *wrist* —

and then it passed, and she sat through the rest of it, and afterwards, in the corridor, she said one sentence to him and it was this:

"That is a bad morning and I am not going to say a single other thing about it in this building, and if you ask me in a yard I will tell you what I think, and it will not be this."

**Anwen Sarr** was in the room and did not speak and was not asked to, and she had put in a clause on the Thursday of the thirty-fifth day that was going to be adopted and was going to be printed and was going to be on a public table, and the man it was drafted to protect was being censured ten days later for having said out loud the thing the clause was about.

She had watched him not produce anything. Nobody in that room knew what she had watched, because what she had watched was a man declining a rescue in the last nine seconds of his own career, and she is the only person in this basin who has spent six years asking a room for a document, and she knows exactly what it looks like when somebody has one and does not put it down, and she knew it in about a second, at about ten past ten, and she has not repeated it to anybody including Kell Bray.

She did not speak. She was not asked to.

**Aldis Prynne** was in the room as a witness to the order of the movements on the ninth of the month, and he was asked nothing, because nothing about the order of the movements on the ninth of the month bears on a censure for an unproved assertion about a cost. He was there for the first forty minutes and then he was let go, and he went, and he had been in the building for thirty-one years and he had never once been asked where a reading came from and he had told the truth about a copy for free in a room and been thanked for it by nobody.

---

And then Corrin Malle read the sheet out again, and handed it across the table, and said the only sentence he was entitled to say.

"If the reader's name comes off, the next one signs it alone."

Marek Vale took the sheet.

"Cor," he said. "You said that to somebody else."

"I said it to a copy clerk," said Corrin Malle, "on the third of the month, in about ninety seconds, in a corridor, and it is the sentence in the minutes of this review, and it is in the minutes because he said it out loud in this room on the Saturday, in front of the assessor and a clerk, and did not know he was quoting a Deputy, and I did not correct him, because I would rather it went into a minute than not.

"And I said it to her and not to you, and I am not going to pretend that was a kindness, and I am not going to pretend it was a cruelty, and I would like to say what it was, because you are owed that and nobody in this room is going to think of it.

"It was this. On the third of the month I had a form in front of me with one line on it and a clerk who had done a correct thing and would lose a glass-tender's examination for it, and there were two names available to me to write on it, and I picked yours, and I was right to, and I have not stopped being right about that, and I said that sentence to a copy clerk in a corridor because I wanted her to hear the reason, and the reason was **you**.

"If the reader's name comes off, the next one signs it alone. That sentence was for her. It was never for you. You are the last man in this basin who can be shown a plate, and I could not put your name on that form, and I said so to the only person in the building who writes things down for a living, and she has been at a copy desk for a month."

Marek Vale stood in a chamber with the windows shut and understood that a man had spent a month defending him by removing him and had said so, in public, in front of a clerk, on the record, and would not be thanked for it and would not be forgiven for it.

"Deputy," he said, and that was all, and he sat down.

---

The session rose at half past twelve and the Chair took two changes to the wording of the report, both of them small, and both of them improvements, and neither of them had anything to do with the censure, which is the way it is done.

Then, at about twenty past one, with the room half empty and the clerk closing a folder, Cassian Rook said the thing that Marek Vale carried to the end of his life and never repeated and never once managed to get out from under.

"Mr Vale. One thing and then I am going to the front of the building, because I have a basin."

"Director."

"I have read the finding. I have read the assessor's sentence underneath it, which is that the reason is not that it was not written down but that the record has never had anywhere to put it. That is a good sentence and it is the best sentence anybody has said in this basin in my working life and I am going to be quoting it in a report in about six weeks, and I am going to be quoting it with a recommendation attached, and the recommendation is going to be a column.

"And I want to say one thing to you about the censure, in front of this room, and I am not going to say it in a corridor and I am not going to say it to you alone, and if you would rather I had said it in a corridor then I would have done it in a corridor and I would not have blamed you, and I have to say it here because it is going to appear in about four years in somebody's argument and I would rather this room had it in the minute first.

"I authorized the claim of the twenty-seventh. I sent it down with my stamp on it, and it was three lines, and it named no place, no time, no effect, no alternative, no sponsor and no expiry, and it put a claim on a ward ledger with a bell on it and no witness and no countersignature.

"It compels nobody. It certifies nothing. It requires none of the six conditions. All it does is make the man who enters the claim answerable, and that is what it is for, and that is what I intended, and I have never been asked to explain it and I am not going to be asked twice.

"And it is the reason there is a censure on you this morning, and there is no version of the sentence in the minute you have just signed in which anybody in this building had to lie to produce it, and nobody did, and I am going to be asked that in about four years by somebody who is cleverer than me.

"So I am putting it in the minute now, while I still have the standing to."

"Clerk," said Mrs Ireson.

"Enter: *That the authorization of the twenty-seventh was given on the ground that a single claim should cover the yard side and the low side; that it was the instrument by which a claim was entered with no concurring seat and no positive routing effect; and that the Director accepts that the censure of the reader is a correct consequence of the method as authorized and does not attribute it to the individual.*"

Nobody spoke.

"That is a good sentence," said Marek Vale, "and it is the fourth good sentence in four days that has been said in that room by somebody who is right, and I would like it observed that it is the only one that anybody is ever going to be able to act on."

The clerk wrote it down, and read it back, and squared the tray with the flat of her hand, and the review of the Bellweather method sat down and got on with the rest of its afternoon.
