# Chapter 34 — The Shape of the Loss

They walked down to the south bank on the Monday morning of the second day of the month because the road was cut, and it took them an hour and ten minutes, and Oren Vale did not say one word about the fact that a man who cannot hear properly on one side walks a river path badly.

Marek had a docket and a lamp and no reason that was his job, and the reason that was not his job was that a shed foreman had told a sergeant that nobody had told him not to go, and Marek had been carrying that sentence for four days the way you carry a plate with a crack in it.

Oren went down the bank on his own feet without the lamp and got there nine minutes before him and stood at the top of the flat with his hands behind his back and looked at it for about two minutes.

Then he went into the shed.

The timber was banded. Four bands, iron, done in the spring. The pump was on its pallet under the cover and the drum was dry. The notice board was outside the door with the sixth of the month on it in paint that had gone grey over the top.

Oren Vale looked at the timber and the pump and the board and then at the flat, and Marek stood in the doorway and waited for the ninety seconds, and got about a hundred and ten.

"Right," he said. "Say the number. Go on."

"I haven't got a number."

"Mr Vale—"

"You've come down a bank with a lamp to ask me a number," said Oren. "Say it out, and I'll give you the number, and then you can go up the hill and feel better."

Marek stood there with the lamp in his hand and the wind coming up the reach behind him and made himself say it, and he said all of it, and he said it in the wrong order, and none of it came out the way he had heard it in his own head on the hill.

"Nine men. A timber stack and a replacement dewatering pump. The wall's got a gravel face at the west cheek and it's shored twice and the second shoring's carrying the first. If they'd gone out on the thirtieth—"

"If they'd gone out on the thirtieth," said Oren Vale, "then in about forty minutes they'd have had the flatbed's nose on the mark, and that's the whole of what your forty minutes is, and I want you to understand that, because I have heard four people this week say the word *window* and not one of them knew what it was for."

He put his hand on the banded stack and did not move it.

"Forty minutes is how long you've got to get the *gear* onto the flat. The wall is not shored in forty minutes. Nothing that size is shored in forty minutes. You get the timber across and you get the pump across and you get nine men and a light, and then you have the night." He took his hand off the stack. "The night is the actual work. Baulks across the upstream face and pack the gravel and shore the headwall and set the pump on a hard bed, and the pump's bed *is* the shoring, so the timber goes first and the pump goes second, and if a man does it in the wrong order he has got a pump sitting on a wall that's still moving."

"How long does it take?"

"Six hours in good air with nine men and a light. It's not done in a night, it's *begun* in a night, and it is finished at about eleven the next morning by two men and a man who has stood on a Bellweather wall before." Oren Vale looked at the flat. "So the answer to your number is: nine men, two shifts, one night, a hundred-weight of lamp oil, about four hundred pounds of gear that would have gone back on a lorry, and one man who has shored one of these before, and he is not on your crew and he never has been."

"Who is he?"

"Dead," said Oren Vale. "Twice, now, if you count the other job."

---

They sat on the pump house step at the top of the reach for about twenty minutes, and it was not raining, and the reach was four feet lower than it had been on the Friday and going down slowly, and the freight apron was a spit of concrete with a new edge on it that was not finished.

"There is a front in three years," said Oren.

"Where do you get that?"

"From the same place you get everything, and I am not going to be snobbish about it." He got a folded sheet out of his inside pocket, because a mechanic of sixty-one who has kept his own arithmetic for eleven years has a folded sheet in his inside pocket, and gave it to his son, and it was four columns of pencil on the back of a district works form from about nine years ago, and every column was a year, and every column had a figure against it, and beside four of the years somebody had written a word.

The words were *front* and they were in four different hands.

"That's the ward's outfall," said Oren. "I did the run for nine years and Marda Loon has been doing it since and she does it off the same kind of sheet, and neither of us was ever asked for it. The years with a line under them are the ones that put water over a road in the low ground in under an hour. Nineteen, twenty-two, and the one before that was forty-one years ago, and the one before that is on the water office's own table, which is in the shed at the Weatherglass, in a public room, on a wall, and anybody may read it."

"How many years is a Bellweather?"

"The Office's own table says under four," said Oren Vale. "Which is a stupid thing to put on a wall and it is the reason I have a sheet in my pocket. Under four. So three is not a prophecy. Three is a Friday. You don't build anything and you don't mend anything on the strength of a Friday, but you would not cancel anything either, and that's the whole of what a calendar is."

"Would the money still be there?"

"What money?"

"The money. The repair's been funded for two years."

"The money's there," said Oren Vale. "The money was there in the spring and it's there now and it will be reversioned at the year end like everything else, and I know that because I sat on the district's works panel for nine years and I came off it in the ninth year, and the panel is the reason, and if you want the thing to happen, the panel does it, and if you don't want the thing to happen the panel doesn't do it, and the panel has never once in nine years cancelled anything in my hearing."

He turned the sheet over and wrote on the back of it, with a pencil he produced from the same pocket, in a hand Marek had last seen on a run sheet in a tower: *30th. Wall overdue. Timber in the shed. Pump on the pallet. Board repainted. Nine stood. No minute.*

"That's what happened," he said, "and there's no minute of it, and there never will be, and I've written it out because there isn't anybody else who's going to."

---

They walked back up the bank. It took an hour and twenty minutes and they got up onto the hard ground about two o'clock and into the yard off Sump Lane at about twenty past, which is a hard-surfaced yard the width of two carts behind a row of workshops, with a bench under a lean-to, and a vice, and about nine years of other men's work on the benches in the way of a yard that is rented by the week and shared.

There was a bracket off the third hook on the door of Oren Vale's end of the shed. It had been bent for a year. It was a plain iron hasp, a foot long, and it had been bent by a door that had been coming off true for about a decade, and there was a set of files on the bench and a hand drill and a cold chisel and a piece of bar stock.

Marek put his bag down and got the vice out from under the bench and set the hasp in it and put a bar against the kink and leaned on it, and the kink came out of it, and then he put the file on the flats and took the burr off, and then found the eye of the hasp full of scale and cleaned it out with a drift and a bradawl, and then dressed the face of the mend so it sat flat on the door, and then tried the door three times.

Four minutes. He did not speak while he was doing it, and the reason he did not speak was that the drill and the file and the brick wall in that yard are all on his left side and he could not hear any of them, and he did not want to say so, and he was aware the entire time that his father was standing about five feet away on his right-hand side and had been on his right-hand side for the whole four minutes without once having to move.

Oren Vale tried the door when he had finished.

"It closes."

"It closes."

"It closed badly for a year." He put it back on the hook. "I could have used a vice."

"You had a vice. It was under the bench."

"I had a vice for a year and I could not be bothered to get up off this chair," said Oren Vale, "and you are the first person who has been in this yard in six years who has not come with a list."

Then he went in and made tea, and there is a thing about his father's kitchen that has not changed and is not going to be described again in this book: a plate of cold something on the table from the morning, a clock in the passage, a hook on the back of the door with nothing on it now.

---

They ate. They did not talk about the claim, because there was no claim to talk about, because he had not told his father there was a claim, and he was not going to, and he understood while eating that he was not going to.

What he was going to be talked out of was not a fact. That is the thing he has had to get used to in the last month and has not, and it took him four days in a room on a ridge to begin to accept it. He was not going to be argued out of the claim. He was going to be handed, across a table, by a man who has never heard the word, the exact and correct reason why the claim was possible at all.

"Do you know what a low-water window is?"

"It's a state of the reach."

"It's a state of the reach," his father agreed, "and it is the only thing in the whole of this business that isn't weather, and that is why nothing happens."

Oren Vale put his tea down.

"You can go your whole life and only ever be shown numbers," he said. "Rain. Levels. A wind. A fall in a pressure glass. A front coming in on Thursday, a cycle, a watch, a foot of water in a drain, all of it, every bit of it, a number or a thing with a number hanging off it, and if you can put a number on a thing then that thing is a matter of public record and somebody has to send somebody. That's the machine. That is the entire machine and I have been on the wrong side of it for eleven years and so have you.

"A window on a reach is not a number. It's a state. A man in a shed on a Friday afternoon with a banded stack and a pump on a pallet has not got a number, and neither has a fortnight or a year or the wall. That job hasn't got a figure against it anywhere, so nobody has to go anywhere, and there is nobody to be angry with, and it isn't weather so it isn't anybody's business, and it sits there. It just sits there, year on year, on a board, in a colour, with a date on it, and the district reads its own drains, and the whole of this city can go under twice while the shed is loaded."

Marek put his cup down, and did it carefully, and heard nothing at all in the doing of it.

"That's the mechanism," he said.

"That's the mechanism," said Oren Vale. "You've said it in about four words, which is fewer than it took me eleven years and a sheet of paper. Whatever it is that has put nine men in that shed for a day and a half — and I'm not asking, and you needn't tell me, and I'd not be much use with it — whatever it is, it did it in a category. There's a slot for it somewhere and it went in the slot and nobody's hand is anywhere near it. That's how it happened. Not anybody deciding. Somebody classifying."

Nobody in that kitchen said anything for a while, and there was a clock in the passage and it was the same clock.

"Marek."

"Yes."

"What have you done this month?"

---

It was the question he had been not-asked for six weeks, and it was asked in the flattest possible voice, and it was asked by a man who had come down a river path to look at a timber stack and had then spent forty minutes of a good morning giving his son the mechanism of his own life.

Marek sat at a table in Low Spindle with a cup in his hand and about four hundred words available to him and every one of them a lie of some size, and the thing that he found — and he found it clearly, and he did not enjoy the clarity, and it was the only clean thing in that kitchen — was that the sentence he wanted was *I have done a very good piece of work this month and it cost a thing I cannot name and I am going to do it again.*

"Glass," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No."

"Then it isn't," said Oren Vale, and got up, and put his cup in the sink, and turned round. "I've not got the energy this week and I never have on a Monday, and you've not got it either, you can see it on you. And you've come down here and asked me a number and I've given you the number and you wrote it on the back of my sheet, and that's a thing I would have done for you at any time in the last six years and I would not have made a meal of it."

He filled the kettle.

"Stay for the tea and go up the hill," he said. "And Marek — come on a Tuesday when it's a nuisance and nothing's happened. You keep coming at the end."

Marek stayed for the tea. He did not tell him about the plate in the tube in his coat, which had a Witness in it dated twelve years back, and he did not tell him about the chart margin, or the cuff, or the annex, or the four hundred and eleven words in the second drawer, or the claim, or the bell, or the four hundred yards of muddy reach, and he was aware that this was the fifth thing in six weeks he had not told him and that it was the fifth thing in six weeks he had come to this kitchen for.

And he was aware, in the way you are aware of a thing you have been carrying for a year and have only just noticed the weight of, that the man who had spent eleven years telling this ward that it runs on a rota and not on a glass had been right about a pipe and right about a ward and had been listened to by nobody at all, and had been listened to by nobody including his son, and that on that particular subject his son had come down a river path and got it in ninety seconds.

---

He walked back up the hill in the afternoon and took the long way, because the trams were slow and the light held, and he did not sleep that night and did not try.

The sheet was in his father's inside pocket and the sheet was in his head, and the four columns of years with a word against four of them were the most useful piece of evidence he had acquired in a month, and it proved nothing whatever, and it was not admissible, and it would not survive a conversation with a Deputy.

And the wall was still standing on a gravel face in a reach, shored twice, with a third shoring worth about four hundred pounds of timber and labour and a pump hire sitting banded in a shed with a board outside it, and it would be re-shored from other timber, or it would not be re-shored, and there is a front in three years, and three is not a prophecy, it is a Friday.

He got to the ridge at about seven and went into the shop and put the tube on the bench and stood looking at the low ground out of a window that faced the wrong way for it, and did not go to the chart room, and did not write anything down.
