# Chapter 43 — The Board at the Freight Apron

There is no bell on a municipal freight apron, and there is no gate, and the gate that people think of is a piece of chain across a cart road with a chain's worth of slack in it and a padlock that has been broken since the spring. Beyond the chain it is a hundred and forty yards of concrete with a new edge on the upstream side that is not finished, and a reach on the other side of it that had come down four feet since the middle of the month and was sitting on its lowest mark of the year, going up again as slowly as it had come down.

Petrie kept a shed at the far end of the apron. He had kept it for about thirty years and the shed was forty feet by twenty and it was timber on a low sleeper floor and the door had a chain on it and the door had a chain on it because a shed on an apron is a shed people come into.

There were nine men in the yard. There had been nine men in the yard since the sixth of the month and they were nine on the Thursday of the forty-second day, and they had not been stood down, and nothing had happened, and they were being paid, and Petrie had run out of ways to spend a shift four days into the fourth week.

On the Tuesday the water office sent a clerk.

He was a decent young man with a satchel and a form and about eleven minutes' worth of embarrassment, and the form was headed *disposal of retained stock*, and the second box asked for a district works number, and the third box asked whether the district was instructing disposal *on this authority* or *on the district's own*, and Petrie read all three boxes standing in the doorway of his own shed with the door open behind him, and then he read them again.

"Right," he said. "No."

"Mr — the wall's gone, sir. It's in the reach. Whatever the timber was for—"

"It's not for the wall."

"It's *shoring* timber, sir."

"It's timber that a district paid for," said Petrie, "against an order, and it is stored against that order, and there is an order number on the band's tag and there is a number on the pump's drum and I will give you both of them if you have got a pen, and neither of them is a wall. That wall's in the reach. That timber is for the next one."

There was about four seconds of the apron.

"That is not a disposal decision, is it," said the clerk, carefully, and got out of the way of a bollard.

"It is a shed keeping a load," said Petrie, "and a shed is not a disposal site, and there is nobody in this basin who can tell me I have to let go of a thing that somebody in a different building has paid for, and I have been doing this thirty years and nobody has ever come down this road to stand anybody down, and I would know, because they would have to come down the road, and I would have seen the cart."

---

On the Wednesday the district's works clerk came, and he had a different form.

That was the sign.

It was a printed enamel sign about eighteen inches by twelve, district works office, and it said in a good plain hand:

**SOUTH BANK DEPOT — LOAD HELD. NO DISPOSAL.**

And in the bottom corner, small, the district works office's name and a serial number.

"Right," said Petrie, and looked at it, and did not say anything for a bit.

"You can have it up," said the works clerk. "It's nothing. It's a notice. It's — look, it's a printed sign, it's got the office's name on it and a number, and it's got to be up by the end of the week or the item stays open, and the item stays open the works panel sees it, and the works panel—"

"Schedules," said Petrie.

"...schedules it, yes. So it's got to be up."

Petrie looked at the sign for about as long as a man looks at a thing he is going to refuse.

"No," he said.

"Sir, it's a notice. It's not a—"

"I've read it four times," said Petrie, "and it's a good bit of printing and your office has put its name on the bottom of it, and the day this shed's load goes anywhere, or the day your office decides it goes, or the day there's a works panel in this district that decides it goes — and there is one, and it sits nine times in ten years, I've sat in that room — the day any of that happens, somebody with your number on the bottom of it will have to walk down that road with a ladder and take it down, and while it's up, every cart that comes over that chain is going to read it and go round.

"I've had a load sitting in this shed since the sixth of the month and a board outside the door with the sixth of the month on it since the autumn, and the sixth of the month has been on that board four times and nobody's looked at it in four years, including me, and that's the correct arrangement. A thing that's been on a board four years and nobody's looked at is a thing that's still true. Your sign would be up for a fortnight and then it'd be a lie with your name on it, and I'd have to look at it every day for a fortnight, and I've thirty years of looking at things that are true."

The works clerk put the sign back in its box.

"I'll take it back," he said, "and I'll put a note on the item, and the note is going to say *load held at the depot, foreman objects to a disposal notice*."

"That'll do," said Petrie. "It'll be the truest thing anybody has put on that item in two years."

---

The Line came on the Wednesday afternoon.

Tarin Senn came down the bank on foot at about eleven in the morning with a satchel and a slate, and she had a purpose, and the purpose was that the yard's column of differences was now in a Council's file and two carriers had discounted the yard's cover in writing over it, and she was not in the mood to be told by a shed foreman that she had made a mistake.

"The Line will supply a board," she said. "Planks, seasoned, framed, and a man to letter it. Four hours' work, and it is ours, and it will carry the Line's name and no other name, and we will maintain it. It goes on the apron where the road comes over the chain, because that is where anybody going to the water office in a cart has to stop and read it."

"What will it say?"

"That is what I have come to ask you," said Tarin Senn. "And I am going to say what I think it should say and then you are going to tell me I am wrong, and if I am right I want it on the board in my words and not yours, and if I am wrong I want to know why on the same page."

Petrie put his tea down, which he had not realised he was holding.

"Go on, then."

"The load that never moved." Tarin Senn had the slate under her arm and she did not look at it, and she said it in the plain voice she used in a yard, and she said it once and then waited. "Sixth of the month. Four bands and a pump on a pallet and nine crew and a Union guarantee on the drum, loaded, ready, on the published window, and it did not move. And the freights went to Low Spindle and the first pump house is still under the reach and it will be there till the first, and the wall's in the water, and the road's cut, and there is not one notice in this basin that says a repair was not done. And the only record of it anywhere in this city is a level book in this shed with a column in it that I have been told has been empty for thirty years.

"Put that on a board. At the apron. Where it can be read from a cart."

Petrie sat on the bench outside his own door for a while.

"No," he said.

Tarin Senn's jaw did about a millimetre.

"No?"

"Not because of the yard's name on it. You've offered the yard's name and I'd take the yard's name on it, and I've not got one opinion in the world against the Kestrel Line and if you put your name on this I'll not hear a word against it as long as I live." He stood up. "It's because you are going to put the thirtieth on it."

"I am."

"No."

"It is the day it happened—"

"It's the day somebody decided it didn't come off," said Petrie. "And there was no somebody. There is no somebody. I have had nine men stood in this shed for a day and a half and I have gone through it and I cannot tell you who did it to me, and I've told the district's warden that in writing, and I've told her it four times, and she wrote it down four times, and it's in her book.

"Now if there's a somebody's name on that board, this city will come and look at it. Not today. Not this year. But they will come, and they'll stand where you're standing and they'll read it, and if there's a name on it they'll find the man, and they'll shout at him, and he'll be a man in his forties with a cap on and a house and a family and he'll never work again. And he won't have done anything. That's the thing. He'll not have *done* anything. There was no decision and there was no instruction and there was no notice and there's no form in this basin that says a repair was not done, and I've had thirty years of a book with a column in it that nobody ever had to use because you don't stand nine men in a shed for nothing.

"So there is no name. And if there is no name, then what is there? There is a load. And a date it went on. And that's it. That's the board. And I'm not having anybody's name on it, and I'm not having the thirtieth on it either."

"Because of the name?"

"Because of the name," said Petrie. "The thirtieth is the day it didn't come off. The sixth is the day it went on. And if I put the sixth on it, then that board says the true thing, which is: this load went on, on the sixth, against an order, and it is still here, and it is still for a wall.

"And the whole of this city has come down that road for a fortnight looking for somebody to shout at, and there isn't one, and if there's one on a board they'll find him, and the second they find him they'll stop looking at the load. And the load is the part I can actually do something about. The load is timber and a pump and a number on a band."

---

He said no overnight, and he gave Tarin Senn a reason for the no that was not the reason, and he said yes on the Thursday morning, and the reason he gave for the yes is the whole of the chapter.

"Right," he said, at about nine in the morning, with the light coming up over the reach. "Do it myself."

"You're doing it yourself."

"I'm painting it myself." He had the Line's plank up against the shed door and a pot of the yard's own white and he had a brush that had been on that shed for nine years. "I've been on this shed thirty years and I keep a book, and the book's got a date at the head of every entry in it, and I have never in my life seen anybody read one of those dates. Not the men, because the men don't need to, they were there. Not the water office, because they come monthly and they read the mark and not the page. Not the district, because the district sends a works clerk about four times a year and he has never once in four years looked at the wall of my shed.

"That's what a shed is. That's the whole of it. A shed is a place where nine men are stood when there is nothing to do, and it has a date at the top of every page, and nobody reads them, and that's correct, because if everybody read them it wouldn't be a shed.

"And a painted board is a date you can't lose. You can't lose a painted board. You can lose a book — I've had this one thirty years and I'll lose it one day and that'll be the end of that. You can't lose paint.

"So if a man is going to paint something on a shed, he's got to paint something true on it. And if he's going to paint something true on it, he's got to paint it himself, because if the yard paints it, it's the yard's statement, and if the district paints it, it's got the district's number on it, and I have just spent two days explaining to two men in a good coat why I don't want anybody's name on it.

"So it's me, and it's the sixth, and it's the load, and there is not going to be one single name on it, including mine, because if my name is on it then in nine years somebody will say *Petrie painted it* and in eighteen somebody will go and look for Petrie's reasons, and Petrie's reasons are that the timber is paid for and the pump is on a guarantee and there is a wall in the reach that this is all for."

---

He painted it on the Thursday and the Friday and it took him the better part of two days, which nobody expected, and the reason it took two days is that he painted the letters and then stopped, on the Thursday afternoon, at about half past three, and stood looking at it for twenty minutes, and then wiped the first line off and started again.

The first line had been the load. What he painted, in the end, in a big flat hand for a man of sixty-eight who has never had to letter anything larger than a date:

**SOUTH BANK DEPOT.**
**SHORING TIMBER AND REPLACEMENT DEWATERING PUMP, STORED AGAINST THE WATER OFFICE'S ORDER 114.**
**LOADED THE SIXTH.**
**NINE CREW ASSIGNED.**
**THE LOAD IS HERE.**

And underneath, in smaller letters, because he wanted it to be readable from a cart and he did not want it to be shouted:

**PAINTED BY THE FOREMAN.**

There is no seal on it. There is no name. There is no date on the thirtieth and there is no word on it that accuses anybody, and Tarin Senn stood in front of it for about four minutes on the Friday afternoon with a slate under her arm and did not say a single word, which is the most she has ever said to anybody.

And then she said, "That is not a load list, that is an epitaph."

"It is a load list," said Petrie.

"It has *the load is here* on it in letters you could read from the chain."

"So it has."

"That is not a list. A list is a thing you check things off. That is a sentence. That is a sentence with a verb in it."

"It's a sentence with a verb in it because a list would go out of date," said Petrie, "and that's the other thing and I haven't said it. In nine years I paint it again. And in nine years the date's the sixth again and the timber's still there or it isn't, and one of those two is true and you can stand at the chain and look at it and find out which in about four seconds, and nobody in this building has ever asked me a question about that timber that I could not answer by pointing at the shed."

---

The word for it came later, and it did not come from either of them, and it came out of a queue in a public building three days after that, and when it did come it was not the name of the board.

It cost nothing.

Which is why it worked, and it is the reason nothing else in this basin has ever worked.
