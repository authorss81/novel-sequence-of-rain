# Chapter 2 — Two Versions of One Notice

At six in the morning Kestrel Yards was a machine made of people shouting numbers at each other across wet concrete, and Tarin Senn had been part of that machine for eleven years and still found it the best argument she knew for staying.

The *Petrel* sat on slip four with her bow line doubled and her port gangway down, forty feet of grey envelope and gasbag, older than the Kestrel Line and better built than anything the Line would ever afford again. Two crew were on the mooring lift, which had been losing a tooth in its rack since the spring and which needed a new pin that would arrive by post on Thursday. A third was standing in eight inches of water that had not been there at four o'clock, shouting up the length of the hull that the starboard chock was fouled, and Tarin watched him shout it twice before she understood that he was shouting it to her and not to the mate.

"Rack it," she called. "We go in an hour."

She had her own glass in her left hand and had been holding it since half past four.

It was an ugly thing, a Kestrel workshop assembly: a copper box, a length of etched tube, a needle that swung because the box swung, and a spirit level she had to check every ten minutes because the needle was honest but the box was not. Nine years ago she had bought it off a wreck-surveyor who did not want it. It had no name. She called it nothing, and the crew called it Tarin's box, and when Ilva Sorn had proposed in the charter book that the Line's instruments be formally named for the register, Tarin had said the box had done nothing to earn a name and the Line had done nothing to earn a register.

She had it up now, thumb on the level bubble, and read.

The turn was coming. Not the way the published window said it was coming, which was a broad soft thing at midday with a margin of generosity built into it, and not the way a man's skin said it was coming, which was nothing yet, too early, her right ear still clear. The box said the pressure was already stacking in the west draw and the stack was about to break.

She got the same reading off it in March and in November, and both times it had been called wrong by somebody with a worse instrument and a louder bench. She had learned to hold the box in her hand rather than trust it to a bracket, because the bracket moved air under the tube and the tube was old enough to have opinions. It was a matter of a degree of body heat, and a person who did not believe in it would get a different answer, and Tarin was aware of exactly how convenient that fact was and had decided some years ago to be suspicious of herself on a regular schedule.

"Seven minutes," she said, to nobody in particular, and made herself put the box down.

Nessa Tarrow was at the route board with a lamp hooked on one arm and a copy sheet under her elbow, and Nessa had done the night watch at Kestrel for six years and had the particular calm of a person who has been awake while everybody else was asleep and has therefore had the whole yard to herself.

"Morning, captain."

"Two things on the board tonight and they were not the same thing," Nessa said. "Your note said the passage opens in the first third. The Office sheet came down the pneumatic at four with the window at eleven, and the window's got the seal on it, so I put the Office one on top." She lifted the corner of the sheet. "I put yours under it. Both times on the sheet, both with the hour against them, so nobody can say one of them was printed later."

"Say that again slowly, Nessa, because I want to hear whether you thought of it or whether somebody taught you."

"I thought of it." She did not smile. "I've watched two notices get pasted over each other down there this year and both times the one underneath was the one that came true."

Tarin looked at the board. Three boards in the yard, one for the Line, one for the Exchange's licensed agents, one that was nobody's in particular and which people read because it was the oldest. The Line's board had her name on the top sheet in her own hand, and under it, Nessa Tarrow's careful addition, and under that the Office seal.

Then Ivo Slate came down the apron with his case under his arm and his licence showing in his buttonhole, because Ivo Slate of the Cloud Exchange was nothing if not legible, and he was carrying the particular expression of a man who has been authorised to be helpful.

"Captain Senn. You're flying the Nacre consignment."

"I'm flying the Nacre consignment."

"I've a window for the same passage." He said it the way a man says *I've a key*. "Eleven hundred to thirteen hundred, sealed by the Weather Office, licensed and covered. Half your published rate, and I can have the manifest countersigned before you lift. Four of the carriers out of this yard have already paid against that window this week. If you take it, you're not the yard's problem at seven."

"Which four?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to them," Tarin said, "and it will matter to me when I want to fly a passage they haven't flown."

Ivo Slate's smile did not move, which was the thing about him. He was forty-odd and had begun at the Exchange as a copyist and was now a licensed agent with a seal and a room upstairs, and every word he said was within the terms of his licence, and every word was also designed to take a thing off somebody else.

He put a card on the board. Sealed. Office window, eleven hundred, half rate, four carriers paid.

"The Office is not wrong," he said. "That's the part people get wrong about my trade. I don't sell doubt. I sell a number that a bank will accept. Your box says the turn comes early. Fine. The Office says eleven, and the Office's number has been wrong by less than an hour in a hundred and forty published windows, and when it is wrong it is wrong in the direction of caution, and caution is what an underwriter is buying." He tapped the card. "You can be early and right. You cannot be early and covered. If the turn opens at six and you launch at seven on a sealed card, captain, you are not a route officer. You are a woman flying a passenger ship in a freight dispute."

"Ivo." Tarin kept her voice level, which took something. "The Bellweather window. You sold it."

"I didn't sell it, captain, I was licensed to receive it." He said it lightly. "Four carriers and a grain factor and two insurers took the Bellweather passage off the Office four days ago, under a standard secrecy clause, on the strength of a figure that has never been published, at a price that assumed it would be the only one available. And I would like to observe, without heat, that you did not know it existed on Tuesday morning, when you put your own reading on a public board in a yard with my licensees walking through it. That is not an accusation. It is a scheduling problem. For both of us."

He had said *without heat* the way other men say *with interest*.

"Then there were two of you," said Tarin, "on Friday morning, selling a number that has never been published, and neither of you thought to put it on a board. Because putting it on a board makes it arguable."

"The Exchange does not publish. That is the entire service." Slate closed the case. "You do. That is the entire difference between us, and it is why the yard is where it is and I am upstairs."

There was a great deal of truth in that. Tarin stood on the wet apron and let it be true, because it was, and because the thing about Ivo Slate was that he never once said a false thing and she had known him for four years and had never seen him lose.

Then Ilva Sorn came out of the route office with the charter in her hand and the expression she had been wearing since February, which was the expression of a competent woman holding a number that nobody with a vote wanted to hear.

"Eleven crews in this yard take work off our name," Ilva said. "Not off Tarin's name. Off ours. We publish our uncertainty because we said we would in the Charter review and because it is the only thing we have that four licensed carriers do not. Do you know what a sealed Office window is worth to a yard with insurance?" She did not wait. "It is worth every charter we lose for a season. Every one. And you are about to throw a month of overhead at a box to prove a point a jury of shipwrights has already decided."

"I am not going to prove a point."

"Then what are you going to do?"

"Take the freight."

Ilva Sorn looked at the *Petrel*, at the water standing in the scuppers, at the sky doing nothing visible, and then went back inside without another word, and the door she let go of hit the frame hard enough to shake the lamp.

"Mr Slate," Tarin said. "I decline the window. I want it noted on your card that I declined it and not that I was outbid, because it will not be outbid, there is no one else in this yard going to launch uncertified at seven, and by noon the Exchange will own this passage and your four carriers will own this yard."

"Noted," said Ivo Slate, and put his card away, and looked at his watch, and made a small mark in a small book, and it was the smallness of the gesture that told her she had just been invoiced.

---

She flew.

The first twenty minutes were the ugliest. The lift went up with the rack complaining in the same note it had used all morning, the crew found the fouled chock and dealt with it in four minutes, and then they were off the water and the yard fell away below them with its three boards and its one lamp still lit, and the lake came up dark and flat and completely without opinion, the way a lake will be for an hour before it stops being safe.

At two thousand feet Tarin took the box off her knee and read it in her hand. The stack was there. She set it in its bracket, which she did not trust, and it said the same thing a quarter of a degree wide, and she took it out of the bracket and read it again in her hand.

"Obliged," she said, to the box, in the voice she used for crew.

The turn came at ten minutes past seven, which was four hours before the Office's window, and it came the way her box had said it would: not as a gust but as a whole change of mind in the air, the western draw loading in against the low ground and then lifting, the cloud tearing into long fingers along the Nacre side, the lake surface going from nothing to white in under a minute.

The *Petrel* went up through it and Tarin put her hand on the release and let the ship find its own way for four seconds, which is the single most frightening and most correct thing a captain does in weather, and then she took the helm back and worked the passage the way she had worked it in March, and in November, and in the year before that, reading the box and the ship's behaviour and her own eleven years, and never once the sealed card, which was still in her coat pocket with the Exchange's name on it.

The passage ran from six minutes past seven to twenty past nine and then began to close, slowly, exactly the way it had closed in March. She put the ship down on the Nacre apron at nine-forty with a hold full of lens blanks and pane stock, and the ground crew took her lines without one joke about the time, because ground crews at the Nacre end do not joke about a turn night and never have.

It was, by any measure that existed, a good passage. She had read it right.

The charter agent met her on the apron with a letter, because there was always a letter, and it was short, and it was dated six forty that morning, which was the hour at which Ivo Slate had put his card on her board.

The Nacre factor had taken the Exchange window instead. Not because the Exchange passage was better. Because the Exchange passage came with a seal on it, and the factor's underwriter in Veyra had asked a question about cover that Tarin could not answer with anything except the word *yes*, and the factor had gone with the man who had a piece of paper that said so.

A month of the *Petrel*'s overhead, gone, in about eleven hundred words in a letter on a wet apron at ten in the morning.

"Captain," the agent said, and then, with real kindness, because the Nacre end is a small place and everybody there knows weather, "you were early. You were not wrong. I want that written down somewhere, because in a month there is going to be a great deal said about the Exchange and the Office and the whole business, and nobody is going to remember a boat."

"Right," said Tarin Senn. "Write it down."

And she walked back to the *Petrel*, and she did not feel proud for about a quarter of an hour, and then she stopped expecting to feel proud, and started feeling the other thing instead, which was the small cold understanding that she had just been made to pay for being right, out of her own pocket, in front of eleven crews, by a man who had never lied to her.

That was when she understood the thing she could not yet say in a sentence. The Office was not wrong. The Office was four hours cautious, and caution is a public good, and if she published that the turn came early she would be asking the yard to fly uncertified for an hour, and a yard that does that twice in a season is a yard nobody insures. So the official number was honest and unhelpful and the honest unhelpful number had a seal on it, and the seal was worth more than the truth, and somebody up on the ridge had signed it.

The yard is not stupid. She had always known that. What she had not known until six-forty that morning was that somebody had put a price on the difference.

She got back to the board at ten past eleven, when the crews came off the apron and the yard was loud again.

"Nessa," she said. "Take the Office sheet down. Put mine up on top. Then put the Office one back underneath it, with the hour against it, and yours, with the hour against that. Same sheet, all three."

"Same sheet." Nessa Tarrow uncapped her pencil. "Both times, so nobody can say which came later."

"Both times." Tarin looked at the Exchange board, where Ivo Slate's card was already gone and four carriers' marks were chalked where a window used to be. She looked at the Line's board, where her own reading now sat above a sealed number, and where the two hours, seven and eleven, sat side by side in a young woman's careful handwriting, and she thought: there is a system for not knowing, and it is three rooms deep, and it runs on a woman with a pencil at six in the morning.

"Captain," Nessa said. "There's a runner on the board. Office sheet, unsealed. Middling."

Tarin took it. It was a Bellweather bulletin, a real one, printed in the ordinary type with no window and no margin of generosity in it: unsettled, western draw. It was true of everything and specific to nothing, and it had been issued for a storm that the ridge had known about for three days and had not put a number on.

"Middling," Tarin agreed. "Board it. And Nessa — the two hours stay up all week. If the Exchange asks me to take my sheet down, you read them the hours first."

"Captain," said Nessa Tarrow, and wrote them up.

Ivo Slate was still on the apron at noon, because Ivo Slate was always still there, writing his small book, and when he saw her come out he closed it and put it away, which told her he had finished, and he took his hat off, which he had never once done for her before.

"Captain," he said. "Thank you for publishing both."

"I published the weather."

"You published it with the hours on it, publicly, in a yard with four carriers on the board." He replaced his hat. "I'll be filing. Not a complaint. An agency report. Time of observation, instrument, and — " a small courteous inclination of the head "— the source of the contradiction, which will be your board, at Kestrel Yards, with a night watchman's hand on it. Nothing improper. The Exchange is obliged to record when a licensed forecast is found to have been understated at the time of sale."

"You are going to tell them I was right."

"I am going to tell them the number was low," said Ivo Slate. "The number being low is what makes your morning expensive. Congratulations, captain. You were right, and somebody else has to pay for it, and the paperwork is how we find out who."
