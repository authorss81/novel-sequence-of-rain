# Chapter 33 — The Man Who Did Not Come

Petrie had been keeping the south-bank shed since before Marek's father was born and he was a grey man of about sixty-eight with a way of standing in a doorway that made it look like the doorway had been built for the purpose, and on the morning of the thirty-first day of the Bellweather autumn he had nine men on the flat behind him and a book on a shelf and a lamp and about forty minutes before the district's cart got up the road that was not there any more.

Neris Kade came down at eight in the morning with a satchel and a lamp of her own and Ampow, and Petrie looked at the three of them for about four seconds and said, "You want to know who stood my crew down."

"I want to know what happened," said Neris.

"Right," said Petrie. "Because they will be the same, and nobody's going to like the answer, and I have been awake with it since Friday afternoon."

---

He had the level book down off the shelf and put it on the bench and opened it, and he turned back three pages and put his finger on a line and then, four lines further up, on another line, and he made Neris read both of them herself.

*28th. Mark covered. Draw on the top.*

*29th. 16:40. Levels read against the mark by W. Farrow, water office district crew, chain and staff, per the monthly check. Flat level with the mark at 07:30 and holding. Window per the published table: 40 minutes, low water. Mark will show about half past seven.*

And underneath, in a different hand, the same pen, a second line, because Farrow had done the ordinary professional thing and written the operational note as well as the reading:

*Advise the shed the flat will take from about half past seven. Advise the flatbed's ground to be inspected first. Nothing to be done this side of the levels.*

"Friday," said Petrie. "Farrow comes monthly, he has come monthly for about nine years, and he is a decent man who does the job the way it is written down. He read it at twenty to five on Friday afternoon with the light going and he wrote it in my book, and he wrote the window in my book, and he told my boy to tell me to have the ground looked at in the morning, and he went off up the hill."

"What time did he leave?"

"Half five. Quarter past. About that." Petrie took his finger off the page. "I can tell you what he said. He can tell you what he said. He's a clerk, he'll remember writing it because he wrote it in the right column. That is the last thing anybody in this city said to me about that flat."

"And the crew were ready?"

"They were ready at half six on Saturday morning, which is when I would have had them ready. Nine men, the flatbed on the blocks, the timber banded, the pump on the pallet, and the ground inspected at first light because Farrow told me to inspect it, and I did inspect it, and I can tell you the ground's good. Nobody has to tell me the ground's good. I've been on that flat thirty years."

---

He had it the way a shed foreman has everything: in order, with times, and without any feeling in it whatsoever, which was the thing Neris had been listening to for forty minutes and had not been able to put a name to until she gave up trying.

"The mark came off at about twenty past seven," said Petrie. "I was stood at the post myself. Mud off the mark, first time since the autumn, and I said the word. You don't say the word for nothing, you say it and then you go and get the flatbed off the blocks, and the flatbed wants about ten minutes to come off the blocks and get round."

"Ten minutes."

"Ten minutes. Which is fine, because the window's forty minutes and I'd have called the start of it half past, so we had it coming and going." He put his hand out flat. "Mark off at twenty past. Flatbed's nose round about half past. We got forty feet onto the flat — that's my figure and it's measured from the post — with the timber banded and the pump still on the pallet and nobody's boots off the bed, and Nell's boy on the staff the whole way."

"And then?"

"And then the reach came back."

"In four hours?"

"In fifty minutes," said Petrie. "Which I've had thirty years for and it has never done it and I've told the water office and there's a queue. It came back at about ten past eight. Two foot on the staff in a minute. Three foot in three minutes. That's a reach filling, not a reach rising, and I know the difference, and there is a difference, and the water office has never once in nine years written it down in the right column."

---

"And then the flat was gone," said Neris Kade.

"And then the flat was gone," said Petrie, "and I got her off, and the pump's pallet went over at the end and four of my men had a hand under it and it went back on the bed and the bands held, and the tide came up round the wheels on the way in and we were on hard ground at about ten to nine."

"And nobody told you not to go."

"No."

"Nobody rang you."

"No."

"Nobody sent a notice."

"No," said Petrie, and he said it a third time, and he looked at her, and he said the thing that she carried up the hill with her and did not put down for about a week. "Nobody told me not to go, madam. That is the whole of what I have been laying awake with. I have had nine men stood in a shed for a day and a half and I cannot tell you who did it to me, because there isn't a *who*. That's a thing you can be sacked for. You can be fined. There is a man somewhere you can go and shout at. I have been looking for one since Friday afternoon and there isn't one and the water office has never sent a crew to stand anybody down in my life and I would know, because they would have to come down the road, and I would have seen the cart."

---

Neris sat down on the bench.

She had come down the hill with four things to establish and she had two of them and the second of them was the worst thing she had ever been handed by an honest man.

The levels had been wrong. That was established, and it was established by a water-office clerk's own hand in a shed's own book, and the clerk had done everything right and had written the window in and had told the shed to have the ground inspected and the ground *was* good and the crew had gone out in it.

And nobody had stopped them.

The remaining two things were: what the Office had published about the thirtieth, and whether anybody else in this basin had a document about any of it.

The first of those came down the ridge in the district post at about eleven o'clock, in the ordinary return, and Neris had it in her satchel and she read it out on the flat with a hand that was not entirely steady.

*Return of the Weather Office, thirtieth day. Bellweather, body. Sustained western draw. Peak rain 02:00 to 05:00, four point one inches on the ridge. Items: (a) Lower Sill headwall, freight apron, failure at approximately 03:30. Structure: concrete, Bellweather era, shored 6 years, condition gravel at the west cheek above the water line, reported to this office spring last. Classified maintenance, deferred. (b) South-bank road, cut at approximately 05:00, forty feet of bank above the third switchback. Classified storm damage. (c) Low ground, ninth ward: six ground floors flooded from below at approximately 03:55. No casualties reported. (d) Upper and middle rows, Hearthward, and the market, dry throughout.*

Every item in it was correct.

Item (a) said maintenance and deferred and said why, in a clerk's ordinary hand, and named the gravel at the west cheek above the water line, which Petrie had said in the sixties and which the water office had had since the spring.

Item (b) said storm damage and said forty feet of bank, and it was a different item, in a different line, with a different hour on it, and somebody in that building had understood that they were not the same thing, and Neris Kade read item (b) twice and thought about a woman in a flat-bottomed lighter at five o'clock in the morning making exactly that point to a man with a cut over his eye, and did not know whether it was the same person and would never find out.

Item (c) said six ground floors and no casualties, and the six was right.

And there was nothing in it about a shed.

There was nothing in it about a mark at the edge of a flat that had come off at twenty past seven, and nothing in it about a staff that a boy had carried forty feet onto a low flat, and nothing in it about nine men and a banded timber stack and a replacement dewatering pump on a pallet with a Union guarantee on the drum which was at that moment standing in a shed, dry, on a pallet, covered, with the bands on, having been loaded since the sixth of the month.

The return was correct. Every sentence in it was true, and it was a complete account of the damage of the thirtieth, and the man who wrote it had had a form with items on it and had filled in the items that were damage.

Item (e) would have read: *a repair which was not performed.* And there is no box on the form for that, and there is no instrument in this basin that could have filled it in, and the reason there is no box is not anybody's decision. It is the form. It is the form and the categories and the fact that the Weather Office of this city has never in the history of the building put a line in for a thing that was not damaged, and nobody in that building has ever been asked to, and no district has ever put up a return with a line about a thing that was not done.

"Ash," said Sergeant Ampow, from the path, after a while. "That is the *best* return I have ever read."

"It is," said Neris Kade.

---

They went along the flat in the daylight at about half past one, because she had decided she was going to look at it herself and not take a report, and Petrie came with her because it was his flat and because she asked.

It is about two hundred yards of the bottom of a reach between a post at the edge and a hard bank, and on the day after a peak it is a sheet of grey mud about a foot deep with a tide-marker standing in it, and it is not impressive, and there is nothing on it. There is no hole in it. There is nothing in that mud that any person could look at and say, *this is where something was lost*, because the only thing that was lost was the forty feet.

The timber was banded. Four bands, iron, done in the shed in the spring and not touched since.

The pump was on its pallet with its cover on and the guarantee on the drum, and the drum was dry, and the canvas had got a mark on it where somebody had put a tool down on it in the spring.

And outside the shed door the notice board was standing where it stands, and it had been repainted four times, and the fourth of those was in the autumn, and the date on it was the sixth of the month, and it said:

*LOWER SILL CULVERT — SHORING TIMBER AND REPLACEMENT DEWATERING PUMP. NINE CREW, LOADED. REPAIR WINDOW ON THE REACH LEVELS AS PUBLISHED BY THE WATER OFFICE.*

The paint on the sixth's date was still on the board and the paint on the board had been on it since the autumn and the wood had gone grey over the top of it and nobody had looked at the date since the day it was painted, because it is a date, and a date is for the people who already know.

"That is the whole of it," said Petrie. "That's what's standing out there. That's what they've got."

"Petrie." She was looking at the board. "Who told you the sixth?"

"Nobody told me the sixth. I put the sixth. I paint it the week it's due, and I've done it every time for nine years, and the next one is a week off, and I'll paint it Sunday if you can find me a road." He looked at it. "Aye. I suppose it's the sixth."

And Neris Kade stood on the top of a low flat in the middle of a reach with a lamp in her hand and read a painted date on a board at a shed door, and understood that the date had been the whole of the record and the record had been four bands and a canvas mark and a date, and that the most important document in the city on the thirty-first of that month was a thing a shed foreman repaints every nine years in a different colour, and that nobody was going to have a copy of it, because there is no institution that holds a copy of a shed's notice board.

---

She got the district book out in the district office at about four in the afternoon and locked the door, which she has never done in nineteen years of this or five years of the job, and she took the ruled page out of the drawer at the back of the desk where it has lived since the eighth of the month.

*NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came.*

Six lines.

The first three were about the seventh, the eighth and the ninth of the month, and the fourth was about the ward's own glass and the plate in a coat, and the fifth was about a district that cannot read its own notice, and the sixth she had written on the twelfth and had gone over four times and had a bracket round one word in it.

And she wrote the seventh, in her own hand, in the room, with nobody in it:

*7. The thirtieth's return does not say the reach levels came down, because there is no instrument in the Office that looks at a reach, and therefore nothing that was lost on the reach can be said to have been lost by anybody. The notice is correct. The district cannot be shown to have been right about the levels and cannot be shown to have been wrong. A window the city cannot see is not a window the city loses.*

She read it seven times.

Then she did the thing that had taken her four days in the previous month and the whole of the previous fortnight, and she wrote the line that says the thing is a document and a document has an author, at the top of the page under the heading, in her own hand, and the date, and she put the district's stamp on it with the stamp she has a key for, and then she put it back in the drawer and locked the drawer.

Because it was the only object in this city that joined the two ends of it. That was the whole of the discovery and it arrived in her while she was locking a drawer.

The claim was a notice. The shed was a silence. A notice and a silence are two ends of the same piece of string and there was one object in Veyra that had both of them on it, and it was a ruled page in a district office drawer, headed *what the notice did not say*, in the handwriting of a warden, with a stamp on it.

And it was not compellable. It was not a forecast. It had no place, no time, no effect and no witness, and there was no instrument in it and no bell, and if she had put it in front of a Deputy tomorrow morning he would have had it stamped in about forty seconds under a rule he had written himself, and it would have gone into a tray on top of two other trays, and the city would have been *right*.

It would have been right, and the two ends would never have touched.

---

She came out of the office at about half past four and Ampow was on the step.

"Warden. The shoring crew's asking whether they're back on the market."

"Yes," said Neris Kade. "They are. And the ward's low side has got a foot of water in it still and the first pump house is under eight feet and it will be a fortnight, so put them on the Hearthward middle run from tomorrow and get them to the throats on the two rows above the wall, and get them into the fourth block's catch pits as well while they are in the district. And tell Ferren I have said so and tell him it is not a claim and there is no more rain coming tonight and if anybody tells him otherwise they are lying."

"Right." Ampow looked at her. "And the hours?"

"The hours are the hours," said Neris Kade, "and they are in the book twice, and they will be in the book twice at the year's end, and there is no column for them in anything else in this city, and I have now written that sentence down in ink on a page in my own office, and if anybody ever asks me why I did it, I am going to be able to hand them the page, and it is going to be the only page in this basin with that on it, and it is going to be worth nothing, and I did it anyway."

She went and stood the pumps.
