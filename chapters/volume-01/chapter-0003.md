# Chapter 3 — What a Ward Cannot Read

The new plate read clean, and it took Marek most of the morning to accept that.

Six movements in the ordinary order, Gather and Weight and Break and Route and Release and Witness, one to the other, each one dull and instrumented and behaving. He had rendered it on the rig twice on day five and once more at six that morning, and every time it had been uninteresting in the specific way that instruments are uninteresting when they are working. Malle's inspection was at fourteen hundred and would be nine minutes long and would end in the word *plate*, and there was nothing on the rig to be inspected except a plate.

The shuffle was not in the tower. The shuffle was in the air, and the cracked plate had only ever been the one piece of glass in the building that had been in a position to be lied to by forty years of strain.

He had the proof. That was the difficulty. Proof was a thing that got better when other people touched it, and the only other person allowed to touch a reading was somebody with an instrument chain behind it, and Marek's chain was a cracked plate with a serial number and a drawer full of tags he had not filed.

"Right," he said, to the empty floor at ten in the morning on day six. "Chain."

The procedure for wanting a slot at the watch board was to file a request through Field Forecasts, and Field Forecasts was Corrin Malle, and Malle's day from eight to six was a solid block of watch assignments with the boundaries marked in his own hand because he liked a page he could see all of. Marek queued in the outer room at half past ten with a request in his hand and watched a district superintendent, a Kestrel route warden, and a hospital administrator go in and out of the inner room in under four minutes each.

Nobody kept him. Malle came out at ten past eleven with his coat already on his arm and said, "Not today. I'm at six watch boards tonight and I haven't got a spare pair of hands. File it."

"I've got a reading."

"Everybody's got a reading." Malle stopped. He was a heavy man and he had been a field forecaster for thirty years and he did not say things he did not mean, and what he said next was not unkind, which made it worse. "Vale. Listen. I'd read yours. I'd read it twice. And then I'd have to take a chain behind it into a room where four district heads are arguing about a school closure, and the first question out of that room would not be *is this true*, it would be *what did the Office stand behind*, and if I say *the maintenance hand's private plate* they will take it as an insult and close the school anyway." He put his coat on. "Bring me something with an instrument behind it. Not a claim. An instrument."

At half past eleven Marek caught a tram to the yards with an empty cardboard tube under his arm, because what he wanted was on a shelf at the end of the workshop lane and it was nineteen hundred across, and a thing that size has to travel in something.

Pell Ardan's shop was under a tin roof at the end of the workshop lane, hot as a forge in the middle of the day and cold as a cellar at either end of it, and the smell of it — wet sand, soda, hot glass, coal gas, and the sour vegetable reek of the etching tub — was the smell of every summer Marek had ever been nineteen in. Pell was at the glory hole with his back to the door, in a leather apron and forearm gloves, drawing a length of tube out of the flame and putting it into a wooden former in one movement that made it look accidental.

"Nineteen hundred across," Pell said, without turning round. "You want it, you have to want it badly, because it takes a man all day to want anything that size, and I am not carrying it down to a tower for a man who is going to be pleased with himself for a fortnight."

"I want to look at a pane."

Pell turned round then. He was thirty-four and had red hair going at the temples and the permanently burned forearms of a man who has never once been careful with hot glass, and he looked at Marek's face and then at the tube under his arm, and his expression closed.

"No," he said.

"It's got to be the big one."

"It's got to be nothing, because it's got to be the Ninth Ward's, and it's been sat on my shelf for two years because the Office won't move on it, and you are not going to tell me you want to look at a pane." Pell came out from behind the bench and wiped his hands on the apron and stopped about four feet away, which was the distance he kept when he was being careful. "Marek. What are you doing with a tube?"

Marek told him.

He told it flat, in the order it happened, because Pell had no patience for a story told for effect: the cracked plate, the bench rig, the six traces, the order. Gather and Break where Weight should be. Witness arriving in the last third. The three air masses inside one cycle. The ridge instruments at sixty-eight to seventy-four hours and unable to agree by six, because every instrument on the ridge measures how much and how fast and from which quarter, and not one of them has ever been able to measure *in what order*, and order was the whole of the danger.

Pell listened to all of it without moving.

"You have a reading," he said at the end, "and you cannot show it to anybody, and you have come to my shop to cut it out of a pane of glass."

"I need a plate. Not cracked. Not a month old. Annealed, thick, big enough to hold two days of a tower's feed without fogging the fine work. There is one piece of that glass in this district and it is a notice the poorest ward in the city has been trying to read for a fortnight."

"That is not what I asked you." Pell's voice did not rise. He had never once in twenty years of knowing Marek Vale raised his voice, and it was a great deal worse than shouting. "You said you were looking. You keep saying looking is free. You said it to your own bench on Monday night, at about eleven, and you have been saying it to this shop ever since, and the shop does not answer you either. Looking is free. Fine. Grant it. Then tell me what happens on the hill when they send a crew up to fit a plate they were promised two years ago, and there is no plate, and the reason there is no plate is that a man in a hurry took a corner out of it to read a private opinion about the sky."

"They're not going to send a crew."

"They were going to send a crew at ten this morning."

Pell glanced at the door. It was twenty to twelve.

"They're late," he said. "Same as they were in March, same as the last time. They will be here at half past midday and they will be told the pane is finished and it will be finished, because I have been sitting on two years of their money and I have kept it annealed and I have kept it clean and I have kept my mouth shut about it, which is the only skill I have that they pay me for." He wiped his hands again although they were dry. "And if you cut that pane, Marek, I cannot put it back. Glass does not take a corner back. You will have a working plate in your coat and a dark frame in that tower and a warden on Low Spindle's lowest row writing her notices out by hand for another two years, and *that* is the price of your reading, and you have known that since the tram."

"Yes," Marek said.

"Right." Pell went and got a folding rule out of a drawer and opened it and shut it again without measuring anything. "Then here is what it costs me, and you will do all of it, and there is no version of this conversation in which you do less. You take every piece of glass work that tower has coming for the next two years. Frame copper, seat rings, the bubbled pane's surround, the spare plates, the lot, and you will do it in your own time, out of your own hours, and you will not invoice the Office one copper farthing, and I will keep the balance on the pane and the balance is mine, not yours, and I will not split it with you and I will not lend it to you. And when a real pane is cut and paid for and hung, you will be the one who fits it, and you will not be thanked for it by anybody, including me."

"Done."

"Also," said Pell, "you will not put it in the register. I know you. You will hold it and look at it and then you will write it somewhere that is not the register, because you have decided the Office is a thing that must be shown rather than told, and if it is only ever shown, nobody is ever given the chance to be shown wrong. So I am telling you now, while we are both standing here being honest in my shop, that if you keep a private reading in a coat, then the ward that has no public glass is going to find out about it eventually, and it is going to be from me, because I will not be able to help telling them."

The tower crew came at half past midday. Two men, a handcart, a docket, and the particular air of men who have been told to expect nothing. Marek stood in the lane and did the arithmetic for them, which was the only way it was going to happen with his name on it.

He told them what he had done, in the doorway, in the rain, without softening it. He told them the corner was gone and the reason. He told them the trace had shown the six movements arriving out of order and that he had not been able to say so to anyone who could act on it, and that he had chosen a piece of their glass to do it with, and that he understood how that sounded, and that it was not his glass.

The older of the two crew listened with his hand still on the cart handle. Then he said, "You've put a hole in it."

"Yes."

"That's the sequence plate, is it. Not a chip for a frame."

"A working plate," Marek said. "Off the crown. Nine inches, ground edge, annealed. Four-four-eight-six, the one your office paid for two years ago and never came and collected. It'll hold two days of the sequence and it won't fog the fine work."

The crewman looked at the size of it a second time. "It'll be a very odd hole in a very poor ward's notice," he said. "That's all right. I've seen worse. What I want to know is whether you're going to come up the tower and tell us what to write on the boards while you've got it in your hand."

"Yes," Marek said. "That's the whole of the reason."

"Then I'll put you down for it." The man took the docket out of his coat. "Vale. Marek Vale, glasswork, owed to the Ninth Ward, against the tower's account, at your own hand, no charge to the ward. Sign there. And if the Office asks who's holding the debt, you are, and I'll tell them you are, and I'll enjoy it, and I hope it takes them longer than two years to understand why."

The cart stood in the lane for a quarter of an hour with nothing on it.

Then the lane was empty, and Marek Vale was alone in a glassblower's shop with a pane nineteen hundred across that two years of somebody's money had been sitting on, and he had to go and get the scribe.

He did it the slow way on purpose, because the fast way is how panes get ruined. He stood the sheet up on the trestle with the crown at eye height, and marked the line in chalk where the plate would come out — nine inches, ground edges, taken well down off the crown so the plate came out of the thickest part of the glass and not off the edge where it tapers. He marked it twice, on two faces, and checked the marks against each other by holding the pane up to the window, and when they agreed he cut the first line with the diamond and picked up the scribe for the second.

The scribe is a steel wheel on a handle and it is the most frightening tool a glassblower owns. You set it on the line, you turn it, and it makes a sound like a wasp in a jar, and if your hand is not completely steady it walks off the line and puts a crescent through the work. He turned it. The sound went on for eleven seconds and then stopped, because the score was through.

He turned the sheet over, scored the second face, and then he put the sheet flat on the bench, put a blanket over it, and broke it.

It broke along the line. Both lines. The pane came apart into a frame that was no longer any use to anybody and a nine-inch plate with a ground edge and a crown that was still in it, and the break was clean enough that he did not have to grind the plate at all, which was the only good luck in the afternoon.

He picked the plate up. It was heavier than it looked. It was cold. And there was a moment, standing in the middle of a working shop with a glaze bucket in one hand, when the whole of the plan came apart in his head, because of what he was holding: it was the ninth ward's *public notice*, and a notice is the one piece of a city's weather that the city is allowed to see, and he was about to take nine inches of it off and put it in his coat.

He thought about Feth's stall at the foot of the Weatherglass steps, at a quarter to midnight on Monday, saying *there's always a second version somewhere and nobody's paid to say it out loud.* He thought about a rider on a bicycle who could not ride a bicycle.

Then he put the plate in the tube, and put the tube in his coat, and swept the frame up, and swept the glass up, and put the tools away, and wrote the ninth ward's temporary in the shop's own book against the two years he owed, and signed it *M. Vale, glasswork, unpaid*, which is not a signature anybody had ever asked for and which was the only honest line available.

Marek walked back up the ridge with the tube under his arm and a piece of paper in his pocket with his own handwriting on it committing him to two years of unpaid glasswork at a tower in the poorest ward in the city, and the specific unresentful shame of the thing was so complete and so plain that he could look straight at it. He had spent a poor ward's public warning on a private certainty. He had done it in daylight, to a crew who had been promised that glass for two years, and he had done it because he was in a hurry and because he was the only person in Veyra who could read what was coming, and both of those were true, and the truth did not make it smaller. It only made it accurate.

And in about the middle of the walk, on the step where the ridge turns and the whole basin comes out under you in the grey, something else arrived that he had not expected and had not earned. Corrin Malle had asked him what would prove him right. Nobody had asked him that. In eleven years the answer to a reading of his had always been *is it right*, which is a question about the past, and here for the first time was a question about the future that assumed the reading was going to be true and asked him to put himself on the line about it, and there was a small cold satisfaction in being asked that which was much worse than pride, because it was the first time in his career that anybody had treated his name as something that could be spent.

Malle caught him on the stairs at ten past two, on the way up to his own inspection, with the rosters under his arm and four minutes in hand.

"Vale."

"Deputy."

Malle looked at the tube. "You've still got it. I can see you've still got the tube." He shifted the rosters. "I can't give you a board tonight. I want to say that in a way you'll hear, because I'm not being kind, I'm being accurate: I have got five hundred and forty people to put under a weather in about a day and a half, and one of them is a collision I've never seen the inside of, and the only person who's seen the inside of it is a maintenance hand with a plate in a cardboard tube." He said it without any relish at all. "So give me the thing you can prove. If you're right about the order, what will I see that would prove it?"

And Marek, who had been waiting three days for a question worth answering, opened his mouth and found that he had prepared for a different one.

"Release before Witness," he said. "In the second turn. If the sequence at the Ninth Ward reaches Release and then takes Witness after it, the six movements are running out of order, and that would account for all six hours of disagreement up there, because every instrument on the ridge is timing the movements against one another, and if they arrive shuffled then the timings mean nothing." He stopped. "It'll be visible on the ward's own glass."

"Which is dark," said Malle.

"Which is a temporary pane with a bubble in the middle of it, so it isn't dark exactly, it's just that nobody can read it," Marek said, and heard himself, and did not stop, because stopping would have been worse and because the sentence was true and because he had asked to come up the tower and it was already in the docket. "Which is why I need to be on the tower when it happens."

Malle looked at him for slightly too long. "That's a proof," he said. "That's not an account. You know the difference between those two things, don't you, because most of the people who come to me with readings don't."

"Yes," Marek said.

"Get me the account when the plate has four hours on it. Your ground in twenty minutes." He went up the stairs, and was at the top before he added, over his shoulder, without turning round, "And Vale — if that plate came out of a ward's notice, that is the first sentence out of your mouth in whatever room I put you in, and if it isn't, you don't get put in the room."

The inspection took nine minutes and passed, which was the right outcome and a small one. Marek stood at the end of the observation floor with his hands behind his back while Malle looked at the new sequence plate in the Ninth Ward feed, tapped the copper seat with one knuckle, and said, "Your ground," and Marek said, "It came up slow. The seat was cold," and Malle said, "It's a plate," which was the whole of the praise available and which Marek had been getting for eleven years and had never once got used to.

By six o'clock the corridors had emptied and Marek went down to the ground floor with the tube, and the ward ledger bench was shut and racked for the night, its nine ward heads dark along the wall, and the copy sheets were in their slots, and a young woman in a copy clerk's grey coat was checking the entry bell's striker against her thumb because bell maintenance was Thursday and she did not trust the Thursday man.

Marek stood at the end of the bench and did not say anything, because it was six o'clock on day six and the front was sixty hours out and he had a tube under his arm and a two-year debt in his pocket and no way at all of making any of it into a thing the city would act on.

She looked up. She had a face built for being doubted and had decided in advance not to be.

"Glass," she said, nodding at the tube. "You're the maintenance bench."

"Yes."

"Then you'll want the observation rack, and it's Thursday." She went back to her bell. Then she stopped, and did not turn round. "Unless you're here about the watch board. In which case you should have come at nine, and it's shut now, and tomorrow it's me again, so you could come at nine, if you wanted."

"I'll come at nine," Marek said.

"Then you'll want to bring something," said Tovan Iles, "and not a tube."
