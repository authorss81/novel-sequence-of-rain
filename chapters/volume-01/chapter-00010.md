# Chapter 10 — A Steady Knife

The roster went up on the wall of the field office at four o'clock, which was Office practice, and which meant that everybody who worked in the building came down to read it in order over about eleven minutes and then went away and lived with it.

Marek read it standing at the back, and then read it again from the front, and then stood there a while longer than a man standing at a roster needs to.

The Bellweather watch. Low ground, ninth ward. Second belt through to the fourth watch, third day. Four crews, two pump houses, one warden, one reader.

*Reader.* It had a name against it. His own.

Underneath the roster, pinned to the same board, was the joint request from the yards and the low wards, and the joint request was the document that the whole of the next four hours was going to be about, and it was two lines long, and both lines were correct.

*The western draw of the Bellweather Front may be used either to hold the Kestrel corridor open for the return lane or to ease the exposed floodplain of the lower basin. These are the same air. They cannot both be had.*

Beneath it, in a smaller hand, an Office note: *The Exchange window of the fifth day is admissible in the corridor decision, four licensed carriers having paid against it.*

Marek read that note four times.

Not inadmissible. Not *the Office's own figure is preferred*. Admissible. It sat in the room with a legal standing, because four carriers had paid money against it, and four carriers' money was a fact in the world, and the Office was going to be asked to prefer a reading of a collision that only one man in Veyra could see the inside of over a number that four businesses had already committed to, and it would be a hard thing to do, and doing it would cost somebody in the yard, and the somebody in the yard would not be the carriers.

He got a sheet off the notice spike and worked the arithmetic on the back of it, standing against a wall, because he could not stop.

The western draw arrives at the basin off the lake. It has to arrive, because the front has it in it. Once it is here, it either loads against the low ground of the lower basin and eases the yard side for a few hours, or it runs hard along the western side and keeps the Kestrel corridor open for the return lane, and the water that goes up the corridor is the water that does not come down the floodplain. Same air. Same hours. And the yards need a corridor, and the low wards need rain on the floodplain in the next six hours, and both of those are the same six hours.

And the Office had asked for one claim to cover both, which was the same as asking for a claim that is true in two directions at once, which is not a difficult forecast. It is an impossible instrument. And the impossibility was in the request, in public, in a district's hand, and the Office's answer to an impossibility was going to have to be a man.

"Marek."

The glass tender's voice came from behind him. "They're saying an hour."

He looked at the clock over the field office door. It said five minutes to five, and the collision figure had been coming down the ridge all afternoon, sliding, the way a thing slides when a western draw decouples from the body of the weather and goes on ahead of it on its own schedule.

"Nine at ten o'clock this morning," he said. "Six at noon. One now. It has gone an hour and a half quiet."

"They're saying an hour."

"Then it's an hour." He folded the sheet into his coat. "Nobody's told the low ground that yet."

"Nobody's told Kestrel either. The board's got the Office window on it and the return lane's still drawn for the small hours."

Marek went down the stairs two at a time, and out into the yard, and across the wet cobbles to the route boards, because he had been going to the boards four or five times a day for eleven years and it was the only habit in his working life that had never once let him down.

There were three boards. The Line's, the Exchange's licensed agents', and the oldest one that was nobody's.

The Office window was on the Line's board, in a printed hand, with the seal on it, and the window said the corridor would be passable from the first watch to the third.

It was the only sheet on the board.

There had been three on it on Tuesday morning: a printed Office window, and underneath it a night watchman's two versions with both hours against them, one at seven and one at eleven, and the whole argument of Kestrel Yards written in a young woman's careful hand. Nessa Tarrow had put the hours up and said that nobody could say afterwards which one had been printed later.

Marek stood in front of the board in the rain for a quarter of a minute.

There was a new sheet, which said, in the same careful hand, *Office window only, per instruction, 17:00.* And under the note, in a different pencil, the two hours had been rubbed out. Not torn off. Rubbed out, carefully, with the flat of a thumb over a piece of paper, so that the paper had not been damaged and could be reused, which meant that somebody had thought about the paper.

Whoever it was had not thought about the woman.

He stood there and understood the whole of the machine, and it took him about four seconds, and it was the four clearest seconds of thinking he had ever done in his life.

Somebody at the Office had looked at a yard board on which a captain's disagreement with the Weather Office was set out in public, with hours on it, and had understood at once and correctly that a board with two numbers on it is a market risk. Four carriers had paid against the Office figure. If the other figure was on the board, then the carriers had bought on a number that a night watchwoman had contradicted, and the Exchange's reallocation risk became somebody's problem, and somebody at the Office would be asked about it by somebody with a legal department. There was no corruption in that. There was no malice in it at all. There was a *policy*, and the policy was that a public notice may be contradicted, but not in public.

And the instruction would have gone out of a records office as a routine thing, in a sentence, without heat, by a man who believed he was protecting the Office from a claim it could not support.

Marek took out his own notebook and wrote, on a page he tore out and folded into four: *Board, Kestrel Line, 17:00. One sheet. Two hours rubbed out. No note, no reason.* And he did not put the woman's name in it, which was a piece of restraint he would be glad of later.

---

The counter at Records and Public Notice had a queue and the queue was made of people who had been told to bring something.

At the end of it a broad-shouldered clerk of about sixty with a face like a closed ledger took a sheet from Marek, read it, and read it again with the particular attention of a man who is checking for a category.

"This is the counter-read," he said. "Orin's. Above the baths."

"Yes."

"It's stamped."

Marek took it back. The sheet was the third printing of a broadside that had come out of the Ninth Baths' drying room, and somebody had set it up in a public baths' back room, which is a sentence he would remember for a long time. It carried a number and an hour and a reservation of the corridor turn, and the number was this: the return lane is short by an hour and a half; the yards must be under sail by four o'clock; the draw opens ahead of the published window, and a ship that waits for the published window misses the lane.

Sable Orin had written *verified* at the top of it in a hand like a signature on a warrant.

Marek read it, and then walked forty feet up the corridor to a bench nobody was using, and took the tube out of his coat, and opened it, and put his plate on the copper frame with the lamp behind it, and read the basin sequence for the western corridor window himself, with his own hands, in about four minutes.

It agreed with her.

Not approximately. The same opening, the same lead, and the same quarter-hour, and the plate had it in the second turn where the movements came out of order, which meant that the only instrument in Veyra capable of contradicting the Exchange's window was an instrument with no chain behind it, and the only other instrument in Veyra that said anything at all about the corridor said it in a public baths and had been filed.

The stamp on the sheet was small and violet and entirely correct. He turned it round. *Reclassified 14:10, 9th day: unattached observation. Not lodged. Not compellable. Not producible. An observation not acted upon cannot be compelled to mean anything at a later date. Rule 6, Charter of Public Notice.* And underneath, in a different ink, in a small, level, unhurried hand that he had never seen before and would know for the rest of his life:

*The Exchange is not a party to any district's preparations and is not required to price them. If a number has not been lodged, no party is obliged to act on it, and no party may afterwards be blamed for not having done so.*

Marek read the last sentence four times.

It was the best sentence anybody in the Weather Office had written in his life. It was a perfect statement of the law. It was true, it was humane, it removed the Office from the market and the market from the district and the district from any obligation to prepare, and it did all of that without a wasted word, and it had been used that afternoon to make sure that the only piece of information in the city which said *the yards must be under sail by four o'clock* could never again be produced by anybody who was not already the Exchange.

Lior Venn had not suppressed a reading. He had done something much more elegant than that. He had answered it.

It took Marek eleven minutes to get to the bottom of what he was feeling, and when he got there it was not anger and it was not the familiar despair of being told that a thing he had seen was not a thing.

It was relief.

Because if Sable's number was not compellable, then neither was his. And if his plate was not compellable, then neither was the gap in the Release of the second turn, or the dry stone throat, or the ninety minutes on the eighth day with four times written in a neat hand on a sheet of bench paper that was by now in a flat drawer under a bench in a locked room.

He stood in a corridor on the ridge with an unreadable plate in a tube and a stamped broadside in his hand, and the ward he had spent a week trying to warn was going to be warned by nothing, and he was going to stand on its floor tonight, and the machinery that had just done this was the same machinery that had, on the seventh day, let a twenty-seven-year-old copy clerk file his plate as an unattached observation with his name on the tab, in her own hand, when she had no more idea what it meant than the rack it went into.

The only difference was that Tovan had put his name on the tab. Lior Venn had not had to put anybody's name on anything.

He found the clerk again.

"Who signed the reclassification?"

"Records and Public Notice. Deputy Venn. It's his hand, and it's his rule." The clerk looked at him with the mild, immovable courtesy of a man who had processed ten thousand pieces of paper. "It's the standard line, sir. Six times this month, under pressure of the sort you're describing. He doesn't argue about them."

"Does he ever lift one?"

"Not that I have seen."

Marek gave the broadside back and went back down the corridor, and stopped at the notice case by the field office door, and read the one line of the current bulletin, which was the ordinary Bellweather sheet with the seal on it and the window on it and nothing in it at all about a lane being short or a ship having to be under sail at four in the morning.

Then he went upstairs to the chart room, because there were eleven frames on the north wall with wet getting into them and he had been told to do the gum work on the eastern ones and there was one more on the end that he had been putting off since the sixth day, and doing a thing with his hands was the only available method.

---

The frames were shallow, wooden, glazed, and older than the tariff. The charts inside were paper, which was a piece of Office foolishness from a generation ago, kept in glass because the chart room's north wall sweats in a cold season and a wet chart is a lost chart. Nine of them were the tower charts, one for each ward, and they were good work: a black line for the watershed, a red line for the tower, a small table of the last four years of local corrections, and along the upper margin, in a fine technical hand, the provenance of the chart itself.

The Ninth Ward chart was the last frame on the end, because the Ninth Ward was always the last one to be brought up to date and its margins were the dirtiest.

Marek had the block out and the gum warm and a cloth in his hand, and he had got the old glass out of the rebate, and he was lifting the chart's paper edge to slide a sheet of waxed paper behind it against the damp, when his thumb found something that was not a chip of old gum.

The upper margin of the chart was trimmed.

Not the chart. The *margin*. The strip of the sheet above the printed frame line, an inch and a half of it, which carried the provenance, and which had been taken off with a knife — not torn, not worn, not eaten by damp, but taken off, cleanly, along a line about a third of an inch from the top of the printed border, and the cut was the work of somebody who had a steady hand and a good blade and a reason.

Marek sat down on the stool.

The cut edge was brown. It was the brown of paper that has been cut and then left alone in a dry room for a very long time, and around it, on the chart itself, the paper was still clean and still pale, because that paper had spent the whole of its life protected under glass, and what had browned was the newly exposed edge.

The trim was old. The paper around it was not.

He put the lamp on the bench and turned the chart so that the light came across it, and what was left of the provenance line came up out of the dust one word at a time.

*CHART PROVENANCE — NINTH WARD TOWER. Sequence reference N9 — / lodged by — A. Vale / SEQ/N9/—*

Between the lodger's name and the sequence reference there was a filed mark: a small violet square with a bell struck across it, and a watch number beside it. That was the mark that went on a claim somebody had rung a bell for, and it was in his mother's hand, and it was eleven inches from the edge of a sheet of paper in a frame.

And then the paper ended.

The signature was his mother's.

He knew it before he had finished looking at it and he knew it in the way a man knows handwriting he has not seen for twelve years, which is not a recognition of shapes but of a speed. Anja Vale wrote the way water went over a weir: it got on with it. No serif on the capitals, no pause at the end of a stroke, the *V* of Vale with a tail that ran on under the next letter. He had last seen it on a grocery list in Low Spindle in the winter she did not come back, and he had been thirty, and he had put the list in a drawer and never looked at it again and had never once, in twelve years, expected to see that hand in a building he worked in.

His mother had signed a witnessed entry against the ninth ward tower.

Not an observation. A filing mark. He had looked at one on Tovan Iles's sheet two days ago and it had a bell struck across it and meant that somebody had struck a bell for that ward, and somebody had been sent to act on it, and it had been a real thing that had gone out into the city and had a name at the bottom of it.

And the reason, and the way anybody could find it, had been taken off the top of the chart with a knife in a room where the weather of this city was kept in order.

Marek sat on a stool in the chart room of the Weather Office at twenty past five on the ninth day of a Bellweather Front, with a warm block of gum in one hand and his mother's name in front of him, and there was nothing in his chest at first. Then there was everything in his chest at once.

Because there it was, in a drawer in a corridor, three rooms deep and running on somebody else's handwriting: a piece of paper on which a decision had been made, and the way to the proof of it, cut off by a hand that did not shake.

And here was the part that made him put his hand flat on the bench to make sure the room was solid.

He had spent a week learning that this was how it worked. He had learned it from a twenty-seven-year-old clerk with a red stamp and a bell on a wire, and from a Deputy who would take a proof and not an account, and from a brokering agent with a courteous hat, and from a copy clerk who had kept a number that nobody had wanted. He had learned it well enough to sit in a corridor at a quarter to three and feel *relieved* because the machinery that had buried Sable Orin's number had also, as it happened, buried his.

And now, in a chart room, on a stool, twelve years too late, somebody else had looked at a piece of paper and decided the same thing about it, and had not merely filed it. Had cut it out.

He was not a man who had been proved right about the institution. He was a man who had spent the last seven days discovering that a filing system run by careful people with clean hands could take a true thing and make it as though it had never happened, and that he had been perfectly content to use the same system, on the same kind of page, with the same good reasons, to make a thing of his own disappear.

His hand went into his left cuff and got the pencil out from behind his wrist, and he wrote on the lining, small and neat, in the third block of space he had left:

*17:20 — N9 chart margin, N9 prov. — N9/ — filed, witnessed (violet sq.). A. Vale. Margin cut: old cut, paper round it clean.*

Three readings and a filing mark. The first was a collision he could see the order of. The second was a gap in a Release that he had spent a day and a half failing to hand to anybody. The third was this: a violet square with a bell struck across it, in his mother's hand, on a chart margin, with the citation cut out from beside it. That was all three of them. The ship under sail by four o'clock was in a torn notebook page in the inside pocket and stayed there, because a torn page is a thing a man can lose and a coat sleeve is a thing a man can be searched.

He put the chart back in the frame. He set the new glass in, and did the rebate, and did the waxed paper behind the sheet, and the better part of an hour of that job went past like weather, and the room filled with the smell of warm gum and hot glass, which was the smell of the only work he had ever been completely sure of.

At half past five he put his coat on, and the tube in it, and the plate, and the broom, and he went down the stairs and out across the yard in the rain, and past the route boards where the Office window was the only sheet on any of them, and down the hill to the low ground of the ninth ward, where four hundred and forty people were going about their evening with one sheet of paper of their own and a pump house and a warden, and where, at about six o'clock, a weather front would come over the Nacre Range carrying three different airs and reach the basin out of order, and where Marek Vale was going to be the only person standing on the floor who could say which order, and was going to have to say it out loud in plain words to a crew of strangers while a warden with a list of four lines listened and did not interrupt.

He was half an hour out.

He had a claim he had not written, a collision nobody else could see the inside of, three things in pencil on the inside of a coat sleeve, and a piece of paper upstairs with a hole cut out of it that he had not told anybody about and could not, for the life of him, work out how to begin.
