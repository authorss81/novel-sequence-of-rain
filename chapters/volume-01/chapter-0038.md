# Chapter 38 — The Yard Bears Its Own Errors

Ilva Sorn had been putting the file together since the Monday before last and she had done it the way she signs a launch, which is to say that it was in order before it was read, and that the order was the only opinion she had ever expressed about it.

She laid it on the table at about eleven on the Friday of the thirty-sixth day and there were five things in it, and she announced them the way you announce a launch.

"One. The yard's column of differences, as published on the board by the route office door from the seventeenth of the month to the thirty-fifth, both figures, both hours, both dates, and the third column headed *difference*, nineteen days of it, of which eight days the third column is not empty and eleven days it is. That is the whole of it. That is the file.

"Two. The sheet of the fifth, which is the sheet the whole of the later work rests on, and which is not in the column, and which I have included because a column that starts on the seventeenth is a column that somebody might reasonably say began after it knew what it was doing."

The room made a small sound.

"Three. The four conditions, as agreed on the seventeenth at the boards, in the yard, in front of a carrier's man, on a slate in chalk. They are in the file as a copy of a slate. I am not going to read them out. They are four lines about this yard, they were written by two people who were in the yard, and there is nobody in this room who was in the yard, and if I read them out somebody will write them down in their own words and in about six months there will be two versions of them and this is precisely the thing they exist to stop."

"Assessor," said Venn, from the middle of the table, "I am obliged to say that is a records objection and that I am going to overrule it."

"Overrule it, then."

"I am overruling it, and I am overruling it because the conditions are already of record in this building in the technical cell's file, entered on the seventeenth by an assessor, and a copy of a slate that is the same as the file is a courtesy and not a source." He put the folder down. "Route officer, I am sorry. Your conditions will not be read out in this room, and they have been read out in a room, and that is not a defeat for you and I would be obliged if you would let me finish."

"Finish."

"I have never lifted a yard board in eleven years," said Lior Venn, "and I was asked about yard boards in a corridor on the third of this month by a copy clerk, and I answered her then and I would answer her the same way now, and none of that has any bearing on this and I am not going to let it have any bearing on this."

---

"Four," said Ilva Sorn, and put her hand on a single sheet, "is my own."

She read it out. She read it in the words she had written it in, in a small flat hand that was the hand of a route officer signing a launch, and she got all the way to the end of it without being interrupted, and there was a moment in the middle of the third line when the whole room leaned, and it did not happen.

*Column of differences, published. Both hours, both dates. Agree, subject to the yard bearing its own errors in public.*

"That has been the top of that column since the seventeenth," said Ilva Sorn, "and the room likes it, and I would like the room to understand that it is not a decoration and it is not a flourish, and that the Line does not publish differences, it publishes errors, and that there is a difference between the two, and the difference between the two is this.

"Errors are corrected. Differences are only ever published, and cannot be corrected, and are not meant to be. If the yard's figure and the Office's figure do not agree, the yard does not get to say which of them is right. The yard says what the yard's box said and when the yard's box said it, and the Office says what the Office's instruments said and when, and the column says how far apart they were. And when the yard is wrong, the column says the yard is wrong, at the size of the wrongness, with the date on it.

"That is the instrument. That is all four lines and the proviso together, and I have watched it work for nineteen days, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me what the equivalent is in the Office's publications, and I will wait."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"There is no equivalent," said Sabin Orle.

"No," said Ilva Sorn. "No, there isn't."

"Five," she said, "and then I have finished and I have been in this room for fifty minutes and I would like it noted that fifty minutes is three times what the Line's conditions took to agree, and that is because you asked me a question you are entitled to ask."

She put a fifth sheet on the table and it was a piece of board slate, or rather it was a sheet of thick paper on which somebody had copied out what was on a slate, in a careful hand, with a captain's chalk line reproduced and the hour on it.

*Line at the mark 11:11 p.m. Accepted. Lane held to 04:00. Difference vs Office: none. — T. Senn.*

And underneath that, in a different and harder hand, in the same reproduction, four lines and a great deal more:

"That is the whole of the yard's file," said Ilva Sorn. "Two figures a day for nineteen days, one sheet from the fifth, four lines from a slate, a proviso, and a wrist. That is everything the Kestrel Line has. Eleven crews take work off our name and every one of them is a family, and I signed the release myself and I would like it read that I signed it myself and not the captain and not the yard's owners, because a route officer is the person in this yard who is actually responsible for it, and there is nobody in this room who knows what that means and I would like somebody to write it down."

---

The assessor thanked her in the flat voice, and then said:

"Route officer, I want to say two things and the first is generous and the second is not, and I am going to give them in that order, and I would be obliged if nobody rushed me on the second.

"The generous one. This is the only document that has gone into this review that demonstrates that a method works."

Nobody spoke.

"Eight days with a difference on them, out of nineteen, and on eight of those days the Office's own bulletin had made the corridor a different length from the one the yard flew, and the yard was right, and the yard said so at the time, on a board, in public, and was wrong on one day out of eight and published the one.

"The Office was short by an hour and a half on the fifth, and the yard said so on the fifth, and the yard was right on the fifth, and the yard is the only instrument in this basin that can time a corridor without a chain behind it, and it is a copper box on a bracket that lies, held in a woman's hand because of the bracket. And that is the whole of the review's affirmative finding and it is yours and I want it in the minutes in those words and I am not going to soften it later."

"Thank you," said Ilva Sorn, and did not look pleased, and looked like a woman who has been told a thing she already knew by a person she had expected to say it.

"The second one." Sabin Orle put both hands flat on the table. "It demonstrates that a method of *comparison* works. It says nothing whatever about the effect of a claim on a prepared district, which is what this review is for, and there is not one line in five sheets from the Kestrel Line that bears on that, and I am going to say that in a generous way rather than a clever one because the route officer has been in this room for fifty minutes and has earned a sentence that can be repeated outside it.

"It is a fact about a comparison of two readings. That is all it is. It is the best comparison ever conducted in this basin and it does not touch the question."

"Understood," said Ilva Sorn.

"I have been asked twice this morning by two different people whether I would stop her at the proviso," said Sabin Orle, "and I would like the minutes to record that I was not going to stop her, and that I am not, and that I said so in advance, and that if anybody in this room thinks the proviso is the wrong instrument they may say so now, in the room, and not afterwards in a corridor."

Lior Venn said, "It is the right instrument and it is not the Office's and there is no prospect of it becoming the Office's, and the reason there is no prospect is that a publication which shows its own errors is of no use to anybody with a commercial interest in the figure, and this Office has four licensed carriers and two insurers holding paper priced on the fifth of the month. I have said all I am going to say about that."

---

Then the assessor asked the only question in the morning that was not about the yard, and it was asked to the whole table, and it went into the minutes.

"Route officer. Your own release. Your own proviso, and the sentence underneath the third line. Is there anything in that file which is *not* published, and if there is, what is it, and why is it not published?"

Ilva Sorn looked at her for about three seconds.

"Yes," she said. "There is."

"Name it."

"No," said Ilva Sorn.

The room went very quiet, and the reason it went very quiet is that the answer was no and everybody present understood what the answer was, which is a boom cable and a day's work for eleven people and about three hundred pounds of the Line's own account, entered by this woman under *errors of the yard, borne in public*, on the column, with the hour on it, on the thirtieth.

"There is a thing in that file," she said, "that is on the column, and it is on the column because *errors of the yard, borne in public* is a heading I invented on the nineteenth of the month and nobody made me, and I put it there because a yard that only publishes the errors somebody else can prove is not publishing its own errors.

"Everything else in that file is public. That is not a thing you say in a room and get thanked for. That is a thing you say in a room and get **a citation**, and a citation is not a compensation and it is not an acknowledgement and it does not put a name in anybody's book, and the Line is not a district and has no assembly and no seat and no standing requirement, and there is nobody in this room or in this basin whose job it is to notice what happened to us on the thirtieth.

"So the file is the only place it is written down, and the file is going into a review, and the review is going to report in the spring, and I do not know what the spring report will say about it and neither does the assessor, and if the report says nothing at all then the wrist and the cable are going to exist in this city for ever in a drawer in a technical cell, in a fair copy, in a small flat hand."

She put her hand on the fifth sheet.

"Right," she said, "and before the Chair takes the file, there is a release, and I have written it myself, and I want it read out, because I have spent a week being pleasant to about two thousand people about it and I would like one sentence of it in a minute in a room.

"*The yard's errors are published as the yard's errors, and are not evidence of any other party's error.*"

Nobody had drafted that. It had been in the yard's file since the Monday before last in her own hand, and it had not been argued for by anybody, because there had been nobody to argue for it in front of until a Council assessor asked her a question of fact in a chamber, and when Sabin Orle looked at the sheet she said, "That is in my hand and not in the Office's."

"Yes."

"Then it goes in the file exactly as written, and the Office does not get to improve it, and I would like it minuted that a route officer of the Kestrel Line drafted the sentence herself and a Council assessor let it stand, because in about four years somebody is going to say that the yard was careful about its own errors and nobody was careful about anybody else's, and this is the paper that says otherwise."

"It stands," said Sabin Orle, and Mrs Ireson wrote it down.

"Two other things and then the file goes in," said Ilva Sorn. "The board copy is in Nessa Tarrow's hand. Nessa Tarrow is the night watch at Kestrel Yards and has kept those boards for six years, and the copy of the column that is going into this file is in her hand, and she is not in this room, and I would like her name on the record as the person who wrote the figures, because everybody in this room is going to go home and say that the yard published its errors, and the figures were written by a woman at half past eleven at night doing it for nothing because nobody had told her not to.

"And the conditions are the slate's. The figures are Nessa's. The proviso is mine. The wrist is the captain's, and the captain is not in this room either, and the captain read it out in a yard at half past three in the morning with the word *BIND* on the board above it."

"That is the whole of my interest and I have declared it, and I said I would, at the first item, and I have said it at the fifth, and I am sorry."

Nobody said it was a conflict of interest. That is worth recording, because a room that has been reviewing the method for two days, in which eight citations have been taken, would have been well within its rights to say it was a conflict, and did not, and the reason it did not is that the thing Ilva Sorn had just declared was a conflict of interest *in favour of the thing the review was for*, and the review's terms of reference required it to establish whether the effect of a claim on a prepared district can be demonstrated from the record, and the Kestrel Line is not a prepared district and the wrist is not a preparation and neither of those facts is a defence of the method or an attack on it.

"Assessor," said Anwen Sarr, from four seats down, and she had not spoken for an hour and a half, "may I ask the route officer a question that is not about the file?"

"You may."

"What is in the file that is not published?"

"That is the question I was asked, ma'am, and I answered it."

"You did not," said Anwen Sarr. "I asked you what is in the file that is not published. You told me there is a thing in the file that is on the column. Those are not the same question and you are too good at this trade for me to believe it was an accident."

Ilva Sorn stood up.

"Twenty-four months," she said.

"Clerk," said Sabin Orle, "will you enter twenty-four months, and that the route officer has answered the assembly's question, and that the answer is **no**, and that I am ruling that the answer stands and that no further question on this point will be taken, and that the Chair's reason for ruling is that a witness who has begun to negotiate with a question is no longer a witness."

Anwen Sarr looked at the assessor for a moment and then inclined her head about an inch, which from Anwen Sarr is a considerable document.

Then Ilva Sorn sat down and squared the launch book that was not in front of her, and said, "One more thing, and then I have finished, and I would like it minuted in the words I am about to use and I would like somebody at the end of the table to stop me if I get any of it wrong."

Nobody stopped her.

"The yard paid for being right twice in a month," said Ilva Sorn. "On the fifth, in a charter, in a month's overhead on one ship, in cash, and nobody put that in a book. On the thirtieth, in an insurance exposure on one passage, which is a number that will not be known until the spring, and in three hundred pounds on our own account, which is a number I know today.

"The first of those I could invoice. I have the charter. The second of those I cannot invoice to anybody, because the thing it is about is a wrist, and a wrist does not have a schedule, and the Line carries the yard's cover on its own book in public because a column that only reports good news is not a column of differences.

"So the yard has been paid twice, in two currencies, and I can show you the first and I can show you the second to four figures and both of them are correct and **neither of them is recoverable from anybody**, and I have wanted to say that in a room for three weeks and this is the room."

She stopped.

"Right," said Cassian Rook, from the end of the table, and he had not spoken for an hour and ten minutes and he had spent the whole of it not interrupting a route officer, and he said now, in the flattest voice Marek Vale had ever heard a man of fifty-two use: "That is in the minutes, and it is the first statement made in this review about a cost that anybody in it has actually paid, and I am going to say the unkind thing that nobody else in this room is going to say, and I am going to say it as the Chair, and I am going to say it because if I do not say it then in six months somebody will say that this review has found a cost, and that will be untrue, and I will have permitted it by sitting here."

He put both hands on the table.

"Nothing in that file is a cost of a claim. A wrist at a mooring on a night when the wind was steady is an accident, and a boom cable is a cable. The corridor held and the yard published its hours and the yard was right about its hours and got a week of a claim's worth of good work out of it, and the Line put four conditions in chalk and got a market position out of them, and *that* is what a claim is *for*, and the yard had it.

"The route officer is not complaining. She has said so in the room. She is asking to be paid for the good week as well, and there is no instrument for that either, and I am not going to let this review discover one by accident, and I would like the minutes to say that the Chair is aware of the temptation."

Then he said, to Sabin Orle, in the ordinary voice: "Item six, assessor, whenever you are ready, and I have an appointment at two which I intend to keep, because it is the only weather in this building that is doing anything."

---

The corridor afterwards was the ordinary end of an ordinary morning in a building, and Maro Hesk was in it, and he had been in it for about twenty minutes, which he explained was how long it had taken him to establish that the yard's file was in and not out.

"Captain Senn is not here," he said, to Marek, without any preamble. "I am not asking for her. I am a man who has been standing in a corridor for twenty minutes and I have concluded that you are the only person in this building that the yard's people will talk to, and I am going to take about ninety seconds of your morning and I am going to be quick about it."

"Go on."

"Two of the four are not coming back." Hesk had his hands behind his back. "I will not name them, you would not know them, and it does not matter. I will tell you why and then I will go and do something else.

"An underwriter prices a yard. I have been doing it for nineteen years and I have never done it in a morning. I look at what a yard's cover is, what a yard publishes, what a yard's mistakes are, and what it is like to be a person who insured against that yard for a season.

"This yard publishes a column with the hours on it. That is a wonderful thing and I have said so and I have said it to about forty people this month. And as of Friday morning that column is in a Council's file, on the record, with a route officer's proviso printed at the top of it, and a cost printed on the third line with the hour on it, and an assessor has said in a room that it is the only document that demonstrates the method works.

"And on Friday morning, two carriers who had paper priced on the fifth of this month against a corridor figure that was an hour and a half wrong took that column, read it, and did a thing I have never known a carrier to do with a document they liked."

"What did they do."

"They priced it," said Maro Hesk. "They took the yard's own published errors and they priced the yard *with* them, in writing, at a number, and the number is not a punishment, it is a discount, and a discount is a thing you cannot argue with in a market, because a market has one opinion and it is a price.

"That is the entire mechanism and it took a fortnight and it was not aimed at anybody and I am telling you it has worked because you will hear it from somebody worse than me inside a month and I would rather you had it from me first, and because I am also going to say the other half."

"Go on."

"The other half is that they are *right*, and they have gone about it in the correct way, and no clause of anything anybody in this basin has ever written prevents it, and the yard asked for the column and got the column, and the column is now a discount, and I do not blame the Line, and I am not going to say in a corridor that I do not blame the Line twice."

Marek stood in the corridor and worked out, quite quickly, that the thing which had happened to the Kestrel Line over the past fortnight was the exact thing that had happened to the ninth ward's public glass, and that both of them had been caused by the same decision made by competent people for good reasons, and that neither of them was anybody's fault, and that **the word for it is that it became legible.**

The yard had been the only instrument in the basin that could time a corridor, and the yard had made that instrument readable, on purpose, in public, at its own cost, in chalk, in front of a carrier's man, because a term sheet that protects a reputation and not a body is not a term sheet a crew can work under.

And what had happened next was that reading had become pricing, everywhere, to everybody, and there was no instrument anywhere in the world that could tell a thing that had become legible from a thing that had been covered up, and a ward that cannot read its own notice is not more honest than a yard that prints its own errors.

It is only quieter.

"Right," said Marek Vale, to a broker of forty-one with his hands behind his back in a corridor on the ridge. "Thank you for telling me first."

"It was not a kindness," said Maro Hesk. "I am a careful man. I did it in a morning and I have nineteen years of mornings and I would like somebody in this building to have got the number from me so that the version that reaches the yard is the version that is true."
