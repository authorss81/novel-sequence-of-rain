# Chapter 16 — A Pipe That Is Already Full

He did not go to the kitchen first. He walked down Tannery Walk to the bottom of it on the Wednesday and then went back up the hill again, because two streets is not a distance and the whole of the afternoon had gone on deciding that it was. He went to the outfall on the Thursday instead, because there was a reason to go to the outfall, and because a man who has arranged to visit his father puts the visit after the errand, and because he had been constructing reasons since the top of Tannery Walk.

The reason was a plate and a gauge and about eleven minutes' work, and it was a real reason. The ninth ward's low ground drains through a run that comes out under a stone apron at the bottom of Sump Lane, and the apron had a tide-gauge on it that the district read by eye and the Office did not read at all, and Marek had a strip of glass in a tube and forty minutes before the light went off the outfall completely.

He got there at about half past four and stopped on the apron, because the outfall was full of men.

There were three of them. The oldest was fifty feet down a ladder into a stone throat with a lamp hooked on his helmet, and he was up to the thigh in moving water, and he was working a bar into a silt trap with the flat of it, and he was doing it the way a man does a thing he has done four hundred times, without hurry and without commentary.

"Vale," said the man on the ladder, without turning round.

Marek stood on the apron in the good coat with the tube under his arm and found that the sentence he had been carrying up Tannery Walk had gone out of him entirely, and that what was left was his father, in a wet waistcoat, in a hole.

"You can get the lantern off the hook," said Oren Vale. "And you'll want to stand on the third slab, the second's loose and it was loose when I left it on Thursday and it's been loose for thirty years."

He got the lantern off the hook. He stood on the third slab. He held the light.

Oren Vale worked for another eleven minutes without speaking, and in that eleven minutes Marek learned four things about the ninth ward's outfall that no instrument on the ridge had ever recorded: that the trap at the bottom had a stone lip on the downstream side that had been chipped by somebody's bar in about 1961 and had filled in with lime in the eighties and had begun to hold a wedge of silt that would hold more; that the apron slab directly over the mouth takes the load off the ladder ring if you put your heel on the crack and not your toe; that the water coming out of the throat on the fourteenth day was running at about half the rate it had been running on the eighth, for no reason anybody could give, and that this was the entire subject of the evening's conversation and it was going to take an hour.

"Right," said Oren at last, and came up the ladder, and put the bar against the wall, and took the lamp off his helmet. "You're going to tell me you came to look at a gauge."

"I came to look at a gauge."

"You came up Tannery Walk at half past four with a glass tube in a good coat and stood at the top of a ladder watching me clear a trap." Oren wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "Say the rest of it, Marek, or don't, but don't do the bit where you pretend you came for the tide gauge, because I have been doing this for nine years and you have never once in your life come to a district for a gauge."

Marek did not say the rest of it.

"I asked you something in the hall on Saturday," he said instead, "and Anwen Sarr read five things out in front of a hundred people, and one of them was that a surcharged outfall does not drain in half an hour."

"That is correct," said Oren Vale.

"It is correct," Marek agreed. "And the row drained in half an hour. And the throat at the bottom of it ran free with a lamp in it and nothing in it. Which means it wasn't the pipe."

"No," said Oren. "It was never the pipe."

He said it the way a man says the name of a thing he has been waiting to be asked about.

---

They did not go into the kitchen until seven, and when they did there was a plate of cold something on the table that had been on the table since the morning, and the kitchen was warm and it was small and it was exactly as Marek had left it six years ago, down to the third hook on the door where the keys used to hang.

He had been twenty-six. He had come down that road with a case and a warrant and his father had stood in the yard and said it was a career, and had said don't make a career of telling people what the weather is going to do to them, and Marek had laughed at him, because fathers are allowed remarks.

The outfall was still in his coat. He had washed the trench out of his hands twice and it was still in the cuffs, which his father noticed and did not mention.

He put a plate on the table.

"Sit down and eat that," said Oren Vale. "And then tell me what you actually came about, because you have had a face on since the ladder."

"Two things," said Marek. "One is a document."

"Uh."

"One is a document Anwen Sarr has asked me for and I have promised her, and I haven't written it, and I've had five days to write it and I haven't."

"Then you don't know what goes in it," said Oren Vale.

"No."

"Right," he said. "Well, that's honest, and it's the first honest thing you've said to me since the door. Ask your other thing."

Marek put his fork down.

"The row flooded," he said. "The lowest row on Hearthward, six ground-floor rooms, nine hours from about twenty-three forty to eight, and then half an hour to clear. The outfall is a straight stone throat at the foot of that row and it ran free. And the drains under the row — there are four catch pits under iron grates — they are cleared on a Tuesday and a Thursday by a crew of four and a foreman, and they were cleared on the Tuesday and not on the Thursday." He stopped. "And I knew the rota. Somebody told me the rota on the Wednesday morning, in a room, out loud, and I wrote it on a pad, and I never asked where the man went."

Oren Vale sat with his hands on the table and looked at his son.

"Aye," he said.

"Yes."

"Well, that's the whole of it then."

"That's the whole of what?"

"That's the whole of what happened," said Oren Vale, "and you know it, and you have known it since Friday morning, and there is exactly one thing missing from your pad and you have not been able to put it down, and I am going to put it down for you and then you can decide what to do about it, and you can do it in your own time, and it will cost you what it costs."

He stood up, went to the sink, and turned round again without filling anything.

"You have written it up in your head as a *surcharge*," he said. "And a surcharge is a pressure standing in a pipe, and a surcharged pipe cannot relieve itself, and the only two things that clear one are the inflow stopping and the pipe being able to discharge. That's a school thing. That's in a book. I learned it in a shed in a yard off Sump Lane in my twenties and I have heard every fool in Veyra say it since, including a man on a ridge about nine years ago, in my own front room, arguing with me about the ninth ward's outfall and getting it wrong."

"I didn't argue with you about—"

"You said a full main can push and I said it can't push, a main can only be pushed, and you said the difference was academic, and I said it is not academic, it is the entire job, and you went off up the hill." Oren Vale put his hands flat on the table. "Here is the difference. In a *surcharged* pipe the water is standing in the pipe and the pipe is the constraint, and when the inflow stops the pressure goes out of the line and the line relieves itself down into whatever is downstream, and it comes down, and everybody says the drains cleared.

"There is another condition and it has got the same name and it is not the same thing, and nobody upstairs has ever known it, because the person who knows it is not upstairs.

"The line is *full*. That is all. The line is full to the crown from the ordinary run of a terrace since Tuesday night, and it is full because it has been full for three days, and it is full because the water that should have gone out of it has nowhere to go while the reach is high — and a full pipe will take a terrace's rain and give nothing back. Not slowly. Not at all. The whole night's water is going in and none of the night's water is coming out, and the wall behind it goes up for exactly as long as the terrace has water in it, and it stops when the terrace stops having water in it, and then it stops in about ten minutes because a full pipe empties the second the upstream is empty.

"So when your row drained in half an hour at eight in the morning, the pipe was not clearing a surcharge. The pipe was *already clear*. The wall came down because the terrace ran out of water, and the pipe had been doing nothing the whole night. It was never the constriction. It was never the constraint. It was a full pipe in a district where somebody was relying on it not being full."

He stopped, and there was a clock going in the passage, and it was the same clock.

"Say the rest," said Marek.

"No."

"Gant Ollis said you did the ninth ward's outfall run for nine years."

"Aye."

"Then I know what a full pipe looks like in a district that leans on it, and I know what it costs, and I know it doesn't need a plate."

"No," said Oren Vale. "It doesn't."

He said it with what was almost satisfaction, which was worse than anything else he could have done to Marek that evening.

"Eleven years I have been telling the ward that the ninth ward runs on a *rota* and not on a *glass*," he said, "and every year they put the glass first in the budget, because glass is a line item with a serial number on it and a rota is a bit of paper in a woman's kitchen with a cup of tea on it, and I have watched every penny they have go on a plate that can only tell you what the sky is doing, and not one of them on the thing that actually keeps the low ground dry, which is a woman named Marda Loon telling four men when to go out with shovels, and having them go."

"That's what Anwen—"

"Don't," said Oren Vale.

The kitchen got very quiet.

"Sorry," Marek said.

"You were going to say her name," said Oren Vale, "and you were going to tell me she said it, and I've heard it, and it's the same as what I just told you and I'm not interested in the version of it that comes off a terrace and up a hill. If a woman on a terrace can tell you the rota, she can tell you the pipe. Your problem is not that nobody in this city can explain it. I am sixty-one years old and I have been explaining it to wardens for a decade and they put it in a book as a *local practice* and go and buy a plate."

He picked his tea up and found it cold and drank it anyway.

"Now. Your document."

Marek put his hands on the table so that they would stay there.

"Ask me the other question," said his father. "You've been sitting on it for an hour and a half. You've asked me one question you already knew the answer to and you've been sitting there not asking the other one since about ten o'clock on Saturday."

There was a long moment.

"Could I ask you about —" Marek started.

"No," said Oren Vale. "You could, and I would tell you, and it would not be any use to you, and I have not got the energy this week and neither have you. Sit down and finish your tea and go back up that hill."

He did not say the thing. He had come down Tannery Walk with a plate in a tube and a gap in a moisture trace and a document he owed a woman, and he had come two streets off his own errand to find out whether the man who had been the reason the low ground had a rota was the man who had been the reason the ward had no glass, and he had spent an hour and a half inside a kitchen with him and had got his mechanism and had not said the sentence.

He got up and put his coat on.

"There's a bracket off the third hook," said Oren Vale, not looking up. "Bent. I've had it a year. I could use a vice."

"I know," said Marek. "Next time I'm down."

"Mkay."

He was in the lane for about ten steps before his father said it, from the doorway, in exactly the tone of a man correcting a figure in a run sheet.

"Next time you're down," said Oren Vale, "come before the water's in it, Marek. Come on a Tuesday in the afternoon when it's a nuisance and nothing's happened. Then you'll learn something, and the district won't have to learn it the other way at four o'clock in the morning, and you'll be able to write your document without a man in a wet waistcoat standing in a hole being right at you."

Then the door, and the bolt, and Marek Vale standing in a lane in Low Spindle at half past eight in the evening with a tube in his coat and a document he could write now.

He walked back up the hill for the first time since he was nineteen, and it took fifty minutes instead of twenty on a bus, and there was no bus that late anyway, and by the top of it he had worked out that the reason his father was the better mechanic was not that his father knew things he did not know.

It was that his father had spent nine years standing in the water being told by people with instruments that the water was not his problem.
