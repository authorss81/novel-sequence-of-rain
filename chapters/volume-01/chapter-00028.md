# Chapter 28 — What the Council's Own Conditions Ask For

The claim as it finally stood on the sheet in his coat, in Marek Vale's hand — he had written it out three times that day and burned the first two in the shop's stove before lunch, out of an entirely superstitious habit he would not admit to under examination — was this:

*Place: the Kestrel corridor — the western lane over the reach, from the point to the yards' mark.*
*Trigger: the Kestrel Line's arrival at the yards' mark, accepted.*
*Effect, as entered: the Kestrel corridor remains passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth.*
*Listed alternative, as required: if the Line's arrival at the yards' mark is not accepted, no corridor is claimed; the draw backs at the western end, the return lane closes on the published band, the four carriers' window is not met, and the low wards' floodplain takes the draw for the same hours.*
*Expiry: 04:00 on the thirtieth day.*
*Sponsor: M. Vale, reader.*

He had got the expiry equal to the effect on purpose, and Sabin Orle had told him to do it on the Wednesday, and the reason was worth more than anything else she had said to him all month: *an expiry later than the effect is a period in which your claim's authority to send people outlasts your claim's stated reason for sending them. Every extra hour between those two is an hour in which a district is obeying a thing that has already stopped being true. Put them on the same line and the sheet is honest about the fact that the claim stops existing the moment it is spent.*

He had put the four conditions of the Kestrel Line in the room before he read the claim out, not after, because the third line of them was that the reading goes up before the claim and he was not going to break a line in chalk to save himself an afternoon.

---

The alternatives had all failed, and every one of them had failed for the right reason, and he had spent two days being instructed in the right reasons by people who were being kind about it.

The first was the Council's own conditions, and Sabin Orle had read them out on the Wednesday morning in eleven seconds and had then sat down.

*A claim certified under the Charter shall carry a named sponsor, a published expiry, a listed alternative, a concurring seat, a reader's attestation, and a positive routing effect at the yards within the expiry window.*

"That is not a condition," Marek said. "That is a shopping list."

"It is six conditions and you satisfy five of them and I have never been in a worse position in eleven years," said Sabin Orle. "Sponsor, you. Expiry, you have it and it is a nice one. Alternative, you have it, and Mr Vale, I want to say that your alternative is the best-written alternative I have read in this basin and I did not enjoy reading it, because it is the first one that names what the claim takes. Concurring seat — I can get you Kestrel. I have already asked and I have a delegate who will sign on Friday. Attestation, I have you." She put her finger on the last line. "*A positive routing effect at the yards within the expiry window.* That is the method. That is the whole of the method, and the method needs a number it can certify."

"Passable through 04:00 is a routing effect."

"It is a routing effect *conditional on the Line arriving and somebody accepting it*," said Sabin Orle. "Mr Vale, I cannot certify a number that depends on a private person doing a thing. If I could certify that, I would be certifying that the Kestrel Line is under the Council's direction, and the moment the Council can direct the Line the Line is not a witness, it is a resource, and every claim I have certified for eleven years has rested on the fact that the thing that made it true was somebody's own free act."

That was the answer, and it was correct, and Marek sat with it for the rest of the morning and a good part of the afternoon, because he had been half-expecting the Council to refuse him for a reason he could argue with and had been handed a reason he could not.

The second was the shape of the claim. An unconditional claim would have done the job. An unconditional claim is a promise about a system, and a promise about a system needs a chain, and there is a drawer in the records room on the ridge with a label on it and inside the drawer a requirement, and Marek had looked at it on the Tuesday and had the plate out of the tube on the bench in front of it, and had understood that the plate in his coat was nine inches of glass cut from the crown of a pane that the ninth ward had paid for and had never been sent, ground to a feed's thickness by his own hand, and entered in no register anywhere in the world.

He could not file it. He could not file it because the moment it was on a sheet with a plate number at the head of it, it was a reader's observation with a number, and a reader's observation with a number is an unattached observation, and an unattached observation is filed and stamped in the day's rack and is not compellable and is not producible and cannot be the instrument of a certified claim. Tovan Iles told him that in about forty seconds on the Tuesday, cheerfully, sitting at the bench, and then she said: "Also you would be signing it. And then it is yours, and not a chain. Mr Vale, a chain is a *plate the building owns* and a *registration behind it* and *a date*, and you do not have the middle one of those three in a way anybody would accept, and I have watched a Director try to argue around that in the eighties and it took a year and a district and it is not a thing you want to spend."

The third was arithmetical and nobody wasted any time on it. A claim that covered the yards and the low wards was a claim that said the same air would do two incompatible things, and every person in the basin had known that since the ninth, and the fact that it was arithmetically impossible was not an opinion in the Council chamber, it was the joint request's own second line.

And the fourth, on the Thursday, at about eleven, in a corridor, in a small level hand in violet ink on the return of a district's ordinary day-book.

The ninth ward had sent the Office its rain depths. It was an ordinary return, it went in the Friday post, and the figures on it were the ninth ward's own, read off the ward's own gauge by Sergeant Ampow, and they were four inches of rain on the twenty-second and two on the twenty-fourth, and the Office's ridge instruments for those two days had nothing like four inches in them.

*Reclassified 28th day, 11:15: unattached observation. The district gauge is not a registered instrument; a reading not taken by an instrument of the Office is not an instrumented reading. Not compellable. Not producible. Rule 6, Charter of Public Notice.*

Marek read it in the corridor and then read the four lines of a district warden's own list in his memory, and understood the shape of the machine completely for the first time, and it was this: the district's gauge was a better gauge than the Office's for four hundred and forty people on a low ground, and the only thing that would have made it count was the Office's own plate in the ninth ward tower, and the only thing that would have made *that* count was a man with a chain and a motive and a reason to be in that tower with a lamp, and none of those three things was available to anybody.

He went and found Malle at half past eleven, which was a mistake, and got what he expected, which was also true.

"The last time you stood up in a certifying room in this city you were unable to support one syllable of what you said, and you were right, and I have put it in a file, and the file is the reason I can put a man on this watch who is not you." Malle did not look up. "You have no standing in a certifying room, Vale. You had some on the eighteenth and you spent it on the nineteenth and you were right to spend it, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I would have given it back."

"That is not what I came about."

"Then what did you come about?"

"I came about the fact that I have got a claim in a drawer that I cannot make true," said Marek, "and that there is exactly one instrument in this basin that can time a corridor, and that it is in my coat, and that I have four days of weather in front of me."

Malle put his pencil down.

"Then you have a claim and a tube," he said, "which is a very familiar condition and it has been fatal eleven times in the history of this building. Go and do the other thing."

---

He did the other thing at about ten past two on the Thursday afternoon, and it took him forty minutes to get down Tannery Walk and forty minutes more to say the sentence, and the sentence was two sentences and he had rehearsed both of them on the way.

"Tarin. I have got a claim that nobody will certify because it has no effect they can put a number on, and the reason it has no number is that the only thing that can time your corridor is a plate with no chain behind it. I am not asking you to believe the plate. I am asking you for a hull."

She was at the boards. She had been at the boards for most of a week, in a yard where the lift worked, with three ships on the apron and a column of differences eight days old, and the yard had begun, in the way yards do, to leave her alone there.

"Ask me for it properly," said Tarin Senn. "In front of the board, with the words in the right order, because whatever you say next is going to be on a sheet in about a day and I want the words to be the right ones."

---

So he said it at the boards, in the yard, in front of about nine people and a man from a carrier's office, with the hours written on a slate beside them.

"I have a claim. It is conditional. Its condition is the Kestrel Line's arrival at the yards' mark, accepted. Its effect is that the corridor remains passable to and through four o'clock on the thirtieth, and its expiry is four o'clock on the thirtieth, and its alternative is that if the Line does not accept the mark the corridor is not claimed and the low wards' floodplain takes the draw for the same hours. I am the sponsor. I have no chain and I have told the Council so and they have certified on that basis in four previous instances.

"I cannot make that claim true by myself. I have not got an instrument with a registration behind it that can time your corridor, and the one I have got is a piece of glass I cut out of a ward's unpaid notice pane in a glassblower's shop and there is no chain on it in the world. So the claim's truth has to be witnessed by a person who can fly the hours and who is not under my direction and who is not in my employment, and there is one such person in this basin and she is standing next to a board.

"I am asking for a hull and a window, and I am not asking you to believe anything. The claim names the Line in the sponsor's own line and not in a footnote. Your reading goes up before the claim. If our figures differ, both go up with the difference in a column. And if the claim is wrong, my name is on it and yours is on the arrival, and the yard's own errors are published as the yard's own."

---

Tarin Senn read the four lines on the slate beside her, in chalk, in her own hand, in the yard, and then she read the proviso under them in Ilva Sorn's small flat hand, and then she stood for about eight seconds in the particular quiet of a woman doing arithmetic that is not about money.

"I have flown the line in dry air on local glass twice," she said. "Both times in the last two seasons. Both times on my own box and my own pattern and my own eleven years, and I was wrong once and the once was the fifteenth of the month, and it cost me a month of the *Petrel*'s overhead and I have never once told anybody which reading it was. So if I give you this, you are asking a question, and the honest answer is that I think I can do it and I have been wrong before, and I am telling you that in front of nine people instead of telling you it in a corridor later."

"Noted," said Marek. "And the four conditions are honoured, and if the claim is true the Line is named, and if the claim is false you will be able to be shown that you were in the lane and I was the one who said it would be there, and both of those go on the board."

"Yes." She put her hand flat on the slate. "And here is the part I have thought about for about six minutes and I am going to say out loud, because if I do not say it now somebody will find it in a fortnight and put it in an argument.

"Those four lines are about the yard. Every one of them is about the yard. The reading goes up, the hours go up, the difference goes up, the Line's name goes in the sponsor's own hand and not in a footnote — that is the yard's *name*, Mr Vale. That is the yard's reputation on a sheet, and it is the thing I have spent a fortnight building, and it is worth what I said on Sunday that it is worth, and I do not regret one line of it.

"There is not one line in the four about my crew. There is not one line about the hands on the rail at the mark, or the two hours a man spends on a wire in the dark, or what happens to a ship that is in the lane at an hour that the yard has published and cannot honour. I wrote those four lines. I wrote them in chalk in front of a carrier's man. And they are a settlement about the yard and they are not a settlement about anybody who has to be *in* the thing."

"So we need a fifth line."

"No," said Tarin Senn. "We do not need a fifth line. A fifth line is another promise and we are short enough of those. What we need is for the person who writes the claim to be the person who is answerable for the claim's *second* effects, and that is not a yard matter, and it is not a term sheet matter, and I am telling you now that I do not know what it is and that I have not got time to find out before the twenty-ninth."

"Neither have I," said Marek. "Write the hours on the slate."

---

She wrote the hours on the slate herself, in chalk, in the yard: the *Petrel* and the two ships strung behind her, the corridor band from a quarter past four on the twenty-ninth, the mark at about eleven at night, the close at four, and her own reading of the draw under the band with the hour it was taken, and *the Office's figure beside it* and the third column headed *difference*, which was the width of a thumb and which Nessa Tarrow had copied out by the time the chalk was off the slate.

"Signed here," said Tarin Senn, and put the Line's own hand under it.

Nobody in that yard cheered. There is not a facility for it and it would not have been correct. What happened is that Ilva Sorn wrote the hours into the route book and made a launch against the morning of the twenty-ninth for three ships and a returning fourth, and a man from a carrier's office asked whether the yard was putting four hulls inside somebody's claim and Ilva Sorn said, without looking up: "We are putting a witness in a corridor at an hour, and the difference between those two things is the entire reason this yard has anything to sell."

And Marek stood in the wet concrete of Kestrel Yards at about half past three on the Thursday afternoon of the twenty-eighth day of a Bellweather autumn and felt the warmth go through him that had gone through him twice before in a month and a half, in a hall on a Thursday and in this yard on a Sunday, and this time he did not even take a second to identify it, because he had a rule about it now, and he knew exactly what it was.

*This is now somebody else's to do.*

Except that this time it was not somebody else's to do. This time he had walked down a hill and asked a woman for the thing, and she had said yes, and she had said it in a yard in chalk with the hours on it, and the yes was on a board, and the board was public, and the claim was going to be entered tomorrow at the ward ledger bench with a bell and a clerk and a Director's approval stamp on it, and the whole of the machinery of it was in his hands.

He had the warmth and he had four days of weather in front of him and a claim in his coat with an effect in it and an expiry on it, and a plate in a tube, and a private instrument that nobody could check, and he was going to go and enter a true thing into a good ledger in a good hand with a witness who had agreed to be there.

And there was not one single person in the city who could have stopped him, and every single thing he was about to do was correct.

He got on the tram at four and went up the hill, and did not sleep much, and the claim sheet went into the inside pocket, and the plate went into the tube and the tube went into the coat and the coat went onto a hook by the bench in the shop, and he stood for a moment looking at the tube the way you look at a thing that is the only reason anybody is going to be able to tell whether you were right.
