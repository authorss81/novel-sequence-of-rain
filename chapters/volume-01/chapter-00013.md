# Chapter 13 — What the Hulls Are Worth

The pin came up from the Nacre road on the Friday post and the lift worked on Monday, and the whole yard noticed, because a lift that works is the difference between a ship going out and a ship sitting in eight inches of water explaining itself.

By ten on the eleventh day Tarin had three ships on the apron and a fourth coming round the point, and she was standing on the boards with her hands behind her back when Ivo Slate came down the lane with his case under his arm and his licence in his buttonhole, in front of a man she had not seen in Veyra before in her life and did not know the name of.

"Captain Senn," said Slate. "May I present Mr Maro Hesk."

Hesk was about forty and dressed like a man who has money and has decided that being dressed like a man who has money is a waste of a good jacket. He shook her hand and did not apologise for taking it.

"Three things," he said, "and I will do them in the wrong order, because the wrong order is the useful one. First, nothing I say here is privileged and I am not licensed to say any of it, so if you want it in writing, tell me and I will come back with paper you can nail up. Second, I am not here about Saturday. Saturday was weather and everybody's got a story about Saturday. Third, your yard board is Exhibit A and I would like to buy it."

"Slate's agency report," said Tarin.

"Slate's agency report," Hesk agreed. "Which he filed on the Wednesday, the day after your board carried an hour that anybody could check and three days before the collision, and which I have had read out to me by a man who does not understand any of it and understood every word of it. *Time of observation, instrument, and the source of the contradiction.* He is quite right that the source was your board, and he is quite right that the Office's window was understated at the time of sale, and neither of those things is the interesting part."

"What is the interesting part."

"That I have paid for it."

Tarin looked at him properly for the first time.

"Four carriers," Hesk said. "A grain factor out of Nacre Holds who has been buying windows off this Office since before you were born. Two insurers who are, and I say this as a man who sells to insurers, the only honest money in the business, because they do not need the sky to be dramatic, they need it to be the same on Tuesday as it was priced on Monday." He turned a hand over. "All of us took a Bellweather corridor window on the fifth day. It was sealed. It was licensed. It was, by the standards of this city, extremely good paper. And on the ninth day the Office's own bulletin put the front in the basin at eighteen hundred and said the strongest turn of the week came through at ten o'clock, and the corridor window we had all paid against was an hour and a half long."

Nobody in the lane said anything.

"Now let me be exact, because I am going to be accused of exaggeration for the rest of my life," Hesk said. "The Office did not lie to me. The Office sold a number it believed, under a standard clause, and the number was about a corridor, and about a corridor the number turned out to be short by an hour and a half. I am not going to call that fraud. A man in a good coat with a glass on the ridge six weeks ago would have told you the same and would have been telling the truth. What I am going to say is that it is a very expensive way to be true."

"You want a refund," said Tarin.

"I want the next one to be a number I can stand behind in front of an underwriter in Red Sill, and there is no such thing at present, and that is not a complaint about your yard or about the Office's honesty. It is a complaint about the city." He looked up at the boards. "Which is why I am standing in your yard rather than in a room with a seal on the door."

"Get to the part where you ask me for something," said Tarin Senn.

"I have not asked you for anything," said Hesk. "That is the entire point of this visit. If I had asked you for something today, in this yard, in front of your night watch, I would have got it, because the joint request is on a wall on the ridge with two signatures on it and it is arithmetically correct, and every carrier in this yard is quietly hoping that somebody in a good coat asks them to put their hulls somewhere in particular. So I have come to tell you what that ask is going to sound like, three days before anybody makes it, so that you can price it."

He said it flatly, the way a man reads out a bill.

"Somebody is going to tell you that the Kestrel Line is a resource. That is the word; get used to it, it is a word that has been waiting for this week. They will tell you that the yards need the western draw held open for the return lane, and that the low wards need that same air to come down on the floodplain, and that the two cannot both happen, and that therefore there must be one claim, and that the claim needs a corridor, and that the only crew in this yard that flies a corridor on its own glass without a seal on it is yours. And then they will ask you to hold the lane, and your box will do it, and it will work, and it will be recorded that it worked, and your name will be on the instrument of a decision that four businesses already paid for."

"And the alternative to holding the lane?"

"There is no alternative to holding the lane," said Hesk pleasantly. "There is only an alternative to you doing it. The air will hold the lane or it will not, and if it does not, the four of us are uninsured on a corridor they have paid for, and then the next time there is a window in this basin, we buy it from the Exchange at a price that has been set by the knowledge that the Office cannot be relied upon. That is how prices are made, captain. Not by greed. By four people getting hurt once."

Tarin had been standing still for the whole of it, which was how she knew it had landed.

"And if I hold the lane and it works," she said, "what do you get?"

"Cover," said Maro Hesk. "A corridor that the Office has claimed and that a licensed carrier flew. My four houses get their premium back and I get to tell the underwriters in Red Sill that the answer to *what happens when a forecast is wrong* is not a shrug but a claim with an expiry on it and a sponsor's name at the bottom." He put his hands in his pockets. "And you get a charter market that believes a yard can be inside a certified corridor without being swallowed by it, which is worth more to you in a year than any single cargo, and which I have no way to buy and can only ask you to keep."

"That," said Tarin, "is a remarkably honest statement of a remarkably expensive favour."

"Yes."

"Right," she said. "Then here is what it costs, and I am writing it down before anybody asks, because I have learned this month that a thing asked for on a Tuesday is a thing agreed to by Wednesday."

She took the slate from the board hook and the chalk, and she wrote on it in her own hand, standing up, in the yard, in front of Slate and Hesk and the morning shift:

**1. If the Line's glass is used to make a claim true, the Line's reading goes on the board with the hours on it, whatever the Office's window says.**
**2. The reading goes up before the claim, not after.**
**3. The claim names the Line. Not the Office, not the Council, the Line.**
**4. If the Line's reading and the Office's figure differ, both go up, and the difference goes up too, in a column, with the hours.**

Ivo Slate read it over her shoulder.

"That is a condition of cooperation," he said, "not a term of a licence, and I am not permitted to agree to it and I am not going to pretend that I can."

"Then agree to it as a man standing in a yard," said Tarin Senn, "and put the same four lines on your board, and if the Exchange is not prepared to have its own board carry what my board carries, then you have told me something about the Exchange this morning that I did not know and had no way of finding out."

Slate considered it for about four seconds, and then took the chalk out of her hand and wrote the four lines under his own header on the Exchange agents' board, in a small level hand, with his licence number at the bottom.

"There," he said. "The Exchange does not publish. But the Exchange's agents may be present on a board that does, and I will be present on it, and I will read it out to anybody who asks me what is on it."

"That," said Maro Hesk, looking at the two boards, "is the first thing anybody in this trade has done in eleven years that will not appear in a pamphlet."

"It will appear in my agency report," said Slate.

"God help you," said Hesk, and put his hat on, and went up the lane, and did not look back, which Tarin noticed, because a man who does not look back at a yard has either got what he came for or has decided not to want it.

She went down to the south bank after lunch, because there was a thing on her list and it had been on her list for two years and she had never once been able to make it an item on anybody else's.

The Lower Sill culvert is a Bellweather-era piece of concrete with a stone dedication plate on the headwall that is the oldest surviving use of the name in the city. It goes under the freight apron and it takes the reach, and the reach takes the low ground of the ninth ward, and the low ground is where Tarin had spent the most frightening two days of the last year with a lamp in a market hall and a woman of sixty-eight in a nightgown.

The headwall is cracked. It has been cracked since before she started flying and it has been shored twice, and the second shoring is a thing that four men put up in a hurry and that has held for six years on the strength of the first.

There is a timber stack in the open-air store at the south-bank shed, banded, weathered, and labelled, and there is a dewatering pump standing on a pallet beside it with a canvas cover on it and a Union guarantee on the drum, and there is a notice board outside the shed, and the notice board has been repainted four times.

The board says: *LOWER SILL CULVERT — SHORING TIMBER AND REPLACEMENT DEWATERING PUMP. NINE CREW, LOADED. REPAIR WINDOW ON THE REACH LEVELS AS PUBLISHED BY THE WATER OFFICE.*

Nobody has cancelled it. That was the part Tarin could never get anybody to hear. There was no decision in any building in Veyra to not repair the Lower Sill culvert. It was simply that it was classified as maintenance, and maintenance has a queue, and the queue had been behind it for two years, and the timber had been banded and the pump had been on a pallet and nine men had been assigned, and in two years the crew had not once been stood down by anybody; they had simply not been required, because the thing they were loaded for was not weather.

The reach levels mattered more than anything else, and the reach only came down when the basin's draw was on it, and the draw comes with a soft dry spring, and this had been a wet one, so the reach had been high all through the Bellweather and the flat at the bottom of it was a foot under the mud and staying there, and the board had been repainted in the autumn with a new date on it and nobody had looked at the date since.

"Window?" said Tarin to the shed foreman, who was a grey man called Petrie who had been keeping that shed since before her father. Behind him the shed door stood open on the flat, and out at the edge of it where the hard ground gave up there was a post with a weathered mark cut round it at about the height of a man's knee, and the mud stood four inches over the mark, and had for weeks.

"Levels come down about the end of the month," said Petrie. "That is when the mark shows."

"Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days, near enough. There is a fortnight of it when it is right and about nine days of it when the low flat will take a loaded flatbed and a pump, and it is not weather, captain, it is water, and it will come when it comes."

"Right," said Tarin. "So nobody has to decide anything."

"No, ma'am," said Petrie. "Nobody has to decide anything. It just does not come."

She stood in that yard for a while longer than she needed to, in the particular kind of quiet a yard has on a Monday, with a lift that worked and three ships on the apron, and she thought about a man in a wet coat at a drain telling four hundred people the order of the sky, and about a foreman on a stone step with a barrow he had not used, and about a ward that had been saved by a sentence and ruined by a Tuesday.

Then she went back up the lane and wrote, on the back of the fourth line of her own conditions, in chalk, small:

*Levels, not weather. That is why nobody has to decide anything.*
