# Chapter 23 — An Instrument's Account of Itself

The outer office of the Weatherglass had a bench down each side and a window that looked east over the yard and a clock that was four minutes fast, and for eleven days in the twenty-second, twenty-third and twenty-fourth of the Bellweather autumn, Marek Vale sat at the end of the right-hand bench and was the only person in the building who was not waiting for something.

The Bellweather watch had been stood down on the eleventh. His name had come off it and gone onto nothing at all, and the list the office kept of the people who were on something had been pinned inside the field office door for a fortnight and a half with his name not on it, and nobody had taken it down and nobody had put a line through his name either. It had simply stopped appearing.

He got up at the same time. He went in at the same time. He was in the shop by seven, and the shop was cold, and there was work in it, because there is always work in a maintenance shop, and he did it.

He reglazed a tide-gauge cover for the water office's district crew. He re-caged an aneroid. He ground and polished a beaker for a district clerk's rain-gauge float that had been slug-eaten since the spring. He went down the hill at half past eleven and glazed two dials for Feth Raal, who has had a stall at the foot of the Weatherglass steps for fifty-four years and has never paid him for any of it, and who told him on the Tuesday that four men had been in asking after narrow forecasts, and that they had stopped coming, and that this was the most interesting thing that had happened to her stall in a decade.

"They went off you," said Feth Raal. "That's what happened. They came in the first week and they went off in the third."

"Four men in a good coat."

"Two of them were," she said. "The other two were the ones you get brought along by." She set the second dial on her board. "It's not a complaint. I'm saying a thing happened."

He went back up the hill with two glazed dials and a new glass of a shopman's glass in his coat and the whole of the day in front of him, and the day did not once require him.

That was the part. He had been braced for it in the way a man is braced for a blow and it did not come as a blow. Nobody stopped him in a corridor. Nobody said anything about the nineteenth. Nobody put him on anything, or left him off anything, or mentioned that there was a thing in his coat. The work of the office went on around the hole in it and nobody apologised for the hole, because the hole had a shape and the shape had a cause and the cause was not anybody's mood; the cause was that the office uses the instrument that works, and the instrument that works on the nineteenth had been a man who could produce paper, and on the twenty-second the man who could produce paper was a woman in a technical cell in the basin house with four certified claims in a folder.

That was the whole of it, and it was correct, and it was the Office behaving exactly as the Office behaves, and Marek understood inside about forty seconds of walking back up the hill that this was worse than punishment, and it took him the rest of the day to work out why.

Punishment is a thing that happens to you by a person. This was not happening to him. This was the building carrying on with the best man it had, in the ordinary way, the way a mill carries on with the best pair of hands it has, and if the man was standing at the end of the bench doing the tide-gauge covers, then that was because the tide-gauge covers needed doing and the man was not needed for anything that required paper.

He had been useful on the ninth night of a Bellweather front to nineteen people on a stone floor. He had been useful on the seventeenth of the same month to a yard, in a way that was still on a slate in chalk. And on the nineteenth he had stood up in a room and been useful in the only way he had ever been useful that actually mattered, which was the way that costs the person who is useful, and the room had gone on clapping, and the room had written down that he was a man who said things he could not support, and the Office had done the only reasonable thing, which was to stop relying on him.

He thought about the plateau. The permanent ringing was gone. What was left was a thinness on the left, in the middle of the range where a reader's judgement lives, and he had learned to put a hand up to that ear in a street and give it a second and then take the hand down and be a man in a street.

---

The Council's report on the trial claim came down the ridge on the twenty-second and went to the file and out to the seats, and Marek read it in the outer office on the Wednesday morning because the outer office got everything, and it was a good report.

It was, in the assessment of a man who has no reason to be charitable to anybody in that building, one of the better pieces of writing the Council has produced in his working life, and he could not find a false statement in it.

*Certified claim entered at the Nacre Gate, nineteenth day. Trigger and expiry observed. The Ferrygate low side drained clear to the certified watch. The certified road took eleven carts and two barrows from first light on the twenty-first. The Nacre Holds grain factor completed four barges on the 04:40 to 08:00 window. The district assembly acknowledged the certification, withdrew its local flood precautions for the day, and resumed ordinary commerce, and has asked the Council to accept that the method as certified cost the district nothing it was willing to record. The method is recommended for continued trial.*

Six minutes. The sixth minute praised the method in a sentence that Marek could have given to Anwen Sarr as a specimen of how a thing is written when the writer has checked.

And then, at the end, in the small print where the technical cell always puts the things it does not want read aloud, the annex. Bound separately, as Sabin Orle had ordered it bound separately on the afternoon of the nineteenth, so that a district reading the file could not miss it and could not mistake it for an objection.

*Annex. Statement by the attesting reader, entered as a statement and not as an objection, unattributed to any district. The reader asserts that the certified claim displaced a coordinated preparation in the affected district. The reader identifies no district, no person, and no instrument capable of being produced, and states that the evidence consists of a thinning in the Release on the gate's record plate. The reader states that the claim is true. Rule 6, Charter of Public Notice, and the note of the Deputy for Records and Public Notice appended hereto.*

It was four lines long. It would be read by nine watershed seats and three regional seats over the next four weeks, and every one of them would read the same sentence in it, and that sentence was: *identifies no district, no person, and no instrument capable of being produced.*

There was a stamp at the bottom of it in violet. It was not the filed mark. It was a records stamp, and it said, in the level hand of a man who had processed a great many pieces of paper in his life:

*Filed as a statement. An assertion not supported by a document is not compellable and is not evidence of a cost. Rule 6, Charter of Public Notice.*

Marek read it three times and then put the folder back on the spike where it had been, in the correct place, in the correct order, and stood there for a moment with his hand on the edge of the shelf.

He did not feel anger. That was the thing he had been braced for and it did not arrive either.

What arrived, instead, in about the time it took to walk back to the bench and sit down, was the understanding that a machine which works like this is not a machine with a fault in it. It is a machine that has been designed to be exactly this, by people who thought carefully and correctly about what a record is for. The reader's statement is in the file. It is bound separately so that it cannot be missed. And the reader has said in his own words that he can produce nothing, and the machine has checked, and the check has come out the way the machine checks everything, and the result is that the statement travels to twelve seats and does work there exactly as a piece of paper saying *the sky is probably going to be unpleasant tomorrow* would work.

Which is to say: it would be read, and nodded at, and filed.

He thought about a woman in a corridor on the ninth of the month, with a clause in a filing, drafted so that a Council could satisfy the first sentence of it and fail the second.

The first sentence of Anwen Sarr's clause four was that no claim certified under the Charter be entered without the cost of that claim being published at the point of entry.

The cost had been published at the point of entry. It was in the annex. It was bound separately. It had a records stamp under it, and the records stamp was correct, and the second sentence of clause four was that the sponsor record in writing that the cost cannot be made public and why.

He had not written it. He had told her in a room with a clerk in it that it was not ready, and she had told him that in eleven years nobody had ever said a thing to her was not ready, and that she was going to use that sentence on people for the rest of her life, and that it would be his fault.

It was not ready. It was going to be ready in about four years, or in about forty, and in the meantime the Office was entirely within its rights and had done everything it was asked to do.

---

Neris Kade's five lines arrived on the Tuesday morning, in an envelope with the district's stamp on it, addressed to the Deputy for Field Forecasts and not to a person, and the covering note said that it was an internal district record and not a public notice and that the warden had promised it on the ninth of the month and was discharging the promise.

Marek was not on it and was not asked to be, and he read it over Malle's shoulder in about ninety seconds because Malle was reading it standing up and holding it at arm's length in the way of a man who has been in the job for thirty years and does not need his glasses until four o'clock in the afternoon.

"That's a fair set of lines," Malle said.

"It is."

"It is not a complaint, though. That's what I want to say about it before anybody in this building starts treating it as one." He handed it over. "She has not complained once. She has written down what a set of notices did not say, in the order they came, with dates, and she has not accused anybody of anything, and if I were a district clerk I would be quietly proud of her."

"She has not been given the plate," Marek said, and heard himself, and wished he had not said it in that building.

Malle turned round and looked at him for slightly longer than the sentence deserved.

"No," he said. "She has not."

That was the entire exchange. Malle put the sheet in a tray on the corner of his desk, on top of two other trays, and went back to a rostle — and then, having stood there a moment:

"She has a right to it, you know. Under anything. She is asking the wrong question. She has been asking me what the notice said. What I would say to her, if she asked me what the notice *measured*, is that a notice is not a scientific instrument, it never has been, and I do not know what the ninth ward's measures on a wet Tuesday and neither does she." He said it with something almost like affection. "And I will tell her that, because it is true, and because she has been sent a piece of paper that is going to be sitting in a tray in this building for four months, and it is not going to be in a tray in four months, and the only way to arrange that is to give the warden an answer she can carry."

He did not give her an answer that week. What came back, on the twenty-fifth, was a reclassification of the list itself as an internal district record, not a public notice — which was correct, and which was in Venn's hand, and which meant that five lines in a warden's handwriting had become a document in a building the warden did not control.

And on the twenty-third, at the counter, in the ordinary course, a small broad-shouldered clerk with a face like a closed ledger stamped one more thing.

It was the Ferrygate district's ordinary return of the fifteenth, the one that had gone into a rack in the technical cell three days before a claim was certified and had sat in the rack ever since because nobody in the building knew what it was.

*Local flood precautions, list. Low side channels and causeway, cleared Tues and Thurs in the wet season, six-person rota, names attached, no forecast made, no claim entered.*

*Reclassified 23rd day, 4:40: local record. Not a notice. No forecast, no place, no time, no effect, no witness; no claim within the meaning of the Charter. Not compellable. Not producible. Rule 6, Charter of Public Notice.*

Marek read it twice.

He read it the second time because it was, like everything that came out of that counter, technically immaculate, and because the covering note in Nell Cray's hand said *There is no forecast in this and no claim. It is not on any public notice and I do not put it on one. I send it because your box asked*, and the box had asked, and the answer had come back eight days late and in the wrong century.

It was the correct classification. Of course it was. The form had asked for a list of local flood precautions and a clerk had supplied a list of local flood precautions, and there is no place in the Charter for a column of days with six names down it and two hours on a Tuesday, because that is not a forecast and does not make a claim and does not bind anybody to anything, and no clerk anywhere in Veyra had ever been able to say the word *promise* out loud in connection with it.

He found that his hand had gone flat on the counter.

And the thing he felt, standing there in the outer corridor at a quarter to five on the twenty-third day, was not anger at all, and he examined it, because he had a rule about examining it now.

It was the specific and quite cold understanding of a man who has just watched a piece of paper go into a drawer in a building he has nothing to do with, and who understands — and understands clearly, and for the first time without a single flicker of a wish that it were otherwise — that the correct classification does not mean that the rota was nothing. It means that nobody can be shown that it was anything. And that the difference between those two is the entire machine, and that he is on the wrong side of it, and that he has been on the wrong side of it since the sixth day of this month when he put a plate in a tube instead of on a bench with a chain behind it.

He stood there for perhaps half a minute.

Then he went back and did the aneroid, and did it well.

---

The joint request was still on the field office board.

It had been there since the ninth. It was two lines long and both lines were correct, and the yards had not withdrawn it, and neither had the low wards, and the Bellweather watch it had been pinned under had been stood down on the eleventh and the pin had been left in the board with a nail in it and nothing under the nail.

Marek read it twice on the twenty-fourth and then wrote, in a private note he did not file, four words: *Nobody has answered this. That is the answer.*

Anwen Sarr's clause four had been answered by nobody, and nobody was going to be punished for that, and the Council would report on the trial in the spring, and the spring would have a different claim in it.

---

He did not write the document.

He tried on the Tuesday and the Wednesday and got as far as the second page, and on the Thursday he came into the shop at seven in the morning, and put his coat over the back of a stool, and took out a sheet of the shop's own paper, the cheap kind, the kind he used for bench sheets, and understood that what he was going to write was not the document and that it was the thing that was left when the document was taken away.

He wrote four hundred words in his own hand and it took him fifty minutes, and it is a good document, and it is true.

It is a maintenance report on the ninth ward tower's sequence feed. It is headed with the tower, the seat, and the serial number of the plate in it, which is the plate he cut out of Office stock in the first week of the month and ground to the feed's thickness in an afternoon and fitted on the Wednesday, and which Malle had put a knuckle against and said *plate* about, and passed in nine minutes.

It records that the feed plate numbered 4471 cracked in service on the fourth day and was rendered, read, and replaced on the same night; that the replacement was cut from Office stock and is the plate now in the seat; that the feed in this seat has a documented dropout, logged in March, cause recorded, the cause being a shear washer on the seat, and that the recorded fault presents as a hard step and a grey crescent; and that on the night of the eighth to the ninth of the month, in the second turn, the moisture trace in this feed thinned over a quarter of an inch of glass and returned on a shallow slope, forty minutes late, and that the recorded March fault does not present in that manner and is therefore not the explanation; and that the feed ran eight days and ten hours from the evening of the eighth to the morning of the sixteenth without fault of any kind, in the largest collision this tower has recorded in nine years, and that a feed that produces no fault in that weather was not the constraint on the second turn.

It records that a spare plate has been cut, ground, arrised, annealed, boxed and dated, and is on the shelf under the seat, and is not fitted.

It records that a permanent brass surround has been bedded around a temporary public pane, and gives the two reasons, and the two reasons are that the permanent was cracked in the autumn and that its replacement was quoted, ordered, and paid for in the district's name and has not been collected, the district's glasswork allocation not extending to a man and a crate sent forty miles, and that therefore the ward's public notice has been unreadable for a fortnight, and that the ward has four hundred and forty people on its low ground and a chalk board and four women with good handwriting.

And in the last third of it, in the same hand and with the same plate number at the head of it, it records the order in which the six movements arrived in that feed on the night of the ninth, which is Gather alone and early; Weight not completing, with Break in on top of it; Route twice, a short hook beneath a long one; Release beginning and completing; and Witness arriving out of order, upon the Break rather than upon the Release.

It is four hundred and eleven words and it is the only true account of the Bellweather collision in the city.

He read it back once. It is all true. It is all documentable. It is entirely in his own hand with a plate number on the top of it, and it is a maintenance report, and a maintenance report is a thing a building has a drawer for.

And it cannot be filed, and he knew it before he had written the second sentence, and he wrote it anyway, and the reason he wrote it anyway is the reason he had got up at seven that morning.

The moment that sheet went into any tray in that building it stopped being a maintenance report.

It would become a reader's private observation. Nine inches of glass cut from the crown of a pane the ninth ward had paid for and had never been sent, ground to a feed's thickness by the maintenance hand himself, carried in a tube in a coat, run in a seat in a public building for one night and taken out at ten past six in the morning by a keeper who had been told to ask nothing, never entered, never witnessed, never numbered in any register, and now producing a reading with no chain behind it whatever. And the building's own rule — not a harsh one, not a rule anybody had ever enforced against anybody, the ordinaryest rule in the Charter — was that a reading with no place, no time, no effect and no witness is not a claim and is not compellable and is not producible, and the moment the words *and in the second turn the moisture trace* appeared in a document signed by a reader, the document would be read as an allegation by a man with an interest, and the plate number at the head of it would be the plate number of a piece of glass that did not exist.

He sat with the pen down.

There was a second version, and it was the version Anwen Sarr had actually asked for, and he had been circling it since the ninth of the month.

The honest version of that document — the document that said what a claim costs, in the sponsor's own hand, naming the turns it displaced and the preparations that stopped being available — could not be built on his own readings, because his own readings had no chain. It had to be built on an instrument. And there was exactly one instrument in the city that carried the collision's order in a registered, instrumented, tamper-evident form on a public building's own plate, and it was a metre and a half above a public door on Tannery Walk, in a locked room, in the poorest ward in the city, in Office glass in an Office holder.

Which was the tower. Which was the plate. Which was also, as far as he knew, the only instrument in the basin carrying a complete Witness movement dated twelve years back, arriving in the glass from below rather than out of the air, in the tower's own stamped margin, on the sixth night of the plate's legible run.

He put the pen down and looked at the wall for a while.

There was a document he could write tomorrow that would be a maintenance report with a twelve-year-old registration in it, and it would be a public document, and it would be sealed, and it would be copied into nine ward files, and every assessor in the basin would read it. It would also be a document about his mother, and a document about a claim lodged and witnessed twelve years ago, and a document that said the Office's own most competent reader had found something in the Office's own most distinguished instrument and had sat on it for a fortnight. It would be a scandal before it was an hour old. It would put the ninth ward's feed and the ninth ward's public frame and the ninth ward's tower in front of a Council inquiry, and the ward has four hundred and forty people on its low ground and no money and an unreadable notice, and the ward would be the story, and the collision would be a footnote in somebody else's document, and the thing Anwen Sarr wanted — an account of what a good claim costs, in a form a district can hold — would be forty feet underneath it and would never come up.

So there were two doors and both of them were shut, and one of them was shut because he had no chain and the other one was shut because the chain belonged to a plate that held a dead woman's filing mark in the margin, and he was the only person alive who knew that, and there was no arrangement of any kind by which telling anybody was not the end of everything he had been for eleven years.

He read the four hundred and eleven words back a second time and folded it in three, and put it in the second drawer of his bench under the sheet of bench paper with four times on it from the eighth of the month, and shut the drawer.

It was the second time in a month he had put a true document in a drawer, and the reason was the same reason both times, and this time he noticed himself doing it, and this time he wrote nothing down about noticing, and the not-writing was the only part of the whole business he was still any good at.
