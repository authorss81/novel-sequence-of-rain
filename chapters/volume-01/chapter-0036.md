# Chapter 36 — The List of Citations

The summons came down at about a quarter past eight in the morning on the thirty-fourth day of the Bellweather autumn, and it came down the ordinary way, which is to say that it was on the spike in the outer office of the Weatherglass and it was not addressed to anybody in particular and it had been there since the previous afternoon, which meant that eight or nine people had already read it and said nothing about it, which was the single most alarming fact Marek Vale learned about it.

He took it off the spike and read it standing up, because the outer office had a bench and the bench was the sort of thing a man sits on when he has decided what he thinks.

*To the Weather Office. — A review is called under the fourth section, which is an ordinary power and has been used eleven times in the history of this Council, none of them lately. The review is to sit at the Council chamber, basin house, second floor, on the days set out below. The Chair is the Director. Citations are as follows.*

And then eight names, in the order a clerk puts names in when a clerk has been given eight names.

1. **C. Rook, Director.** 2. **M. Vale, reader.** 3. **C. Malle, Deputy for Field Forecasts.** 4. **L. Venn, Deputy for Records and Public Notice.** 5. **A. Sarr, chair, Hearthward rain-garden assembly.** 6. **T. Iles, entering clerk, ward ledger bench.** 7. **I. Sorn, route officer, Kestrel Line.** 8. **K. Bray, clerk, Hearthward rain-garden assembly.**

He was the second name on it.

He read the whole thing again, standing up, with the light off the frames and the room beginning to fill behind him.

And then he read it a third time, which is when he found it, and what he found was that the word *inquiry* was not in it anywhere, and neither was the word *claim*, and neither was the word *Bellweather* in a sentence with *thirtieth* in it, and that after a month of people being extremely careful to say the word *method* when they meant the word *claim* he had finally been handed a piece of paper that was careful in exactly the same way.

The terms of reference were on the back, in the same hand, in the flat drafting that this Council used for anything it intended to hold somebody to.

*To establish whether the effect of a certified or authorised claim on the preparations of the districts affected can be demonstrated from the record; and if it cannot, to report that it cannot.*

He read that sentence eleven times, standing at the spike, and a man came in behind him and said good morning and went upstairs and did not look at him, which was generous, and he was grateful for it in a way he could not have said out loud.

The second half of the sentence was the one that did the work. He could tell that the moment he had finished reading it, and he could not for about nine minutes tell anybody else, and by the time he could it did not matter, because by then three other people had seen it.

---

He went down to the shop at ten past nine, because the shop was cold and there was work in it, and because he needed forty minutes of something with his hands in it before he said anything to anybody. The work coat was on a hook by the bench with a cardboard tube in the pocket, and the inside of the left cuff had two readings and a filing mark in it in three blocks of pencil, and the third block was full, and he did not go to write in it, and the not-going was the only part of the business he was still any good at. He did the aneroid again, the one he had done on the twenty-third, because it had come back with the same fault and a note saying *it has done it again*, and a thing that comes back is a thing you did not do properly.

Halfway through it he stopped with the reamer in his hand and put his hand up to his left ear the way a man does about four times a day. The fine end was still gone, about a third of it, and it had been four days now, and the ringing on that side had gone from being the loudest thing in his head to being the thing he checked for, which is worse, because it means it has become furniture.

He moved the bench lamp to his right, which he now did without thinking about it, and finished the aneroid, and it was a good job, and there is no instrument in this city that will ever record that it was a good job.

---

The first session of the review was at eleven, and it took four minutes of anybody's time.

Sabin Orle was forty-four and had been in the Council's technical cell for eleven years and had come up to the Weatherglass with a folder and a chair and a great many sheets, and she had driven up on a Sunday evening coach in the rain on the seventeenth, to hear a district refuse something, and she had lost nothing by that and had said so.

"Right," she said, at about two minutes to eleven. "We are going to do this in the ordinary order and the ordinary order starts with the Chair's interest, and then the terms, and then the citations, and then the evidence, and I am going to read every one of those out loud because a thing that is not read out loud in a room is a thing that four people remember differently."

"Sabin," said Rook, "I have said it in writing and you have it in the folder."

"You have said it in writing and it is in the folder and the folder is not a witness," said Sabin Orle. "Clerk."

A clerk of the Council's own — not the ward ledger bench, a woman of about sixty called Mrs Ireson who had been taking minutes in that chamber since before the bell on the ridge was the bell on the ridge — got a sheet up in front of her.

Cassian Rook put both hands on the table. He had a low-ward district's way of standing with his weight slightly forward, and he did not stand up, because there was nowhere to stand up to.

"Interest," he said. "I put the Bellweather notice up on the twenty-seventh with my approval stamp in the corner, and I sent down the authorization of the twenty-seventh on the authority of which the claim of the twenty-ninth was entered, and that authorization was mine, and the claim was entered under it, and the claim held, and the claim is one of the things this review is looking at.

"I am also the man who asked for this review, in a memo of the second, which is in the folder, and I wrote in that memo that the Council's spring report on the method requires a section on what a claim costs, and that this office is not able to write that section, and that a body which cannot write the section should ask a room to find out.

"Those are my interests. I chair. I do not sit as a witness. I will sign the minutes. If anybody wants to move that I should not chair, they have until Friday, and I would take it seriously, and I would not be offended, and I would probably grant it."

Nobody moved it, and not from cowardice: Rook had put a stamp on a notice that had been right, the corridor that ran a claim and held it to the hour was intact, and the only injury in three weeks in a basin of a hundred and forty thousand people was a mooring hand's wrist. Every one of those facts was a fact in his favour and none of them was one he had manufactured.

Mrs Ireson read it back, and it went into the minutes with the time against it, and Marek read his own copy on the Wednesday morning and the time was 11:04. The Director of the Veyran Weather Office had declared his own interest four minutes after a review began and had then spent the rest of the session being scrupulous, and there is not one dishonest sentence in that review from beginning to end, and by the end of it Marek Vale understood that he was more frightened of Cassian Rook than he had been of him a month before, and did not sleep well on the Wednesday night for it.

---

Anwen Sarr's clause four was the first item on the agenda and the agenda was posted in the outer office on the Tuesday afternoon with the summons on the spike beside it, and Marek read it standing up for the second time in two days and then went and did the only thing that was available to him to do, which was to go and ask the eight cited people what each of them was going to be asked, one at a time, on their own ground, without telling any of them what the others had said.

That is a real method. It is not a method anybody teaches. It takes a full day and a half and it comes out as eight separate conversations in which you are not a listener, because you cannot be a listener, because the moment you are a listener you have told them what somebody else said and the eight of them are now in a room with each other and the whole exercise is worth nothing.

The second name on the list was his own, and a man cannot put a question to himself, so there were seven of them and he got six, and he saw them standing up, and he did not sit down in one of them.

---

**One. The Director.**

He went to him first, which he had not wanted to do, because a signal was the one thing in this that he could not buy back afterwards.

The Director's outer office was as bare as a stage and Rook was in the middle of it with two sheets, and he gave Marek eleven minutes and did not once ask him a question about the claim.

"Two things," said Rook, when Marek had asked what he would be asked. "You will not be asked about the thirtieth, because the thirtieth is not what this review is about and I have said so in the terms and I will hold that line myself.

"And when you are asked, you will be asked what a claim displaces, in general, and you will answer it in general, and you will be tempted — I have watched you do it twice, in this building — to answer it in the specific. Do not. Not because the specific is wrong. Because it is unprovable, and in about forty minutes somebody who is very good at their job will put the unprovable next to the provable, and the provable will win, because it is a document. And it will not win because it is true."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Where does the plate come from, Mr Vale?"

He had the answer ready before the question was finished, which was the first time in four weeks he had been ahead of himself in a room, and he recognised the sensation and did not enjoy it.

"A plate with no chain behind it in my coat," he said. "You knew that when you sent for me and told me to bring the order and not the evidence. I have not got a chain and I have not manufactured one, and if anybody in that room asks me where the plate came from, the answer is a glassblower's shop at Kestrel Yards, and I will say so in those words if I am asked in front of a clerk."

"Yes," said Marek.

---

**Three. The Deputy for Field Forecasts, who was not in.**

Corrin Malle's office door was shut and the outer office said, without any particular interest, that the Deputy was in the technical cell and would be back at about two.

---

**Four. The Deputy for Records and Public Notice.**

Marek went and did the records counter and Lior Venn in about nine minutes, because Venn was at the counter on a Wednesday, and had been at that counter on every working day for eleven years, and would be at it as long as he was at it.

"I am going to be asked whether a reclassification is a record," said Marek, "or something about the sixth of the month and the classification column, or the ninth ward's glass. Which."

"That is a stupid question to ask a man who is not a lawyer," said Lior Venn, without looking up, "and you have not asked it. You have asked a better one, which is whether the thirtieth's reclassification was correctly made. It was. It is in the Records day book, countersigned at ten minutes past nine on the third, and the violet sheet went out with the district returns, and there is no version of the question that gets an answer out of me that is not already in a drawer in this building."

"Good," said Marek.

"You are not grateful. You are relieved, and I have spent eleven years telling the two apart, and relief is the worse of the two." He put the pen down. "Mr Vale. One observation, and I am making it because it will cost you nothing to have heard it now. You will be tempted, in that room, to distinguish between a thing that was *classified* and a thing that was *hidden*. Do not. They are the same act. The only difference is which drawer it went in, and I have the keys to four of them."

"Yes," said Marek Vale.

"Go and ask Venn about the thirtieth," said Venn, and went back to the counter.

---

**Six. The entering clerk.**

Tovan Iles was at a copy desk in the west end of the records room and it was half past one in the afternoon and the west end of the records room is a bad place to be sent to by anybody with a request.

Marek had not spoken to her since the third of the month. That was thirty-one days, and he had gone down past the ward ledger bench every working morning for thirty-one days, and she had been at the copy desk every working afternoon for thirty-one days, and the two of them had been about thirty feet apart in a building for a month and had not managed it once, and neither of them had any idea what the other one was doing, which is the ordinary condition of two people in the same large building.

He stood at the end of her desk for perhaps a minute before he spoke, which he noticed, and which he would think about later.

"Miss Iles."

"Marek." She did not look up. She had a rule about the desks and the rule was that the person at the desk does not stop. "Four hundred and six. I've done six hundred and ten. If you're here about a form, the form counter is the other end."

"I'm not here about a form."

"Then you're here about something you think I can help with, and I can't, and I'm going to keep writing for about four more minutes and then I'll be at the end of a sheet and I will give you a minute." She dipped the pen. "You look terrible, by the way. You look like a man who's been waiting for something."

"I've been."

"Everybody in this building has," said Tovan Iles. "That's not a complaint, it's a job description. The sheet's the only thing anybody in this building is actually paid to do and about a third of them do it."

He waited the four minutes. It was the four minutes and he could not have told anybody afterwards what he thought about for any of them.

Then she squared the stack, put the pen in the tray, and said: "Right. You've come about the review, and you want to know what I'm going to be asked, and the answer is *nothing*, and I want you to hear that from me rather than find it out on Wednesday, because it is a thing I would like one person in this building to be told by somebody other than me."

He waited.

"I am not on a panel," said Tovan Iles. "I have no standing, I have no vote, and I have not been given anything. When the list went up on the spike yesterday there were eight names on it and one of them was mine and the seven others have all had it in their in-trays since Tuesday afternoon, and I had it at eleven o'clock this morning because Pet the runner came up with a copy, because that is still how this building tells a copy clerk that a thing exists."

She said it entirely flatly. She was not asking for anything. That was the thing about her that Marek had never been able to get used to and had stopped trying to: **she was not asking.**

"You entered it," he said.

"I entered it in one hand without lifting the pen, and I read it back, and it is correct, and it is in the record, and there is nothing wrong with it anywhere, and there has never been anything wrong with it anywhere." She turned the chair about forty degrees, which was as close as she got. "That is why I have nothing to be asked. The only thing anybody could ask me is whether I wrote a good entry, and I did, and it isn't a question anybody asks, because the answer to it is never interesting to anybody but me."

"Tova—"

"They will ask you where the plate came from," she said, "and you will answer it, and I will be two rows back copying the answer, and that is what I am for. Four hundred and six a day, and about a third of them are somebody's answer to something, and I do not know what any of them were about, and that is the correct arrangement and I would fight anybody who tried to improve it."

She picked the pen back up.

"You did not ask me for anything, which I noticed," said Tovan Iles. "You did not ask me for anything, and you have been going down past that bench every morning for a month, and this is the first time one of us has said hello, and I want you to know that I have noticed both of those things and I am not going to do anything about either."

"Right," said Marek Vale.

"Send it in," she said, "and tell the ward's head's lamp is out, and tell them the glass-tender's examination is in the spring and I will not be sitting it, and you knew that a month ago."

---

**Seven. The yard.**

Ilva Sorn came down to the Weatherglass on the Wednesday afternoon because the review was going to take the yard's file and a route officer does not send a night watchwoman with a column and a captain's chalk.

"It is a review of the method," she said. "It is not a review of the claim, which is *yours*, and it is not a review of the classification, which is Malle's. Read the terms again. It says the effect of a claim on the preparations of the districts affected. The Line has no preparations. The Line has hours."

"Then here is the shape of what is coming," said Ilva Sorn. "On Friday the yard's file goes in. I have been putting it together since Monday. There is a third column on it and there is a proviso in my hand at the top of it and I am not going to be able to read the proviso out in a room without somebody stopping me, and if somebody stops me I am going to be angry, and I would like you to be in the room when it happens so that I can be angry at you instead."

"They won't stop you."

"They will stop me at the first line," said Ilva Sorn, "and they will be right to, and I am going to say the whole thing and I am going to say it in the words I wrote it in."

---

**Eight. The clerk who wrote it down.**

"Mr Vale. If the assessor is going to put her two sentences side by side, and one of them is a sentence I wrote down on the eighteenth from her dictation, and the other is a sentence I wrote down this month at her dictation, and they do not agree—"

"Then they do not agree," said Marek Vale, "and both of them are in your hand, and you will be the only person in the room who can say that without anybody believing you."

"Yes," said Kell Bray. "That is what I wanted to hear and I have wanted to hear it since Monday."

---

**Five. The assembly.**

Anwen Sarr was the last of the eight and he went to Hearthward on the Wednesday evening, on foot as far as the tram, and she was in the upper assembly rooms above a market hall with boards across the doors and a season's seed stock inside them, and the market had not been open since the eleventh of the month and would not be open for another three weeks.

She had the summons on the table and the agenda beside it and a cup of tea she had not drunk.

"You have come to tell me what I am going to be asked," she said.

"I've come to tell you that you are second item on the agenda on Thursday and that Ferren is on the list of people who have been asked to attend and does not know it yet, and that you should tell him yourself and not let him be fetched."

"Which is the second of two things and you have got about nine minutes and I have got nine minutes, so put the first one."

Marek put the first one down.

He told her the terms of reference, exactly as they were printed, in the flat order, without any opinion in it. And when he came to the second half of it he read it out twice, because he had worked out by then that it was the second half that mattered, and he wanted her to have it in her ear in the shape it was actually in rather than the shape he had been carrying it in for a day and a half.

*To establish whether the effect of a certified or authorised claim on the preparations of the districts affected can be demonstrated from the record; and if it cannot, to report that it cannot.*

Anwen Sarr listened to it with both hands round a cup she had not drunk from, and did not interrupt, which was the first thing about her that had ever frightened him in a room and it was on the ninth of the month at ten in the morning and there had been a hundred people behind him.

"Second half," she said.

"Second half."

"That is a sentence about my clause four."

"It is a sentence about your clause four," said Marek Vale.

"It is a sentence about my clause four, drafted in the Council's hand, by somebody who has read my clause four, and put into a set of terms of reference by a room, and the sentence does not say what it is for." She put the cup down. "Say the rest of it. You have not said the rest of it and you have been here four minutes."

"The review has been called because the Council's spring report on the method needs a section on what a claim costs, and this office cannot write that section, and so this office has asked a room to find out. That is in a memo of the second day, in the folder, and the Director's own interest says so. It is not an attack on anybody. Nobody has called this as an attack. It has been called because the arithmetic of a report got to a line that cannot be written, and the only instrument that writes lines is a body sitting in a room for five days."

"That is worse," said Anwen Sarr.

"Yes."

"It is much worse, and I know it, and I am not being comforted, and if you had come in and told me it was an attack I would have had something to be angry at by Friday." She turned round and looked at a market hall with boards across the doors. "Do you know what the second half of that sentence does to me? It is the first sentence anybody in this basin has written down that agrees with me, and it agrees with me *on the record*, and it does it by conceding the entire argument, and it costs that Council nothing, and I am going to be obliged to be grateful for the destruction of everything I asked for."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Do not be smug. You will be at that desk on Thursday and you will be worse at it than I will, and I have been at that desk for six years and have never once got what I wanted, so I am speaking from enormous professional strength. Ferren will be here at nine. You will sit at the back. You will answer the question and not the question under the question, because I have watched you do the other thing and I have not forgiven it and I am not going to today."

"Yes," said Marek Vale.

"And one more thing, and then I am going to make you go away so that I can think about this for two hours." She came back to the table. "The two districts that prepared are not on that list. Not you, not me, not the Director, not the yard, not a single one of the places that lost something. Eight names, and every one of them is a person who made a record, and the two that lost it are not in the room."

"I know."

"Then get it in," said Anwen Sarr. "I will not ask you for it twice. If that is not got into the terms before Thursday morning I will find a way of getting it into them myself, and I will do it in a way that costs somebody their afternoon, and I have been saving the afternoon for six years."

---

He got it in for about nine minutes on the Thursday morning and it did not go in, and Malle got twenty minutes of the same and it did not go in either, and by twenty past ten on the Thursday of the thirty-fifth day the review was sitting with eight citations on a list and the two districts that had actually prepared sitting in a corridor outside the chamber with no right to be let in, and that was the morning, and nobody in the corridor said anything about it, because the two of them were a Council assessor on her way to Low Spindle on her own business and the second name on a list of eight, and neither of them was on it as the thing that had lost something.

Aldis Prynne was not on the list either.

Prynne was the reader on the long watch, and the long watch had gone up on the twenty-sixth with six days on it and a name on the reader's line, and Aldis Prynne had read the order of the movements on the ninth of the month in a certified run and got it right, and the only reason he had got it right was that the Deputy for Field Forecasts had put the whole of the ninth of the month into three places in this building on purpose so that a reader could work from it.

And the review was going to sit for five days in a room that had the order of the movements on the ninth of the month at the top of its file in three places, and it was going to decide what a claim costs, and it was going to do it without asking the one other man in the basin who had read that order correctly and been paid to.

Marek Vale stood in a corridor outside the Council chamber on the Thursday morning of the thirty-fifth day of a month in which nothing whatever was happening in the sky, and understood, entirely and for the first time, that the trap he was in had not been built by anybody.

This one had not been built at all. It had been arrived at, by arithmetic, by nine good people in a room over about six weeks, out of a genuine and entirely reasonable desire to put a section in a report, and it had caught him anyway, and it would go on catching people who had not been in the building for eleven years, for four years, or ever, because there was nothing in it that required a villain and nothing in it that required anybody to want him caught.

He had been caught in daylight, again, by a man who had not built it, by a method that had no author he could find, on the first morning of the thirty-fifth day, eleven minutes before Anwen Sarr's clause four came to the table in a chamber with the windows shut.
