# Chapter 42 — A Deputy's Answer

Corrin Malle got to the basin house at about half past seven on the morning of the fortieth day, which was ninety minutes before anybody wanted him, and shut the door of his own office, and did not open it for two hours and ten minutes.

He had come up on the Sunday coach with a folder. The folder had been in his hands on the platform at the bottom of the hill and it had been in his hands on the coach and it had been in his hands on the stairs, and it was empty, and he had known it was empty since the Friday, and he had carried it anyway, because a man who turns up at an inquiry with nothing in his hands is a man who has decided to have nothing, and there is a difference between the two and it is worth about forty minutes of a room's opinion of you.

He put the empty folder on the desk and looked at it for a while.

Then he did the thing he does at about eight o'clock, which is to go and get the rosters, and there were no rosters, and that was the first thing about the morning that he had not arranged for.

---

Here is what Corrin Malle had, and he has never in his life put it in a list, and it took him about two hours in a shut room to find out that it was a list.

He was fifty-four, with two children, one of them at the technical cell's own school on a grant and one of them eleven, who had asked him in the spring why he was never home at six and been told *because there is a thing at four o'clock*, and had said *what thing*, and had been told *the weather*, and had said *you do the weather?* and gone back to her plate. He had thought about that exchange for four months, which was longer than he had thought about the thirtieth.

He had been in the building for thirty years and a Deputy for sixteen of them. He had watched four hundred and forty people get out of a ward's low ground on a night in the second week of the month because he had put a reader on a watch, and he had watched the same ward lose a season of seed stock in the second week of the month because he had put a roof over a market, and both were correct and both were in a folder in a different building.

And three weeks ago, on the Sunday evening before the Council sat, he had got on a coach out of hours with a sandwich he had not been asked for, and had gone up a hill, and had sat at the back of a chamber, and had watched a method he staffs succeed, and had been the only person in that room who had not stood up.

He had a rule about that and the rule was *a Deputy who cannot explain a thing does not leave the effect sitting on the ledger unattributed*, and the rule is in the Charter, Section 9, and it has been in the Charter longer than he has, and he had never once in thirty years had to read it, because in thirty years he had never had an effect he could not account for.

And then he had, on the third day of the month, at about half past eight in the morning, with a pen about an inch above a piece of paper, put the word *maintenance* next to a wall that had been gravel at the cheek since the spring.

He had reached for a rule.

That was the sentence, and it took him about ninety minutes to get to it and he never said it out loud to anybody, and it is the sentence that explains the rest of his life: **the thing he reached for was a rule, and the rule worked.**

Not a person. Not a lie. Not a favour. A rule, in a book, written by somebody else about a different century, which said that if you cannot account for an effect you classify it to the nearest true description; and the nearest true description of a wall that has been gravel at the cheek since the spring and has been on a deferred schedule for two funding years is a wall that was overdue.

The rule is correct. The rule is not corrupt. The rule has kept nine of these alive and Malle has said so in a room on the Tuesday and meant it, and the rule did exactly what it is for, and it took a thing out of the record that a person had put in the record, and nobody had to decide anything, and that is why it worked, and it worked on him, and it worked inside an hour of him working.

He sat in the shut room for a long time after that.

Because here was the thing he had got wrong, and he had got it wrong in about four seconds and had not noticed for five weeks, and he had noticed it in the corridor outside the review's chamber on the Friday morning when a route officer published a wrist.

He had believed — and he had gone on believing it, and he had said out loud to a copy clerk that he had believed it — that a classification is a way of not having to put your name on something.

And a classification is not a name.

If a Deputy had put his own name on the thirtieth's item (a), with the gravel and the two funding years and the bell at half past three, then on the twenty-eighth of the following month somebody could have come into a room and said, *Deputy Malle, on the thirtieth, at half past three in the morning, at a structure you knew was overdue — what did you do?* And he would have had an answer, because it would have been a question about a man.

He had not put his name on it. He had put a **word** on it, and a category is not a person and cannot be shouted at, and a Deputy cannot be made answerable for a category because a category is not him. The wall is not Malle. *Maintenance failure* is not Malle. Malle put a word where a name would have gone, and the word held, and no clerk in this building could pull it out and no district in this basin could pull it out.

And the reason that was wrong, and the reason nobody in the room was going to tell him, and the reason he had noticed it in a corridor on the Thursday was that **a word is a better hiding place than a name**, and everybody in this basin believed the opposite, and everybody in this basin was wrong about that, and the belief is not stupid, and the belief is eleven years old in this building and is the Charter's own.

*That the residual exposure of a prepared district is a single option and is not a compensable loss.* He had said that out loud. Sabin Orle had minuted it. Rook had asked for it and had wanted it minuted **on a day when he was the one making the claim**, and it was the single most defensible sentence any of them had produced in three weeks, and it was the sentence that had let all of them sleep.

Malle got up at about ten and put his coat on and stood in front of the glass in the door, which shows the corridor and not the room, and said out loud, in an empty office, to nobody:

"That is not a name."

And then he said, in the same voice, about nine seconds later: "That is not a name either, you know. That is a category as well. You have just found that out about your own sentence."

---

He went in at about ten past one, and he had brought nothing, and he said so before anybody asked.

"I have no paper," said Corrin Malle. "I have no file. I have no witness. I have been in this building for thirty years and I have a memory and I have four certified trial claims in a folder in a locked drawer two floors from here and none of them is about this.

"Whatever you want me to say, I am going to say from memory and you are going to weigh it however you like, and I am telling you that at the top so that nobody spends the afternoon deciding how much of it to believe."

"Deputy," said Sabin Orle, "sit down. Nobody has asked you anything yet."

"Then I have pre-empted you, which is a thing I have done twice in five weeks and both times I was right and one time it cost a district a clerk's post." He put his hands flat on the table. "Ask."

---

They asked it in the plainest possible form, and the reason it was in the plainest possible form is that Sabin Orle had drafted it herself, on the Saturday, and had shown it to no one, and had spent the whole of the Sunday working out how to ask a Deputy for Field Forecasts a question whose honest answer would damage him and whose dishonest answer would damage the review.

"Deputy. Item two. The entry for the thirtieth says, of the Lower Sill headwall, *maintenance failure*. That entry was made on the third day of the month and it was countersigned in the Records day book at ten minutes past nine that morning and entered in the field day book at the ward ledger bench at eleven, and the violet sheet went out with the district returns.

"Two questions. **First: why does the entry say maintenance failure?**

"And I want the answer to be in your own words and I want it to be about the wall."

Malle did not look at anybody.

"It is a true statement about the culvert," he said. "The structure was a Bellweather-era concrete headwall, shored in the season ending nine years ago and again six years ago, with a foot of its west cheek gone to gravel above the water line, reported to the water office in the spring and carried on a deferred maintenance schedule for two funding years without execution. The failure at about half past three on the thirtieth is a maintenance failure. No weather event on the thirtieth bears on the condition of that structure, and I can put a hundred and eighty people in the technical cell in front of that and every one of them will agree with me, and about forty of them will say it more forcefully than I have.

"And it is not a true statement about the thirtieth. Both of those are mine. I have said both of them out loud to the only person in this building who writes things down for a living, on the third of the month, at about half past eight in the morning, and she will tell you she was told and she is correct, and I did not tell her to be careful, because I do not tell people to be careful about things I have said in a corridor."

"Minuted," said Mrs Ireson.

"**And the second question**," said Sabin Orle, and she leaned forward about four inches, which is the whole of her physical range. "Deputy, this has not been asked you before and I want to tell you that before I ask it, and I want to tell you that I have spent two days deciding how to ask it and I have asked two members of this Council's own staff how to ask it and both of them told me I was making a fool of myself, and I am going to do it anyway because the review's terms of reference require a finding and the finding turns on this and I am not going to let a finding be thin.

"**What did the classification do?**"

---

Corrin Malle, who had been Deputy for Field Forecasts for sixteen years and had been right about four out of five things every day for thirty years, sat at a table in the Council chamber and did not answer for about nine seconds.

And then he said, "Nobody has ever asked me that."

"No," said Sabin Orle. "I am aware. That is the second half of the question and the reason I am asking it in public."

"Then I am going to have to say two things and the first of them is going to sound like an evasion and it is not one." He took his hand off the table and put it back. "I do not know.

"Not *cannot find out*. Do not know. I have not been asked, and I have had four days, and I have gone back through every form that went out of this building that week, and there is a classification column in the field day book and it is filled in by Records and not by the field, and there is no second column, and there is no line under it, and the day sheet for the thirtieth says *maintenance, deferred* and does not say anything else about item (a) than that, and I have looked, and there is no second thing.

"And that is not a gap in a record. That is a form. The form has items for damage and an item for casualties and an item for claims, and item (a) went in the damage box because item (a) was in the damage box, and there is no box anywhere in this building for what a classification does. There is not one. I looked for one this week and I did not find it and I have been in this building for thirty years."

The room was completely still.

"But the Chair would not accept that," said Malle, "and neither would I, and I am going to answer the question anyway, because the question was asked and because a Deputy who cannot explain a thing does not leave it sitting there, and I said that sentence out loud on the third of the month to a copy clerk, and I am not going to unsay it now that it is inconvenient.

"What a classification does is remove a thing from the categories that can be complained about.

"That is the whole of it. That is what it *is*. A classification does not make a thing untrue and it does not make a thing secret — there is no such thing as a secret classification in this building, I can walk you to the violet sheet, you can read it, it is in the technical cell's file, the *Records* day book is not a locked room, the classification itself is public and always has been.

"It removes a thing from the categories that can be complained about. A maintenance failure is damage, and damage is an item, and an item is a thing a district can complain about, because a district can complain about damage to its own ground, and a district has been able to do it for thirty years. A thing that is not damage is not an item, and the moment it is not an item it is out of the reach of every district in this basin — because to point at a thing you have to be able to say what it is, and if you cannot say what it is then you have not got a complaint, you have got a feeling.

"And that is the design. That is *the design*, and it is older than me, and I did not draw it, and I am not the author of it, and I want to say that last part clearly because the Chair is going to ask me who did, and I have been asked by my own staff this week and I have told them the truth, which is that I cannot find the author and neither can anybody else I have asked, and I have been in this building for thirty years."

He reads nothing. He has never read a sequence in his life, and there is no instrument in this basin he would need to; his whole trade is the plate in a frame and a lamp behind it, and he does it in his own head about four times a year and badly.

"That is what a classification does. It moves a thing out of the reach of complaint, and it does it without anybody deciding anything, and the reason it does it without anybody deciding anything is that the rule says *classify it to the nearest true description* and every description in the box is a true description of something, and mine was, and mine is the nearest one I could find at eight o'clock in the morning with a pen in my hand and a form in front of me.

"And I want to say the last of it and then I will sit down, because I have thought about it for five weeks and this is the only sentence I have."

---

"You put a word on it," said Marek Vale, from the back of the bench, and did not mean to speak, and everybody in the room heard it.

"Deputy," said Sabin Orle, "the witness has not been asked."

"I know," said Marek. "I am sorry."

"No, you are not," said Corrin Malle, without any heat at all, and did not look round. "That is all right. You are not sorry and I would rather have the true version. Go on, Mr Vale. I have been waiting five weeks for somebody in this building to say it and I would rather have it said in the open than have it in a corridor for the rest of my life."

Marek looked at the table.

"You thought putting a word on it was the same as putting your name on it."

"It is not the same," said Corrin Malle.

"No," said Marek Vale. "It is not. It is better than that, and that is what I have been not understanding for five weeks. A name can be answered. A word cannot."

---

Nobody said anything, and Mrs Ireson wrote it down, and then Corrin Malle did the thing that the minutes record as item two, fourth paragraph, and which is the only thing he volunteered.

"There is one more thing," he said, "and I am volunteering it, and no person in this room has asked me for it, and I would be obliged if the Chair would rule that it is a statement and not evidence, because it is a statement and I have thought about it for a week and I have not been able to make it into anything else.

"**This office has never, in the history of the building, put a line in a return for a thing that was not damaged.**"

He put both hands flat on the table.

"Not once. Not in my thirty years and not before them, so far as I know, and I have been back through the water office's front-return tables because they are on a wall in this building and anybody may read them, and they go back a long way, and there is not one item in any of them for a repair that was not done.

"And I do not know whether that is a rule or a shape.

"I want to be very exact about that, because it is the difference between an institution and a machine. A rule is written down by somebody, on a day, and you can find the person who wrote it and you can complain to the seat that wrote it. A shape is what is left after forty years of every individual doing the entirely reasonable thing. And I have been in this building for thirty years and I have never once seen anybody decide it, and I have asked four people in the last nine days and two of them did not know I was asking, which I found out afterwards, which is how I know they are telling the truth.

"So I cannot tell this room whether the reason there is no line in a return for a thing that was not done is because somebody decided there should not be, or because a couple of centuries of clerks filling in boxes have never had a reason to write one.

"And I want it in the minutes that a Deputy for Field Forecasts does not know, and that he has looked, and that he cannot find the author, and that this is not because anybody has concealed it. Nobody has concealed anything. I have looked in four drawers this week. It is simply not written anywhere, and there is a whole generation of us who have all behaved the same way about it, and I have been one of them for thirty years, and I would like the record to say that, because in about four years somebody is going to ask, and the honest answer is going to be *nobody decided it, and that is worse*."

---

"Minuted," said Mrs Ireson, and then said, "Deputy, I am obliged to ask whether you are aware of what you have just said about the institution."

"I am aware of what I have said," said Corrin Malle. "I would like the Chair to rule that I have said it on the record and that I have not withdrawn it, and I would like the Chair to notice that it is a statement and not an objection, and I would like that ruled too, because the assessor ruled the same way for Mr Vale on the nineteenth and I am not going to have it be said that I have come in here and made an argument. I have made a statement. I have been making statements for five weeks. That is what I do."

Then the Chair, who had not spoken in an hour and forty minutes, said:

"Deputy, the review has made no finding against you and is going to make none, and I am going to tell you that now, at the fourth paragraph, before the fifth, because I think you have just made yourself the most useful witness in this review and I would like you to be able to hear that while you are still standing up.

"You are not reclassified. You are not censured. You are not moved. You are not before any disciplinary body, there is no form open against you, and this office has recommended nothing against you and is not going to, and I am the Chair and I am telling you so in a room with a clerk in it before the afternoon session, so that it is not a rumour in a corridor in about a month."

"Thank you, sir," said Corrin Malle.

"That was the easy half," said Cassian Rook. "Now I am going to say the other half and I am going to say it in the Chair's voice and not as a friend, and I would like Mr Vale and Ms Sarr and the route officer and the clerk to hear it, because it is the reason the review is going to recommend a column and nothing else.

"What you have just said, Deputy, is a finding. It is a correct finding and it is the most serious thing anybody has said in this review and it has been said by the officer of this Office in the witness chair, in terms that nobody can argue with, and I am not going to pretend that it is not a finding, and I am also not going to pretend that it changes anything, and I would like the room to understand that those two sentences are not in tension. They are the entire architecture of this Council.

"If there is no line in a return for a thing that was not done, then a thing that was not done has nowhere to go, and a thing that has nowhere to go cannot be a claim on anybody, and a thing that cannot be a claim on anybody is not a cost. And that is not a decision anybody took. It is forty years of a form, and it is the reason a district in this basin with two hundred and twenty people on its terraces can be hit on the seventh and be given a season back, and be hit again in a different way, and be given nothing, and be right to be given nothing in a way that will hold up in front of a court in about four years.

"**And there is no version of this that gets fixed by a Council writing a rule that says *put a line in for a thing that was not done*, because the moment this Council writes that rule it has to answer the question of what a thing that was not done is worth, and who wrote it, and how it is compensated, and whether it is a preparation or a plan — and if it is a plan then it was never a preparation and it was always a choice, and if it is a choice then somebody chose it, and somebody in this basin chose it, and the review's terms of reference were to establish whether that somebody could be shown.

"And they cannot. And you have just told the room why they cannot, and you have told the room that you cannot find the author, and that nobody decided it.

"So the review is going to say the second half of the terms of reference in the words of the terms, and it is going to recommend that a claim's cost be published **as what the claim closed**, and it is going to recommend nothing else, because anything else it recommended would be a rule, and there is no author for any of the rules we are standing on and I am not going to start."

---

Nothing happened for a minute, and then Ilva Sorn said, from the table, "Director, does the Line get to be in the second half of that?"

"No, Director. The second half is that a claim's cost may not name the person who chose it."

"Then that is the only part of it that is worth doing and it is worth doing for a reason that is not on this Council's list at all, and I will say it in a sentence and then I will sit down, because I have got a yard.

"Under it, a yard cannot be put in a claim that a captain has made a bad call about. Under it, a yard cannot be bought out of a market it has chosen. Under it, a **person** cannot be priced, and a person is the only thing in this whole business that anybody in this room would actually want protected, and I have spent nineteen years trying to write four lines in chalk that protected eleven crews' worth of families, and I have never once got a clause that did it.

"And the reason I have never got it is that a clause has to name a thing it forbids, and there is nothing here to name, and the only way I have ever found to protect anything at all is to print it, and printing a cost names the person who caused it the moment there is a person to name.

"So the column prints the cost and does not name the man, and that is the first rule in this basin that protects a person, and it is doing it by refusing to publish, and I would like somebody to explain to me how that is a defeat."

Nobody explained it to her, and she sat down, and it is in the minutes.

Then the assessor ruled, and her ruling was in the ordinary flat voice, and it went on the record in the nineteen words the review is remembered for, and they are the only finding, and the rest of the review is the argument about them:

*That the effect of a claim on a district's preparations cannot be shown from the record, and so reported.*

And that is the whole of the finding, and it is the sentence the terms of reference were written around, and it has been true since a Deputy put a word on a thing on the third day of the month instead of his own name, and five days of evidence in a room had established it, and the only person in this basin who ever got it out of anybody was a district clerk in Ferrygate, who got it out of the assessor on the Sunday morning in ninety seconds by answering a question about a sheet of paper that had never been sent anywhere.

---

The session broke at about ten past three for the assessor to take the room and there were four people in the corridor in about four minutes.

Malle got about nine feet down the corridor and stopped, and put his hand on the wall, which he had not done in front of a person in thirty years.

The thing that had happened in that room was not that he had been asked what the classification did. It was that he had answered it out loud, in a room with a clerk in it, and the answer had been true, and it had turned out to be a finding, and he had made himself the most useful witness in a review that recommended nothing against him.

He stood in the corridor and did the arithmetic, quickly, the way a man does arithmetic he has been avoiding.

*He has not been censured. He has not been moved. He is not on a copy desk. The review will recommend nothing against him and has said so in a room with a clerk in it, on the record, from the Chair, before the afternoon session.*

*He put a category on a thing instead of his own name, and the category held, because it was the true nearest description, and a Deputy who cannot account for an effect is entitled to classify it, and the Charter says so in Section 9, and Section 9 is older than he is.*

*And in six days a column will be printed that says what a claim closed and does not say who closed it, and it will exist because he was right on the third of the month, and his name will be on the report, and no person in this basin will ever be able to show that he did anything to anybody.*

He put his hand flat against the wall and said the sentence, out loud, in a corridor, to nobody, in the flattest voice he had:

"That is a good outcome for me."

And then he stood there for another minute and worked out, because he is a competent man who has never once in thirty years been wrong about a procedure, that he was going to have to be able to say that sentence out loud again in about a month, to somebody who cared, and that there was not going to be anybody who cared, and that that was the correct arrangement, and that the correctness of the arrangement was the reason he had done it.

And then he straightened his coat and went back up the hill to file a report on a river water temperature, because that was in the tray, and because he was still the officer of this Office and the weather was still doing something.
