# Chapter 1 — The Shuffled Plate

The lamp over the second bench had been failing since March and Marek had developed a way of working that did not depend on it, which was to sit with his back to the bench and let the first lamp throw his shadow forward across the work. He had never reported the second lamp. Reporting it would have started a paper process, and the paper process would have finished in the spring of some year he did not expect to see.

It was twenty-one forty, and the replacement plate for the Ninth Ward feed had to be on that rig by morning, and it was going to have to be the best piece of glass Marek had cut in two years, and the cracked one was going to have to be rendered first, because a man who throws a plate away unread is a man who has already decided he does not want to know.

The Weatherglass maintenance shop sat under the eastern ridge with its back to the wind and its front to the valley, so that everything the weather had to say arrived at the door before it arrived anywhere else. Cold came up through the floor slabs in the way it always did, patient and slightly damp, and Marek had the paraffin heater going anyway because his hands were the instruments. Behind him the observation floor was dark and emptying, coats and umbrellas and the smell of wet wool going down the stairwell in one long movement, the way a building breathes out at the end of a day.

The aneroid was on bench one, a brass drum the size of a fist with a fatigued spring and a pointer that had gone crooked at the ninth hour. The rain gauge was on bench two, waiting for a float that had been eaten to half its length by something with an acid stomach and good taste for brass. Both were ordinary. Both were the kind of work that existed so that a person who could read a trace in a badly cracked piece of glass did not have to think about it.

The Ninth Ward plate was on bench three under a square of oilcloth, and Marek had put it there at nine o'clock and had not touched it since, because a man who opens a package at nine and looks at it properly at ten is a man who tells himself the ten o'clock version is the first honest one.

He pulled the oilcloth off.

The crack ran from the lower left corner up and to the right in a clean diagonal, the kind of crack that comes from heat on one edge and cold on the other, and the plate itself was a hand's width of thick sequence glass with a ground edge and a serial stamped into the corner in a die that had been worn smooth by forty years of similar stamping. Four thousand four hundred and seventy-one. He knew the run because Pell cut the last tower bundle in a single week four years back, when the Office still ordered glass by the sheet instead of by the year, and the two plates that came out of it had both gone into the Ninth Ward tower: four-four-seven-one into the sequence feed, and four-four-seven-zero into the public frame, which is where a poor ward's warning is supposed to be. That tower had gone unfunded ever since.

He said, out loud, to the bench and to nobody, "Four years on a marginal allowance. You never had a chance."

Then he did what he had done to cracked glass for eleven years, which was to give it a lamp, a wet cell, and a place to be wrong in a way that could be measured.

The rig was his own. A copper frame to hold the plate at a slant, a brass lamp behind it that threw heat through the glass from one side, a wet cell that put a film of moisture on the other, and a ruler laid across the front to give the eye a scale. Cracked glass keeps what sound glass throws away. A plate under strain holds the strain in the fracture itself, and the fracture will render it as long as you keep the heat even and do not touch the frame, and Marek had learned that at nineteen in the rain-maintenance apprenticeships from a glass-tender who could not read a trace at all and who had said, that day, that the crack is where the reading lives, which had sounded like nonsense for a year and then like the only true thing anyone had said to him.

He adjusted the lamp. The film came up on the glass in a slow bloom, and the six traces began to come out of it in the order they always came out, because a sequence is a sequence is a sequence: Gather first, then Weight, then Break, then Route, then Release, and last of all Witness, which is the slowest and the most expensive and the one no instrument in the building could be trusted with on a bad night.

Gather came up out of the film and stood where it should.

Weight started, and where Weight should have continued, Break took over, early, hard, with a shear in it that Marek felt in his teeth before he saw it on the glass. Then Route arrived doubled, a short hook and a long one on top of each other, and Release tried to happen and did not finish, and Witness turned up in the last third where Release belongs, and the whole thing lay there on the frame like a hand of cards put down in the wrong order by somebody who knew the rules and was in a hurry.

His hand was not braced on the frame. The bench came up and met him in the back of the skull, not hard, and the lamp swung, and the film on the glass broke and ran.

He sat on the floor with his back against bench two for a while and let his eyes go. The headache arrived from behind his left eye and settled in, the way it did, and joined the older ache that had been living in the base of his skull since the summer he was twenty-six. He had a bent needle in his left hand. He did not remember picking it up. It had come from the aneroid, and the aneroid was four feet away, and there was no version of the last ninety seconds in which that was possible.

He could not get his eyes down off the room. There was nothing in it to look at. There was a cold shop, a paraffin heater giving up, and a plate on the floor with the film still sliding down it, and he looked at all of it anyway, the way a man looks at a door that has just been locked in a room he is still standing in.

"Right," he said. He got up. He put the plate back under the oilcloth. He washed the frame, dried it, and stood for a moment with both hands on the copper rail while the shop ticked and cooled around him.

The reading was free. That was the thing to lean on, and he leaned on it, because it was the one piece of professional comfort he had. Nothing had been spent. He had looked at something, and looking is free, and the cost of looking was a headache and a bent needle and a bad night, and if he had seen this in the field with a crew depending on it he would have gone back to the instruments and checked the aneroid and the wet cell and the temperature of the lamp and he would have eaten a cold supper and slept and looked again in daylight.

He said it out loud anyway, to the bench, because it is better to hear the sentence.

"Nothing spent. Just looking."

It did not help as much as it should have.

He read it a second time anyway, because one reading is a mood and two readings is a reading. He let the lamp go down and came up and did it cold, with the film left where it had dried, and got the same six movements in the same wrong order, and this time he kept his feet under the bench, which was the whole of the difference between the first hour and the second.

Then he cut the replacement.

There was a sheet of sound sequence glass on the rack behind the door, Office stock, a hand's width thick, and there was a mark on the frame of it that had been there since the day it came and that he had looked at perhaps nine hundred times. He measured nine inches with a folding rule and marked the line in chalk on two faces and held the sheet up to the lamp to check the marks against each other, because a scribe that walks off a line puts a crescent through the work, and a crescent in a tower's feed is a reading that nobody can see and everybody has to act on. He scored it, and broke it out, and ground the edge on the wheel until it was square under a thumbnail and would go into a seat without a shim.

It was the best piece of glass he had cut in two years and it was dull. It was an ordinary repair, it corrected no bias, it cost the ward nothing, and by the time Malle put a knuckle against the copper seat in two days' time and said *plate*, the whole of it would be nine minutes long and nobody would remember that it had ever been in doubt.

He set it on the floor under a second cloth, squared to the edge, out of the way of his own boots, and washed his hands.

He sat down and did the work, because the work was the aneroid and the gauge and Feth Raal's barometer, and because a man with four hours of sleep and a head full of a shuffled sequence is a man who should be doing something with his hands.

The aneroid took an hour. The spring was fatigued and the pointer was bent at the ninth hour, and when he straightened the pointer and set the drum on the test plate and brought the chamber back up, the pointer went true through the first eight hours and then wandered a half-millimetre to the right and stayed there. He tried it twice more. He got the same half-millimetre. He wrote the fault on the tag, which was the correct procedure, and then he did not file the tag, because a tag filed at twenty-three hundred is a tag that somebody reads at eight in the morning and acts on, and a half-millimetre of wander on a four-year-old chamber is not a storm. He put the tag in the drawer with the other tags he had not filed, which was the second drawer from the top and by now the most honest piece of furniture he owned.

The rain gauge was simpler. The float had been gnawed. He found the culprit in the cistern, a small grey slug with a shine to it that suggested opinions, and he put the float back on its pin anyway because the pin was not bent and the damage was cosmetic, and he noted the slug in the maintenance book because the maintenance book is a record of what the instrument has been exposed to and not only of what is wrong with it.

Feth Raal's barometer was on the last stool by the door, wrapped in a shawl, and Feth had left it with the shawl because Feth thought a shawl was a kindness and a barometer was a fragile thing that people brought to her because it was the only instrument in Low Spindle that anybody trusted.

It was a good barometer. It was also, and Marek said so to the empty shop while he worked, forty years old with a face of silvered glass and a dial that had been repainted in the wrong colour in some decade he had not been alive for, and the glass across the face had crazed in a fine web that threw two faint ghost needles off the real one.

He did not reglaze the face. He cleaned the movement, freed the click, oiled the pivot with three drops and not four, and set the dial to true against the Weatherglass's own wall reading. Then he sat with it on his knees.

The ghost needles puzzled him. Two of them, faint, on opposite sides of the real one, and they did not wander the way a bad dial wanders. They kept their distance. In eleven years of reading other people's glass he had learned to distrust an instrument that agreed with him completely, and an instrument that shows you two extra needles at a fixed separation from the true one is telling you that something is layered on top of what you can read, and the layers are not the same size as the weather you are reading.

He would not have been able to explain that to Feth, and he would not have tried. He wrapped it in her shawl and set it on the shelf by the door where she would collect it at four, and wrote on the wrap in grease pencil: *mainspring tired. read it true, not close.*

Then, because the shop was empty and the heater had gone to a sullen glow, and because he had been alone for nine hours with something he could not put down:

"Marek. If you are wrong about this, you will have to say it out loud to somebody. Out loud. To a person. Not to a register."

He said it twice, because once is a thought and twice is a promise he might keep.

The clock in the shop said half past eleven. Four hours of sleep, if he went now. The plate was under the oilcloth and the trace was on the plate and the trace was a fact about the sky three days and a bit out, in a sequence he had no instrument chain for, in a ward whose tower was fed by glass from a bundle that had been cracked in two places in the same four years.

He turned the other thought over, because there was another one under it and it was the one that would keep him awake. The Office had the front at three days and a bit and could not narrow it, and the disagreement between its six best instruments was six hours, and it had been six hours for three days without one sentence written upstairs about why. He knew why now. The instruments timed the movements against one another. If the movements arrive shuffled, then the timings are measuring the wrong thing, and every number on the ridge is a true number about an order that is not the one in the air.

He wrote on the inside of his left cuff, in pencil, small and neat against the lining: *21:40 — N9 feed 4471 — cut 22:05.* Time, plate number, tower. Three facts and no interpretation. Then he wrote the thing that actually mattered, which was the word order, because a trace without an order is a photograph of a room: *G · Wt B · R R · Rel— · Wt. Rel began and did not finish.*

The chart room's register was two floors up and he was not going to it. He had done eleven years of weather work and he had never once written anything into that register that was not backed by an instrument chain, and there was no chain in the building tonight that had produced this, because the aneroids and the wet cells could tell him *how much* and *how fast* and *from which quarter* and none of them could tell him *in what order*, and order was the whole of it.

He put his coat on. In the pocket was the timetable for the yard tram that ran to Kestrel in the morning, and in the cuff was a pencil note, and in his head was a sequence with two men in it who had not agreed to be there.

He went up the stairs past the dark observation floor, and out into the cold, and the front was three days and a bit out and had not yet decided to be a disaster.

Down at the foot of the Weatherglass steps a light was still burning in the barometer stall, which at a quarter to midnight on the fourth day was the most sensible thing Marek had seen for six hours.

Feth Raal was eighty-one and had kept the stall open fifty-four years and had re-glazed her own dials badly in the fifties and would not let anybody tell her so. She was sitting on a stool with a mug and a blanket over her knees and a brass instrument in her lap that was not hers.

"That's your own dial," Marek said. "You've had it back three days."

"I have." She lifted it so he could see the two faint ghost needles standing off the true one on either side. "Now look at that and tell me it's my eyes."

"It's not your eyes. It's a doubled trace, and I don't know what makes it, and I want it on my bench tomorrow."

"You'll have it on your bench tomorrow." She set the dial on the counter and drank her tea. "I had a man in a good coat in here at nine o'clock tonight, asking about narrow forecasts. Not a route, not a passage. A *window*. And a Bellweather one, and he wanted to know if there was a second version of it anywhere, and I told him I am a shopkeeper and not a printing house." She set the mug down. "There's always a second version somewhere, Marek. And it isn't a secret. It's just that nobody in the building is paid to say it out loud, and everybody knows it, and so it gets to be a thing you don't talk about at half past eleven at night to a repairman with a headache."

Marek stood in the wet outside her stall for a moment.

"Nine o'clock," he said. "A good coat."

"Two of them were outside at eight," said Feth Raal, "asking the same thing, and neither of them would come in, because in my line of business the ones who will not come in are the ones who are writing something down."
