# Chapter 32 — Two Columns

The second pump house on the Bellweather reach has a lintel in it that a crane cannot get a pump through, and a warden who knew that rang Kestrel Yards at about ten past midnight on the thirtieth day of the Bellweather autumn, and the yard was awake at one in the morning with a lift working and a rigger standing on wet concrete, and Tarin Senn took the *Wren* down the reach herself because she did not want to send a junior into a reach in a peak and because it was faster.

The *Wren* is a flat-bottomed lighter with a lifting boom on a gantry and eleven people in the Kestrel Line who will go out in it in weather, and the lift on the *Wren* is a different animal from the yards' and slower, and the trip down the reach in the peak rain took fifty minutes and every one of them was made out of nerve rather than out of judgement.

They came in under the freight apron at about ten to one with the peak rain going through it sideways.

---

The freight apron at Bellweather is a hundred and forty yards of concrete at the bottom of a reach, and the Lower Sill culvert goes under it, and the reach's water stands against a concrete headwall at the apron's upstream end that is older than the ward line and older than the tower and is the oldest surviving use of the name in this city, cut into a stone dedication plate on the west cheek, with a date on it that nobody has had to look at since the wall was last shored.

The wall had been shored twice. Once, a long time ago, properly. Once, six years ago, by four men in a hurry, and it is the second shoring that is carrying the first, and everybody in the trade has known that for about four years and nobody has ever written it down, because writing it down would be a report and a report about that wall is a maintenance item and maintenance items have a queue.

Nineteen days ago the item had come off the top of the queue, because a thing that has been funded for two years and deferred for two years was funded, and the timber was banded, and the pump was on a pallet with a Union guarantee on the drum, and a board outside a shed had been repainted with a date on it, and nobody had looked at the date since the autumn.

A municipal apron man called Doak had been on that apron since about five in the afternoon and had been watching the reach come up for nine hours.

"It's holding," he said to Tarin, over the boom, in the rain. "It's holding the way it held at two o'clock. It is not going to hold all night."

"How do you know?"

"Because the water is still on the rise and it has been on the rise for two hours and the wall is doing what it did in the sixties and I have been on this apron in the sixties." He put a hand on the concrete. "There is a place on this wall about a foot above the water that is not concrete any more. It is gravel. It has been gravel since the spring and I have been telling the water office since the spring and there is a queue."

"Right," said Tarin Senn.

They got the pump house's slings and a coil and a spare gland housing off the apron in forty minutes, which is fast for a reach in a peak, and nobody was hurt doing it, and Doak's whole crew was there, and at about ten past two Tarin had the *Wren* out in the middle of the reach with a hundred feet of line on the apron bollard and the boom up.

At about half past three the headwall went.

---

It did not go with a noise anybody would write down afterwards. That is not how concrete lets go at three in the morning in a peak, and everybody who has ever heard it has said the same thing, which is that it goes like a man clearing his throat.

Four hundred tons of Bellweather-era concrete with two shorings and eleven years of deferred maintenance came out of the upstream end of the apron and went into the reach in about nine seconds, and the water that had been standing nine feet deep on the upstream side of it went with it, and Doak was on the apron and went into the water to his knees and came back up the apron on his hands and did not break anything and was not hurt.

The reach took it. The reach took the water and the gravel and about two hundred feet of apron edge and a bollard, and the level of the reach, which had been climbing for nine hours, came up four feet in one minute flat and stopped, and stood, and then began, very slowly, to come up again.

That was the moment the lowest four blocks of the ninth ward went under.

---

It went the way water goes back up a drain, because that is exactly what it was, and Low Spindle's lowest four blocks do not drain into a river: they drain into the Bellweather reach, through the reach's own run, into that reach, and every drain and culvert and outfall in four hundred yards of Low Spindle has for a hundred and thirty years discharged into a thing that was held in by a wall at the bottom of it.

For nine days that reach had been a bucket. The ward had been putting nine days of rain into it, four hundred and forty people and two pump houses and a school and a hundred and thirty years of drains, and the bucket had a wall on it, and the wall held.

At twenty past to four in the morning the bucket was full and the wall was in the reach, and everything the ninth ward's low ground had sent away for nine days came back up out of the ward's own pipes.

It came up through the drains. It came up the culverts. It came up the low ends of the ward's run where the run goes under the fourth block, and it came up the gully at the corner by the district office, and it came up the four catch pits under iron grates in the fourth block and made a thing of the noise, and it came up the throat at the bottom of Sump Lane, which is a straight stone throat a man can put his hand into and which was, at about ten to four in the morning, throwing nine inches of brown water out of a mouth that a lamp used to be held in.

It came into the ground floors of the lowest four blocks at about the same time and in the same quarter of an hour, from below, which is the worst way and the way that nobody who has not seen it can be made to understand: not over a wall and through a door, but up out of the floor.

Neris Kade had the whole of the low ground clear before two o'clock.

That is the thing about the ninth ward on this night and it is in Sergeant Ampow's own hand in a wet notebook and it is in the district's book in two places, and it is the reason nobody in that block died: they had been in the ground floors since three o'clock the previous morning. The wardens had put them up into the upper rooms and the assembly rooms and the second pump house in a run that had started at four o'clock in the afternoon of the twenty-ninth and had finished at about eleven, and it had finished, and it had been finished for five hours before the ward's own water came back up out of the ward's own drains.

There is no column anywhere for a day's shift of a district's whole population moved up four floors by about thirty people with torches and handbells and no forecast and no claim and no money, and there never will be.

Six ground-floor rooms in the fourth block went under again at about ten to four. They had been wet on the ninth of the month and they were wet on the thirtieth. The ward's books count six and the ward's own count on the morning says six and the district office would not argue the figure if anybody brought nine.

Nobody was hurt. The first pump house drowned, which is a different sentence and means that the pump that had been taking the ward's water down to the reach for nine days had its intake under eight feet of reach by six in the morning and stayed there for a fortnight, and the district got its water out of one pump instead of two for a fortnight, and that is the whole of that cost and it is in pounds.

---

At about a quarter to five Tarin Senn had the *Wren* in the middle of the reach with the boom up and two lines on the apron bollard and a man on the apron who wanted to get to the top of the switchback, and she took him up.

They got up to the hard ground at about ten past five, on foot, in the dark, in the peak, and the man got his lamp and went up the path to see whether a water cart could get through, and Tarin Senn stood on the flat at the top of the reach with the whole of the basin in front of her and the peak coming off it in sheets and the ridge a black line with a light on it.

The south-bank road came down at about five o'clock.

She was four hundred feet above it and two hundred yards away and she watched about forty feet of wet hillside let go off a root and take thirty yards of road with it into the cut above the apron, and it took about four seconds, and then there was a great deal more noise than that for about two minutes, and then there was no noise at all except the rain.

Somebody's lamp came up the path behind her. Doak, on his knees in the water, with a cut over one eye that he had not mentioned.

"Right," he said, when he had got his breath.

"Right," said Tarin Senn.

"That's the road."

"That's the road."

"That's not the culvert," said Doak.

"No," said Tarin. "It isn't. And you're going to write it in two different lines, and you are going to say it in those words, and if anybody comes out here in a fortnight and tries to put the road under the culvert because the culvert's on the sheet and the culvert's old, I will come back down here and I will stand in front of your desk and read the rest of it to you."

"You'll have to come down in the dark."

"I'll come down in the dark," said Tarin Senn, "and I will bring the *Wren*."

---

They stood on the top of the reach in the rain until about twenty past five, and then she took nine men down the path in the dark to the yard on the far side, and at about half past six the whole of the second pump house crew and about two hundred people were standing on the low side of the fourth block watching a district's water come back up out of its own drains, and nobody said anything that was worth putting in a book, and at about seven Neris Kade put a warden on the top of the reach with a line and a lamp because she was not going to have anybody stand in that.

At about nine, with the rain easing to something ordinary, Tarin Senn took the *Wren* out into the reach.

The reach was a different place. It was nine feet of moving brown water where a foot and a half of slow brown water had been at four o'clock, and the freight apron was a spit of concrete in it, and the west end of the apron was gone, and about two hundred feet of the upstream edge was gone with the headwall in it, and the dedication plate was under nine feet of water somewhere in there, which is the oldest surviving use of the name in this city and which a barge with a drag could find in a fortnight.

She took the *Wren* up under the apron's overhang, which is the only reason a flat-bottomed lighter with a gantry is worth owning in a basin where the yards are two hundred yards away and the reach is a mile wide, and she had the beam on the underside of the apron's lip and the boom up, and she got a line on the pump house's intake screen at about ten to ten, from underneath it, with eight feet of reach water over the apron and the lift taking the weight.

The intake screen had gone. That is the thing that saved the second pump house. The first pump house's screen had gone with the wall and its pump was under the reach; the second pump house's screen was a cast thing on the low side of the apron and it had come away on its mountings and was lying in the water at an angle, and had it stayed in its mountings it would have taken the pump house's intake and the pump house with it, and the pump house is the one that stayed dry.

She had the boom's cradle on it at about a quarter to ten and had it up and in over the apron lip by eleven, and it came out of the water at half past eleven in the afternoon like a drowned dog coming up a slip, and it went on the bed of the *Wren*, and the pump house was pumped from about two that afternoon, and the second pump house came back to its full rate on the first of the month.

There is no claim anywhere in that, and there is no branch, and there is no spell, and the whole of it is that a yard which is an airship yard and not a dredging yard happened to own a flat-bottomed lighter with a gantry, and a captain who had been down to a shed twice in a month knew what the reach could do, and a warden at midnight rang a yard and asked for a boat that could get under a freight apron.

The cost of it was a boom cable, a day's work for eleven people, and about three hundred pounds of the Line's own account, and Ilva Sorn entered it under *errors of the yard, borne in public*, on the column, with the hour on it, and nobody argued with her about it.

---

Here is the other thing about the thirtieth, and the only person who has ever been in a position to say it clearly, and she has never been asked and would not have known what to do if she had been.

The headwall was overdue. Not damaged-in-a-storm overdue. *Overdue* overdue, in the way a thing is overdue when it has been on a funded list for two years and a deferred list for two years and has been shored twice and has about a foot of its face gone to gravel, and the water office has a queue, and the queue is the correct instrument for deciding what gets done next, and everybody in the city has behaved correctly about it at every hour of every day for eleven years.

The road was a hill.

There is no relationship between those two sentences. A hill above a switchback on a soft bank with two hundred feet of it standing up under a conduit comes down in a peak, and it has come down before, and it will come down again, and the water office's engineer will say so in about four months and the cost of it will be four hundred yards of new cutting, and it is storm damage, and storm damage is what storm damage is called, and the fact that it happened on the same morning as the headwall and two hundred yards from it is one of about four million things that happen on a morning.

And what was not there, on the thirtieth, anywhere on the flat or the apron or the reach or the low ground or in any book in any building in this basin, was a nine-person municipal convoy with a banded timber stack and a replacement dewatering pump standing at the top of the reach at twenty past seven in the morning on correct water with a mark showing.

Nobody standing in the water that day could have seen a branch. There is no such thing to see. A branch is not a hole in the world and it is not a mark on a wall and it does not leave anything behind that a man with a lamp can put his hand on; what it leaves behind is a thing that does not happen, and a thing that does not happen is not evidence of anything except to a man who knew about it on Tuesday and wrote it down on Tuesday and had somebody who could check.

The only man in the basin who could have seen it had been reading a plate in a yard, at half past four in the afternoon on the twenty-ninth, in a good coat, with a claim in a drawer.

And the water went out of the lowest four blocks of the ninth ward at about two in the afternoon on the thirtieth, and the freights were cancelled, and the apron would be out of business for a month, and a wall that has been a landmark since before the ward line was drawn was under nine feet of the reach it was built to hold, and Tarin Senn stood in a flat-bottomed lighter in the middle of it at about two o'clock in the afternoon and looked at a basin full of water and a hill coming down four hundred yards away and did not know any of it, and could not have done anything about any of it, and was not the person anybody should have asked.
