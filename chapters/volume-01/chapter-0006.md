# Chapter 6 — The Lowest Row

The leading rain came in at eleven with no wind in front of it, which was the only good thing about it, and Tarin used that good thing immediately and did not have time to enjoy it.

Three ships on the grass above the Hearthward market, a crosswind coming round from the west by the back of the reservoir, and a hundred and forty tonnes of *Wren*, *Sparrow*, and a barge called *Penny* that had no business being on a terrace at all and was there because its owner had paid for the grass. Mooring crews from the Line worked in the dark with lamps on their helmets and a spreader rigged on the reservoir wall, and the work was not difficult until the crosswind arrived, and then it became a different job entirely, because the wind took the *Wren* forty degrees off and tried to walk her down the slope of the grass in the direction of the market.

"Spare line on the bow! Take the slack off the quarter or she'll go round!"

The Line's people were good at this. She had spent nine years watching a yard's worth of riggers turn a wet hillside into a mooring field in the dark and she had never once had to shout at them about it twice, and the thing that made it possible was that none of them would tie a ship to a terrace in a blow without being told exactly what the ground was doing under the grass, and Tarin could tell them, because she had landed on Hearthward a hundred and forty times and had spent a night on that low row in a shed with a woman in it, and she knew where the ground gave and where the retaining wall leaned.

The claim had been true, and Tarin found herself acknowledging that fact in the middle of a mooring, which she had not expected and did not much enjoy.

The gate shut and stayed shut. The upper rows were dry. She had seen it when she came up over the ridge at half past ten: the upper spill gate shut and sweating, the tanks on the upper terrace full to their marks, no water anywhere on the middle row where there ought to have been an inch of standing water by now. The word they had published at eleven had been doing exactly what it said, and half of Hearthward was going to sleep dry, and the seed vaults on the high side were going to be all right, and Tarin had found herself thinking *Vale was right about that* in a tone she disliked.

Down in the market, though, the rain was finding every joint it had.

There was a set of catch pits at the bottom of the lowest row, four of them under iron grates the size of a cartwheel, and Tarin had stood over them six days ago on a Thursday morning in the dry, with a cup of somebody's tea in her hand, and watched four men and a foreman take a whole week out of them with long-handled scoops and a bucket between them, because she had moored on the grass above that row on the way to Hearthward and had a spare hour and nothing to do with it, and because the sight of four people working in a hole is the sort of thing you stand and watch when you are tired.

The foreman had straightened up halfway through and looked up at her ship sitting on his terrace with her nose over the wall, and had shouted up that the row would take anything now. Tarin had called down that it would take anything on Thursday too, and he had said that Thursday was his as well, and she had thought at the time that it was a funny thing for a man to say about his own week, and had not thought about it again.

Six days. It had been clean pits on Thursday last and it was due again on Thursday this, and a row with a clear outfall on the third night of rain is a Thursday problem, and a row without one is a problem that has quietly moved itself to next Tuesday, and the pits were now four feet under water and she could not have told one of the men from the other in the dark.

"The market's the worst of it," said Ovett, over the noise. "Forty, fifty people still under the awnings with crates out. Hallard's stall's on the worst of the pitch and he's taken the tarp off it to sell the last of the—"

"Tell him to put it back on and put his crates on the high kerb."

"He'll lose the crates."

"He'll lose the crates or he'll lose the shutters off the row behind him. High kerb. Go on."

At eleven twenty the market cleared faster than she expected, and Ovett counted sixty-one people out of it onto the high side, and the clearing was not orderly. People took what they were carrying and went up the terraces, and a woman with two children and a basket of seedlings stood under the market's arch for four minutes refusing to go up because her own mother was on the fourth row and had been in the house since Tuesday, and Ovett went and got her, and Tarin went and stood in the arch and did not say anything at all to anybody, because there was a version of a sentence about the lower drains that she had been thinking since midnight and it was not the sentence to say to a woman holding a basket.

At eleven forty the lower row's drains filled.

Tarin felt it before she saw it. There was a noise she knew — a noise like somebody running a bath full and standing on the plughole — and it came up out of the row under the retaining wall, and the row's outfall, which is a stone throat the width of a cart and as old as the terraces, made a sound it had not made in three years of her coming here.

The surcharge had come up.

You could see it on the wall, which was the part nobody argued about. A dark line came up the plastered face of the retaining wall from the outfall, and it stopped, and then it came a little higher, and Tarin stood in the rain and watched six ground-floor rooms on the lowest row go under the line where a wall has to be wet before the water behind it decides to come through the plaster instead of going back down the drain.

She knew which six. She had moored a crew's boat off the second one on the same night, and she had a hand-lettered number on a chalk board in a yard in Kestrel from a woman of sixty-eight coming up the terraces in her nightgown with a cat in a basket, and the number was 4, and there was a 4 chalked on the door of the second room from the outfall, and there were five others inside twenty metres of it.

The water did not come through the plaster. That was the mercy in it, and it lasted until about two in the morning, and by then the plaster had decided that the drain was not going to take it and the water found the doorway of the first room, and then the second, in the way water does, patiently, the way a creditor does.

Nobody died. She made sure of that by two in the morning and she went on making sure of it every twenty minutes after that, with Ovett and one of the Line's people walking the row with a lamp, and later with a warden, and later still with a warden and a copy from the ninth ward who wrote everything down, including a line about the seed vaults' lowest drawers that Tarin would carry around in her pocket for the rest of her life.

The vaults were cut into the terrace face behind the market, and the lower six drawers of the vault were eighteen inches off the market floor, and nobody had put them up, because the vaults are Hearthward's answer to a dry year and everybody on the terraces has understood for two generations that the vaults are not to be moved in a storm, and that night the water came up eighteen inches and stayed up eighteen inches for six hours.

A season's seed stock. Ninety drawers in that vault cut into the terrace face, and it was the lower six of them that sat eighteen inches off the market floor, and every one of those six is ruined, most of them still in paper and tins, and there is no way to write that on a gate and have it mean what it means.

---

Marek Vale was on the terrace at half past eight in the morning, and he had been there since eight the previous evening, and he looked like a man who had read one instrument for eleven hours and had got an answer he did not want, and had then gone down onto the reach at about six with a lamp to put his hand in the outfall throat, and had come back up without saying so much as good morning.

Tarin came down off the grass at eight with a cup of the market's worst tea in her hand and the row behind her going white as the water went out of it, and the man from the ridge was below the market on his knees with a lamp and his hand in the outfall throat.

"Six rooms," she said. "The second one's got a woman and a man in it and they're sixty-eight and seventy-one and they're furious and they're dry, and everything they own is not. The vaults are gone. Market's shut until the end of the month and the assembly's blaming the Office, which is fair, and which I am going to have to listen to for a month while they are correct."

"It held," Marek said. "The gate held and the upper rows are dry and the water's where I said it would be."

"It did." Tarin drank the tea. It was as bad as she had expected. "Say something to me about that, because you have got a very particular face on and I'd like to know which part of it I'm looking at."

"You've been here since eight last night," Marek said. "I came up at eight. You've been here twelve hours and I've been here eleven and neither of us left, and there are two hundred and thirty people on these terraces who went up in the night because a bell rang, and that is the reason the upper rows are dry."

"That's the reason the upper rows are dry," Tarin agreed, "and the market is shut for a month and six people's rooms are ruined and a season's seed is in the market hall and I have got eleven people on my payroll who are not going to get paid this month because of a claim that worked."

"Yes," Marek said. "Both."

They stood in it. That was the thing she would remember afterward, and it was the thing that made the rest of it possible: for about forty seconds on a wet morning, neither of them pretended. A captain who loses a month and a researcher whose claim did exactly what it said stood in eight inches of receding water and let both halves of the arithmetic be in the room at the same time.

Then Tarin said: "Who stood the garden crew down?"

"Yesterday afternoon. The water steward says she clears them Tuesdays and Thursdays."

"This is Thursday."

"He told me he would," Marek said. "At the bench. Yesterday. Before the rain. He said *I'll clear them*, and she said she had been telling him for nine years without being asked, and I wrote the times down on a pad, and then I went up to the hall and read the claim out, and at half past twelve a man from Kestrel wanted the wording of the condition, and then it was raining, and I did not come down."

The rain had stopped some time in the night and started again in the small hours, and it was doing a light, businesslike drizzle now that made everything look rinsed.

"So he stood down," Tarin said.

"I don't know that he stood down."

"That is not the same as saying he didn't."

"No," Marek said. "It isn't."

"You were in the room with him."

"I was in the room with four people and I spent forty minutes on a runoff calculation I did from a ward sheet, and I never went to Hearthward in my life before yesterday evening, and I asked for the day of the week and I did not ask where he went, and I did not ask who clears them when he does not, and I did not ask what the last time looked like, and I had a claim to write and a bell to strike and I wrote the claim and struck the bell." He had said all of that in a level voice, and then he stopped, and Tarin watched him arrive at the end of it and watch himself arrive at it.

"That last bit," she said. "That was a list."

"Yes."

"You read it out like a fault report."

"It was a list," Marek said. "It was a list of the questions I did not ask. There is one more."

"Then ask it."

"There isn't one," he said. "That's the list."

And he did not say the other thing, and the other thing was sitting in his mouth where she could almost see it, and it was this: *if the garden crew had cleared the drains at the first hour, the lower row's outfall would have taken the load in three hours instead of nine, and the surcharge would never have come above that line, and six rooms and a season's seed would be dry this morning, and the claim I made would still be true, and the market would still be shut for a month, and there would be nothing to complain about except the weather.*

He did not say it, because saying it would have required saying what made it true, and he had not asked, and a man who does not ask is a man who cannot be answered.

Tarin put her cup down on a stone kerb.

"I am not going to ask you a second time tonight," she said, "because it is eight in the morning and you have been up all night and there is a glazier coming to the market hall for that storm pane and I would rather you answered a stranger than me." She turned to go, and stopped, and did not turn round. "But I have been wrong about the Office before, in a room, with a crew behind me, and I was not wrong, and it cost me the crew anyway. So I know what a right answer is worth on its own. Don't do that to the low row. Do it to me some other time."

She went up.

At eight twenty the glazier came up off the terrace road with a pane under her arm and a putty knife in her boot, an ordinary Kestrel tradeswoman of about fifty who had done the market's storm pane twice before and who got up on the lower row to have a look at a frame that had swelled.

Ferren was on the step of the shed at the bottom of the row with four shovels leaning against the wall and a barrow he had not used.

He was a heavy man of about fifty-five with wet trousers rolled to the knee and a way of standing with his arms down that made him look shorter than he was, and he had been the rain-garden foreman on those terraces for nine years, and he had been in a room on a ridge at ten past eleven the previous morning with a claim on it.

The glazier asked him about the frame. Ferren told her, patiently, that the frame was fine and the sill was the problem and that she would want to let the timber dry a fortnight before she set anything in it, and then he stopped, and stood there looking at the barrow.

"Twenty-three forty," he said. "That's when it filled."

"You want to come and look at the sill with me?" the glazier said. "I've got it on my sheet."

"Aye." Ferren did not move. "Hang on. Hang on, let me get it right."

The glazir waited, because glaziers wait; it is a job that is nine parts standing still.

"It filled at twenty-three forty and I was stood on this step," Ferren said, "because I always am at that time, because I do it Tuesdays and I do it Thursdays and I have done since I had the crew, and I have a shovel in my hand and I have a barrow behind me, and I am stood here — and the water is coming up the row — and I am stood here." He looked at his own hands with an expression of complete bafflement, the way a man looks at a door that will not open although he has the key. "I could not tell you what I did with the evening. I was not stood down. Nobody stood me down. I was not ill and I was not sent for and I did not lose my watch." He turned the shovel over. "I remember the whole night. I remember the rain starting and the gate going and the man off the ridge being up here on the glass until the light came, and I remember thinking that the drains were clear and we were all right. I remember that like a thing I did this morning."

"Perhaps you did clear them," the glazier said kindly, "and it's gone from you the way hard work does."

Ferren shook his head. "No, I didn't," he said. "There isn't a Wednesday's work in those drains. I've been out on a Tuesday and I can tell you what I left in them, and there was nothing in them last night because there was nothing in them for the whole day, and I couldn't tell you why, and I have been sitting on this step for two hours trying to tell you why." He lifted the shovel an inch off the ground and set it down again. "And there's a thing I can't get past, and it sounds daft to say out loud, and I'm saying it out loud because you lot are strangers."

"Go on."

"There was nothing wrong," said Ferren. "That's what I can't get past. It was a bad night, and the water came, and the low row went under, and every one of those things was going to happen whether we'd been out there or not. And I've been sitting here feeling glad." He looked up at the glazier with the particular embarrassment of a man who has spent his life being sceptical. "I didn't clear them, and I was glad, and I don't know what that's for, and I'm telling a stranger because I don't want to be carrying it round on my own until dark."
