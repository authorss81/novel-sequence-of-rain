# Chapter 20 — The Signature

The rain-garden assembly rooms at Hearthward are two rooms above the market hall, and the market hall had been shut since the Friday before last with a season's seed stock in it and boards across the doors, and on the eighteenth day of the Bellweather autumn Anwen Sarr held a meeting in the upper one with eleven people in it and the whole of the weather settled outside the window.

She had not called a meeting. Nobody had asked for one. She had received a request for a signature on the Friday, and she had read it twice on the Saturday, and on the Sunday night she had sent for the clerk and for the man from the ridge, and the clerk had sent for the chair, and the chair had sent for the two other people in the district who had to be in the room for a decision of this kind, which was Ferren, because the Hearthward rain-garden crew's standing order is his and there is no rain-garden standing order in this district that is not his.

He came in at ten past six and stood by the door with his cap in his hands, as he had stood by a door on the ninth day, and he had not spoken on that day either.

Kell Bray was the clerk. He was thirty-one and had been twenty years in a ledger, four of them for this assembly, and he kept the assembly's own book and the district's returns and the day book, and he had brought all three down from the room above and had them open on the long table before anybody came in, because that was what you did.

Anwen Sarr stood at the head of the table with her hip against it.

"Right," she said. "Mr Vale, you have been sent for. Sit down. I have asked you here because I am going to say something about you in a room with a clerk in it, and I would rather you heard it from me in front of the clerk than found out in a fortnight."

Marek sat down.

"I will be about twenty minutes," she said. "You will be permitted to answer at the end and I warn you now that I do not want an answer, I want the document, and the document does not exist, and we both know that, and if you produce a piece of paper in the next sixty seconds I will be extremely suspicious of it."

---

"The Council's request," she said, and turned the sheet round so that the whole table could see it, "is one page. It is the best one-page document I have received from that building in six years and I am going to say so in front of my own clerk, who has been extremely patient with me for four years.

"It asks this assembly to give one signature. Not to endorse the method. Not to agree with the Council. To *concur* — a specific word, and they have printed the definition underneath it, which no institution has ever done for me in my life — with one narrow certified trial claim at the Nacre Gate release, for the length of a market season, with a named sponsor and a published expiry and a listed alternative, so that the Council can find out whether a certified claim helps a district that has prepared for it or makes its preparations unnecessary."

She put her hand flat on it.

"That is a good request. It is specific, it is bounded, it has a stop in it, and the woman who wrote it has told us in plain words what the trial is *for*, which is to find out whether we get better or worse. Do you know how many times in six years an institution has told me in plain words what it wanted to find out about me? None. Not one. I have been a difficult woman to a series of careful men for six years and every single one of them has explained to me why they needed me to agree, and this one has explained to me why they want to find out."

"The assessor is here," said Kell Bray.

"Yes," said Anwen. "Sit at the end, Sabin, and let her come to the table when she wants to."

Sabin Orle was forty-four and had been in the Council's technical cell for eleven years, and she had come up the hill on a Sunday evening coach because a district clerk had telephoned her on Monday morning and told her, in the clipped voice of a woman with a ledger open, that the Hearthward rain-garden assembly was declining to concur and would like to know the reasons for the record before they left the room.

She came to the table now, and she was not defensive about it, and she had a folder.

"I have four certified trial claims in that folder," she said. "Four, in eleven years, all of them narrow, all of them with an expiry, all of them with an alternative on the sheet, and I will put it in front of you. I would like the concurrence because I would like the Hearthward seat, and I want to be straightforward about why, because you are entitled to know that I have an interest.

"The method works. I will not pretend otherwise. Every certified claim I have entered in eleven years has been true, and three of them were true in circumstances where an uncertified forecast of the same shape would have been wrong. That is not nothing. It is the reason the Council has a quorum at all." She squared the folder. "And what I do not have, and cannot get, and have been asking the technical cell for two years to give me, is a single controlled test on a district that prepared. Because every claim I have ever certified was made in a district that had done nothing, and I do not know what my method does to a district that has done everything. I would like to know. Somebody in that building thinks it will make them worse, and I would like to spend a market season finding out whether that is true, and I have asked three assemblies and two of them have said yes without reading it, and one of them has said not yet, and she is the only one who read it, and I have driven up here on a Sunday evening because I would rather be in this room than in the other two."

Nobody said anything for a few seconds.

"Right," said Anwen Sarr. "Then I am going to refuse you, and I am going to do it in a way that you will be able to file, and I am going to do it in front of my clerk, and I am going to give you the reasons in writing whether you want them or not."

"Go on," said Sabin Orle.

"Two reasons, and the second one is the real one, and I am putting it first in my own head and second on the paper because I want the paper to be usable by somebody who was not in this room.

"First. The trial is to find out whether a certified claim helps or harms the preparations of a district that has prepared. There is a district in this basin that prepared, six weeks ago, on the strength of a claim with an expiry on it, and that district is mine, and the answer to your question is already sitting in my market hall in six ruined rooms, and the answer has not been published. The Council cannot run a controlled test on the method's effect on prepared districts while the one prepared district in the basin has not been told what it paid. I am not refusing you on principle. I am refusing you on *sequence*. You cannot measure the thing before the first example of the thing has been measured."

Kell Bray had his pen down. He was not writing fast enough and he did not pretend that he was.

"And the second," said Anwen Sarr, "is that in September I put in writing to that man that I required the cost of a binding claim to be published, in a document, with the sponsor's own name at the top of it, and I have had an undertaking in a corridor that he would bring it. That was on the ninth day. It is the eighteenth. There is no document. There is not a page of it. And I am not going to put this district's name under a trial of a method, in front of a Council, in front of the market factor, and in the minutes of this assembly for the next eleven years, while the district that is owed the truth about it is waiting on a man who is in this room."

She did not point at him. She did not need to.

Marek said nothing.

"Second," said Sabin Orle, after a moment. "That is fair. That is more than fair, and I have got a file of four claims and not one of them has ever cost a district anything in a way I could name, because I could not name one, and I have been uneasy about that for two years." She looked at the folder and then at the man at the table. "Mr Vale. What would this assembly have to see in order to concur?"

Marek found that he had answered a version of this on a stone floor two weeks ago and had not noticed that he was doing it again.

"The cost of the Hearthward claim, in a document, in the sponsor's hand, naming the turns it displaced and the preparations that stopped being available," he said. "And if the cost cannot be reduced to things somebody can be shown, then the document has to say that too, in the same words, and take the same space up. I can write that. I have been trying to write it since the ninth. It is not ready."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"Then you have until the Council sits," said Sabin Orle, "and I will tell you the Council sits on the nineteenth, and I will tell you why, because it is on the front of the folder and I would rather you had it now than heard it from a notice."

"The claim is entered tomorrow morning."

"Right," said Sabin Orle. "Then it is entered tomorrow morning, and it is entered with a different seat, and the Nacre Holds delegate has already concurred and I telephoned her on the Friday." She closed the folder. "I am not going to pretend I am sorry. I am sorry about the method and not about the seat, because the seat is a chair and the method is a thing, and I can get a chair."

---

Anwen Sarr read the refusal out loud to the room before she signed it, which is the practice in that district and which nobody has ever asked her to do.

"Clause one. The assembly declines to concur in the trial claim named in the Council's request of the fifteenth. Clause two. The assembly's grounds are that the Council cannot test the effect of a certified claim on prepared districts while the cost of the only prepared district's claim is unpublished, and that this assembly is that district. Clause three. The assembly's refusal is not a criticism of the method, and the assembly will concur with any future claim in this basin that carries a published cost at the point of entry. Clause four. This assembly's standing requirement, submitted separately and in addition: that no claim certified under the Charter shall be entered without the cost of that claim being published at the point of entry, or the sponsor recording in writing that the cost cannot be made public and why."

Kell Bray wrote all four clauses in one hand without lifting the pen except at the ends of lines, and had to be given the second page, and did not have to be given the second page.

Anwen Sarr signed at the bottom. Kell Bray countersigned it as clerk. He stamped it with the assembly's ordinary stamp, which is a rain garden, and dated it the eighteenth.

"That goes in the post tonight," she said, "and the fourth clause goes with it, and the fourth clause is going to be read by nine watershed seats and three regional seats and it is going to be the most comprehensively ignored document in the history of this basin, and I am sending it anyway, because the alternative is that I do not send it, and then in three years' time somebody can say nobody asked."

"Clause four," said Sabin Orle, standing up and taking her folder. "Is that a legal requirement or a moral one?"

"It is a legal requirement in the first sentence and a moral one in the second, and it is drafted so that a Council can satisfy the first and fail the second, and I have done that on purpose." Anwen Sarr held her hand out. "I have wanted to have this conversation for two years and you have given me forty minutes of it for free on a Sunday evening, and I would like it recorded that I think the trial is a good idea and that I am refusing it because you have got the order of your own experiment wrong, and those are two different sentences."

"They are two different sentences," said Sabin Orle, "and one of them is going to be in the minutes and it is not going to be the one you want."

"I know that," said Anwen Sarr. "It is the only thing about this room that I am certain about."

---

They got her out of the building at about twenty past seven and the coach had gone and it was four miles to the tram in the dark with rain coming back across the valley from the west, which was not yet a front and was not going to be one.

Marek walked her to the bottom of the market steps. Ferren came out with his cap on and went off down toward the row, and did not look in, and Kell Bray locked up behind them and went home without saying anything at all, which was the single most respectful thing that had happened in that room.

Anwen Sarr stood in the wet at the bottom of her own steps and looked at him.

"Two things," she said, "and then you can go and be sick somewhere."

"Yes."

"One. You did not answer me. I gave you the floor in a room with a clerk in it and you gave me four sentences and then you told the assessor of the Council that you did not know how long it would take, which is the answer of a man who has decided in advance that he is going to fail and has decided to be honest about it early so that nobody can say later that he was surprised." She let that sit. "It was the right thing to say and it was the worst thing you could have said, and I want you to understand that both of those are true and that I would not have been angry if you had lied to me."

"I did not lie to you."

"You said *it is not ready*," said Anwen Sarr. "In eleven years of that office and in six years of my sitting in that chair, I have never once been told by a reader that something was not ready. I have been told that a thing is a records question. I have been told that a thing is not a question the record answers. I have never once been told a thing was not ready, and I did not know that the sentence existed, and now that I know it exists I am going to use it on people for the rest of my life, and I am going to be extremely unfair about it, and it will be your fault."

Marek did not have an answer to that and it was a mercy that he did not have to find one.

"Two," she said. "You are going to think that I did that in there because of you. I want it on the record in front of nobody that I did not, and I am telling you now while it can still be stupid of me to deny it.

"I did it because the order was wrong. The method is to be tested on a district that prepared, and the only prepared district in this basin has not been told what it paid, and there is no version of that experiment that is not a lie told to a room. If your document had been finished this would have been a signature on a Monday afternoon and you would not have heard about it. If your document had never been promised there would have been a different reason and you would still not have heard about it." She turned up her collar. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"You did it because the experiment was in the wrong order, and I have made it harder to argue with, and you would rather argue with me about a document for a week than argue with a Council about an experiment in three."

"That," said Anwen Sarr, "is the whole of what I wanted. Go on. You have got a tube in your coat and a plate in it and a face on you that says you have found something and cannot say what it is, and I have watched men carry that face since I was nineteen, and it is the worst face there is, and it is the only face in this basin that I have never once been able to get anything out of."

She went up the steps and into a market hall with a season's seed in it, and shut the door, and Marek Vale stood on the wet cobbles at the bottom of them with the fourth clause of a refusal in the post and nothing in his coat but a piece of glass and a cuff with three blocks of pencil in it.
