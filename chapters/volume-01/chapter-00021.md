# Chapter 21 — The Minutes

The Council's technical cell meets in a long room on the second floor of the basin house with the windows shuttered: a bench down each side, a plate rack along the north wall holding fourteen certified record plates in felt, a bench press, a rubber mallet on a hook. There are a hundred and eighty people in the cell over the course of a week. On the nineteenth day of the Bellweather autumn there were twenty-six in the room at nine in the morning.

Marek Vale was at the end of the right-hand bench with a plate in a frame in front of him and a lamp behind it, and he was there because a Council assessor had telephoned the Weatherglass on the Monday morning and asked him, and he had said yes in about four seconds and then spent the whole of Monday afternoon unable to work out why.

The answer was that he was the only reader in the basin who had read the order in a collision, and a claim about a release at a gate is a claim about order. The question a gate release turns on is not *how much* and it is not *when*. It is *what is standing behind it* — what has been prepared, what has been arranged, what has been left available — and there is one instrument in this city that shows order, and it had been in his coat for a fortnight.

He was being used. Properly, on the record, in a room, for the thing he was for, and he had wanted it since he was nineteen.

---

The claim was on the bench in front of him in Sabin Orle's neat hand, and it read:

*Place: the Nacre Gate, the upper reservoir, the Ferrygate low side and the market road to the ferry landing.*
*Trigger: the release, held to 04:40 on the twenty-first day.*
*Effect, as entered: the Nacre Gate releases at 04:40; the release does not exceed the published band; the Ferrygate low side drains clear to the second watch; the market road to the landing takes loaded carts from first light on the twenty-first day.*
*Listed alternative, as required: if the release is not held to 04:40, the low side floods to the low marker and the autumn market closes for the season.*
*Expiry: the second watch of the twenty-first day.*
*Sponsor: S. Orle, assessor, water scheduling.*
*Concurring seat: Nacre Holds.*

"Narrow," Sabin Orle said, from the other end of the bench, "bounded, an expiry at the second watch of the twenty-first, an alternative that is worse for the district and is written down. Hearthward would not look at it. I am not bitter about that. Are you?"

"No," Marek said.

"You have read the gate's record five times since Friday. Is the order consistent?"

Marek read it again, and then he read the certified record plate from the rack, which was fourteen months of the Nacre gate's own sequence at the head of the feed, and it took eleven minutes, and nobody in the room interrupted him, because the twenty-six people in that room were professionals and they know what eleven minutes looks like.

He put the lamp down.

"The order is consistent," he said. "The Release in the gate's record runs shallow all through the upper reservoir draw, and the trigger is inside the movement and not before it, and the movement closes inside the expiry. I attest that the claim is consistent with the instrumented record at the gate."

"Thank you." She made a mark. "Mr Vale, your attestation is entered as a reader's and not as a sponsor's, and the sponsor is me, and if this claim is wrong it is mine and not yours, and I would like that on the record and I would like you to check that it is on the record."

He checked that it was on the record.

And then she did the thing that was going to cost the district three of its preparations, and she did it because it was correct, and she did it in a level voice with a pen in her hand.

Marek saw the claim stand up out of the plate.

Not a glow. Nothing in the technical cell is a glow. What happens is that the trace under the plate firms, the way water firms in a shallow pan when the wind comes off it, and the six movements on the record draw closer together and hold, and there is a line of flat glass in the frame that a plate makes when a claim is being taken, and the glass says what it says:

```
CLAIM: the Nacre Gate releases at 04:40 on the twenty-first day; the low side drains clear to the second watch; the market road takes loaded carts from first light.
ONE POSSIBLE TURN WILL CLOSE.
```

He read it twice.

It did not say which turn. It did not say whose. It gave him the exact words Sabin Orle had just had entered and told him, in the flattest possible voice, that one of the things still available in the world was about to stop being available because the Council was about to make this true in front of twenty-six people.

He had seen a panel like that once before, on a bench on the ridge, at eleven o'clock on a Thursday, in front of a copy clerk and four people who had walked up a hill in the rain. He had not slept properly for four days afterwards and a district had got six rooms wet and a season's seed into a market hall, and the claim had been true.

The bell went at ten. Not the entry bell — the certification bell, which is a hand bell, rung once, by the assessor.

"Right," said Sabin Orle. "That is entered at ten, and it binds on the trigger and not before, and the trigger is the release, and the release is at 04:40 on the twenty-first, and the district has just under forty-three hours to do whatever it is going to do. Thank you, Mr Vale. You are free to go or you are welcome to sit at the back, and if you sit at the back I am going to ask you to say nothing, and I want to explain the asking before I do it."

She came down the bench and sat on the end of the right-hand one, in the seat next to him, which she had not had any reason to do.

"Two reasons," she said, quietly, under the noise of a room restarting. "One, anything said in this room this morning goes into a document that nine watershed seats and three regional seats will read in four weeks. And two, I have read the Hearthward entry, and everybody has. If you tell nine watershed seats that a certified claim costs somebody something, the first thing that happens is not that anybody writes down what you said. The first thing that happens is that every one of those seats spends the next four years asking what you are hiding. And the third thing that happens is that they never find out."

"That is all true," Marek said.

"It is all true, and it is a technical matter, and I am telling you as a technical matter, because I would like to use you again." She looked at the plate in the frame. "The Release on that gate's record is the shallowest I have seen in a certified run. How shallow?"

"Shallow," Marek said. "And it is not a dropout. A dropout drops hard and leaves a hard edge and a crescent, and this feed has one in it in March with the cause written on a tag in a drawer, and I filed the cause. This thinned. It came back on a shallow slope, late, with nothing standing behind it. A Release with nothing behind it does not release."

He put the lamp down very carefully, because his hands had begun to do the thing they did.

Sabin Orle went back to the bench and the room began its ordinary morning, and Marek Vale sat at the end of it with a certified claim in the rack behind him and a smear in the middle of a Release on a plate that belonged to a regional body and had no chain he could put behind it, and read the rest of the plate in the ordinary course of his job, because he was on the roster and it was Tuesday.

Beneath the fine trace, where the registration goes cold and coarse, there were three shapes, and he went back two days with the lamp oblique and let his eyes go soft, and none of them was weather.

The first was a repetition: a time-pattern, regular, going on and on, the same interval over and over with a column of days down the side of it. He had seen ten thousand of those. That was a schedule. Somebody's, written out, repeated, cheap.

The second was a count: a crossing schedule, a number of passes at intervals, in and out over a fixed span, laid out the way a ferryman lays out a night.

The third was not an instrument's at all. It was two entries, side by side, small, in a hand — and a sequence plate does not hold handwriting, so what he was looking at was not handwriting but the shape a set of marks leaves in a registration when a person has gone near an instrument that is writing everything down. Two entries. Two. He could not have told a soul what they were for.

Three preparations, and there was a fourth shape he had seen the month before in a chart room, on a strip of paper with a piece taken off the top of it, and the two were not the same kind of thing and he was not going to pretend they were: the paper had had a piece cut off it by a hand, and what he was describing had been done to a room full of people by a bell.

He could not produce one word of it.

---

Malle found him at half past twelve in the ante-room of the basin house with a sandwich he had not asked for.

"I have come up on the Sunday coach," he said, "out of hours, to watch a method I staff succeed. Do not look at me like that. It is going to succeed. It is the best-formed claim I have seen in nine years and I put half of it there, and I am going to sit at the back of that room at two o'clock and clap."

He gave Marek the sandwich and Marek did not eat it.

"I am going to say one thing and then leave you alone for an hour." Malle put his hands in his pockets. "I have read the Hearthward refusal. I have had clause four on my desk since yesterday afternoon and read it four times, and I want to tell you the two things it is."

"Which?"

"It is a good document, and it is going nowhere, because every one of those seats would have to publish a cost and nobody in this basin can produce one, including you." He took his cap off and put it back on. "And nobody is entitled to say it is wrong. It is a reasonable demand and it is unanswered, and that difference is the whole of the next four years. But *nobody is entitled to say it in a room where a claim is being certified*, Vale, and if you do, the Office cannot use you again, and I am the man who decides that, and I will decide it in about nine minutes."

"Why?"

"Because a man who says a true thing he cannot prove costs this Office more than a man who says a false thing he can," said Corrin Malle. "I watched a district superintendent do it in the eighties. He was right. He had no chain, and he had a district that had trusted him, and after he said it out loud in a room in the ordinary course of a Tuesday the district stopped reading the Office's figures as instructions and went back to its own. Which is a district with a barometer and a bucket and nothing else, and the difference is not measurable and I have had to live with it for thirty years. He was a good man and he finished his career on a copy desk and he was right, and the Office went on being right about the weather, and the districts went on making their own arrangements. I have been in a river. I have held a man. I am telling you what the thing costs and I am telling you that I am right about the cost."

"And am I right about the claim?"

"You are right about the claim," said Corrin Malle. "That is not the interesting part and you have known it since you were thirty-one. Sit at the back. Clap if you mean it. And do not stand up in that room and tell them what the claim cost, because if you do it today you will not do it again, and if you do not do it today then you will still be here in a year to do it with something in your hand."

He went out. He did not eat the sandwich.

---

The afternoon session filled the room to about eighty, which was more than that chamber was designed for, and a man at the back opened the shutters two inches, and the light came in in a bar and lay across the top of the plate rack.

Sabin Orle reported the certification at two o'clock and every part of it was correct. A claim entered under the Charter with a named sponsor, a published expiry, a listed alternative, a reader's attestation and a concurring seat. A release scheduled for 04:40 on the twenty-first. And then three pieces of paper from the district, and the room was warm about all three, and it was right to be.

A market factor with forty years' reputation in the northern basin had booked eleven carts and two barrows for first light on the twenty-first, on the strength of a claim entered four hours ago.

The Nacre Holds grain convoy's agent had taken the 04:40 to 08:00 window with four chartered barges, and had lodged the booking through the Council's own office, with a time on it, before the day was out.

And the third piece of paper was from the Ferrygate district assembly, and it was four lines long, and Sabin Orle read it out in the flat voice of a woman reading a district's letter, and the room did not make a sound.

*District acknowledges the certified claim. The district withdraws the rain-garden clearing rota for the twenty-first and returns the crew to ordinary work. The district cancels the ferry's extended night hours for the twenty-first, the low road being certified passable. The district thanks the Council.*

That is the whole of it. Four lines. It is a courteous letter and it is a completely correct letter and there is nothing whatever in it that anybody in that room could object to, and every one of those decisions is the district acting, in advance and on the strength of a certified claim, exactly as the method intends a district to act.

Somebody at the back of the room said *that's the point of it* to somebody else, and was not being smug about it, and was entirely right.

The minutes were read at half past two, and they were the best minutes Sabin Orle had ever taken, and the sixth of them said, in the ordinary formula:

*That a certified claim, entered with an expiry and a published alternative, was observed to be responded to by the affected district within one day of entry, with the district's own preparations withdrawn and its ordinary commerce resumed; and that the method may be reported to the Council as having cost the district nothing that the district was willing to record.*

That was the minute. That was in the room, and it was in the minute, and it was going out to nine watershed seats and three regional seats and a public table in four weeks.

Marek Vale stood up in the second row from the back of a room he had been invited into as a working instrument.

What he said was this. He said it slowly and in plain words, and he had not decided to say anything until forty seconds before, and had spent the whole of that forty seconds finding out that there was nothing to prepare.

"The claim is true," he said. "I want to say that first, in those words, because everything else I am going to say is going to sound like an attack on the assessor and it is not. The claim is true and the market will open and the convoy will make its window and the release will not be bigger than the band, and I attest to none of that — I attest to the order, and the order is right — but it is true.

"And it spent a branch."

"Mr Vale," said Sabin Orle, "the Charter provides that a certified claim displaces the turn that most directly contradicts it, and that the displaced turn is a branch, and that this is the mechanism and not an allegation. Everyone in this room knows it. Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that what it displaced is not weather," Marek said.

He heard the room change, and he kept going, because stopping was the thing he had always done.

"There is a smear in the Release on that gate's record plate. It is not a dropout — a dropout drops hard and leaves a hard edge and a crescent, and this feed has one in it in March with a cause written on a tag in a drawer, and I have filed the cause. This thinned. It came back on a shallow slope, forty minutes late, with nothing behind it. That is the shape of a Release that has had the thing standing behind it taken away. It happened at ten o'clock this morning, in this room, when that bell was rung.

"And under the plate, in the registration, there are three preparations still legible, and they have gone out.

"A rota. A column of days with the same interval down it, repeated, cheap, hand-written, kept by somebody in a district that never sent it anywhere. That has gone out, and it is the thing your third piece of paper says the district withdrew.

"A crossing schedule. A ferry's night, counted, in and out, over a fixed span. That has gone out, and it is the extended hours you have just heard cancelled.

"And two marks in a place where a person wrote something down near an instrument that was recording. A sequence plate does not hold handwriting, so I cannot tell you what they are. They are the only two things on that registration that are not machinery, and they have gone out too, and there is no notice anywhere in the world that has anything to do with them."

He stopped, and the room was very quiet, and Sabin Orle had her hand flat on the bench.

"I cannot tell you whose preparations those were," Marek said. "I want to be completely clear about that, because it is the part that matters. I can tell you the shape of three of them. I cannot tell you the name of one. I could not produce a document if you asked me for one, and if you asked me where the evidence is I would have to tell you it is a smear in the middle of a Release on a plate belonging to this Council, read by a reader with no chain behind him, in a shuttered room, on the morning it happened. That is the whole of the evidence. There is no other. It is not a document and it is not in a drawer and it will not survive the week.

"So the call was made correctly, and cheaply, and in about nine minutes, and it was made by a competent person following the correct procedure, and everybody who applauded just now was right to. And the method's success is measured in options removed. That is all it measures. I do not think anybody in this room is doing this on purpose and I would like to say that as clearly as I can, because nobody is. It is a good method and the good method is what is doing it."

He sat down.

---

The silence lasted perhaps four seconds and then a man near the front said, in an entirely reasonable tone, "That is not a challenge, Mr Vale. That is a mood. A challenge is a thing you can be shown to be wrong about."

"No," Marek said. "It isn't."

"Then it is not a challenge."

"No," Marek said. "It is a thing I saw."

Sabin Orle asked him to go through it again, and he did, and she asked him four questions, and he answered three of them, and she asked the third one twice.

"Who wrote them?"

"I don't know."

"What were they for?"

"I don't know."

"Then you have three shapes and no district and no names and no means of establishing that any of it ever existed," said Sabin Orle. "And if that had been said with a sheet of paper in your hand, this room would have adjourned and the minutes would have been re-opened and the Council would have had to answer it. I am not saying it to be unkind. I am saying it because it is the entire reason the method you are worried about is the method we have, and that is not an accident either. It is not accidental that a claim's cost cannot be documented. It is the design. A cost you can document is a cost somebody can be compensated for and argued about, and a Council that had to compensate districts for every displaced preparation would not certify anything at all, because you cannot displace a preparation and put a price on it. And if you can put a price on it, then it was never a preparation. It was a plan with a budget. And a plan with a budget is not the thing we are trying to keep."

Nobody in that room had ever heard that sentence, and it was, Marek understood, completely true, and it was the reason, and it had been said by a woman who would lose nothing for saying it.

"I am going to rule," said Sabin Orle, "that the certification stands, that the reader's attestation stands, and that your statement is entered in the minutes as a statement, unattributed to any district, made by the attesting reader, and not as an objection, because it is not an objection, it is an unproved assertion. You may have the last word and I suggest you spend it correctly."

Marek looked at the plate in the frame with its flat line of claim-glass still in it, and at eighty people in a room that had been built for forty.

"I have no last word," he said. "That is the whole of what I came to say. I have a plate in a locked room in a poor ward's tower and three blocks of pencil in a coat sleeve and a week of not sleeping, and I have just described what your claim cost to a room full of people, and I cannot prove a syllable of it, and the claim was true, and the market is going to open. That is all. I am not going to insult anybody here by pretending it is more than that."

Sabin Orle put her hand down and looked at the room.

"The certification stands," she said. "The minutes of the fourteenth to the nineteenth are closed. Clerk, the Ferrygate letter goes in at the head of the file and the Nacre Holds booking goes in under it, and the reader's statement goes in the annex, and I want the annex bound separately so that a district reading the file cannot miss it and cannot mistake it for an objection." She took her cap off. "Thank you all. It is a good morning's work and the method is sound and the Council will hear from me about the market on Friday."

The room stood up.

And the applause started again inside the first ten seconds, and it was for the method, and it was entirely sincere, and a good many people in it were applauding a man who had just told them, correctly, in front of all of them, what the method was made of.

The clerk came down the bench with the minute on a board to be signed. Marek signed without reading it, because the signature line is at the bottom and the room was loud.

Then he read it, because the clerk had put the sheet down in front of him and taken it away again, and three lines is not long.

A reader's attestation, entered by name. *Attestation of the order: M. Vale, reader.* A clause recording the district's response and that the district's preparations were withdrawn. And a footnote, in the clerk's small hand, giving the file cross-reference of the Hearthward rain-garden assembly's refusal of concurrence, and clause four of it, and the day.

He stood in a room full of people clapping for a method and understood the whole of the afternoon at once.

Every assembly in the basin would now read his name in the same breath as the word *cost*. Not because of anything he had done wrong. Because he had been right, out loud, in a room, and had been asked afterwards for the paper, and had not had it, and the room had gone on clapping, and the minute had recorded all four of those facts on one sheet of paper in the ordinary order of business.

The Office would stop using him, because the Office cannot use a man who has been shown to be unprovable in public.

And no assembly in this basin would ask him anything again.

At the back of the room, in the fourth row, Corrin Malle put his cap on. He had not applauded. He had put a sandwich in a young man's hand and told him the price of the thing he was about to do, and the young man had done it anyway, in front of everybody, which is what Malle had told him in the rain that he would do, and which was the only reason Malle had come.

Malle went out through the side door before the room did.

Marek Vale stood in the second row of the back of a room with a plate in a rack and three blocks of pencil in the left cuff of a work coat, and the whole of that room went out into a settled, cool, harmless evening, and the claim was true, and he had said the true thing out loud, and nobody could prove one syllable of it, and the applause did not stop.
