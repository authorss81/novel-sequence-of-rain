# Chapter 25 — Nine Days in a Full Pipe

The rain did not stop on the twenty-fifth of the Bellweather autumn. It stopped, and came back, and stopped, and came back, and on the ninth day it stopped for about five hours and then came back harder, and by the twenty-fifth Low Spindle had had nine days of it in fourteen.

Four hundred and forty people live in the ninth ward's lowest four blocks. They had the pumps running, both of them, the first house at full and the second house at what Sergeant Ampow described as a rate nobody in the ward has a figure for, and the third pump — the one with the cracked gland that had been bolting a new one out of a shed in Ward Six since the morning after the collision — was doing what a third pump does, which is to be the one that is there.

The office had published nothing since the ninth.

Neris Kade understood the ninth-day bulletin better than anybody in the basin, because she had read it twice and had a boy who had copied it out, and it was still true, and it still said nothing about the low ground beyond a depth in inches and a watch in words, and the district had four hundred and forty people and a school and two hundred and forty children on the roll and a warden whose authority cost nothing and was not counted anywhere.

The ground was not doing anything dramatic. That was the thing she could not get anybody in the district council to understand and had stopped trying to explain. There was no wall of water. There was no gutter going. There was nine days of rain into a catchment that had been taking rain since the seventh and had a line under Hearthward's terraces that was already full, and the ward's own line was already full, and a full line takes a terrace's rain and gives nothing back, and the wall behind it rises for exactly as long as the district has water in it and then stops in about ten minutes because the line was never the constraint.

The water in Low Spindle's lowest four blocks on the twenty-fifth was standing about two inches deep in the streets that ran to the outfall, and it had been standing there since the twenty-second, and it was not rising, and that was the whole problem, because two inches that will not go anywhere is a thing you cannot argue with a council about.

---

She had the answer. She had had it since the Saturday before last, and it had cost her nothing at all to get it, and it was the single most useless thing she possessed.

"Sergeant, run it past me again."

"Aye." Ampow put his cap on the table. "Sergeant, we get it off a mechanic called Vale who is sixty-one and retired and who has been telling this ward for eleven years that the ninth ward runs on a rota and not on a glass. And I got him down the yard off Sump Lane on Saturday because Marda asked me to, because she'd heard it from a man who heard it off the gardener's boy, and he stood in the yard in his coat and said it in about ninety seconds and then he asked whether we wanted him to say it at a council meeting and I said no."

"Why did you say no?"

"Because he did not offer," said Ampow. "He offered and I said no, and there is a difference, and I would have been within my rights to have said yes, and it would have cost the ward nothing to say yes, and I have been turning it over since Saturday and I would have been a fool to spend it."

It was the correct decision and Neris did not argue with it, because it was not hers either.

What she had done on the Sunday, on the back of the fourth flyleaf of her own notebook, without telling anybody, was write the mechanism out in eleven lines and the warden's authority in the twelfth, and the twelfth line said: *a warden may close a street, empty a school, order a crew out and requisition a boat on her own signature, with no forecast, no claim and no counter-signature, and it costs the ward a morning of wages, and it is never once entered anywhere as a cost because nothing happened in the sky.*

Nine days of it. Nine days of a warden closing streets and emptying two schools and sending crews out at two in the morning to clear catch pits on a rota with four slots in it and one man who filled two of them.

And the answer to the answer, the thing under the mechanism, the thing that a council would ask next: if the ward is on a rota, and the ward runs on a rota, and the rota is a woman called Marda Loon telling four men when to go out with shovels, and the ward has been on that rota for nine days, then why is there water in the streets?

And the answer to that was: because a rota of four slots is a rota for clearing catch pits, and a full line does not care what is in your catch pits, because the water is not in the catch pits. The water is in the line, and it came down the terraces above in a week of rain and there is nowhere for it to go, and the line that should take it out is under the market at Hearthward and is as full as this one, and has been since the seventh.

Neris Kade had a district of four hundred and forty people standing in two inches of water that would not go, and eleven lines in a notebook that explained it perfectly, and a document in a building on the ridge that had just been reclassified in a level hand as an internal district record and not a public notice.

---

The reply from Malle had come on the twenty-fifth, at about ten in the morning, and it was four lines long, and it was correct, and it had cost her the thing she had made the list for.

*M. Kade, district warden, ninth ward. — The five lines are received. They are an accurate and, in my view, valuable record. They are not a public notice and cannot be published as one, and they do not call for any action by this office, and under the Charter of Public Notice they are an internal district record, which is to say that they are now a document in this building and not a document in yours, and I am obliged to tell you that as a matter of plain practice rather than of anything you have done. You asked for it to be read. It has been read. — C. Malle, Deputy for Field Forecasts.*

Read.

It had been read by a man in a room on the ridge in about ninety seconds and had been put in a tray on the corner of a desk on top of two other trays, and the five lines in a warden's handwriting were now a piece of paper in a building with a locked drawer in it, and a district could no longer produce its own record of its own omissions because the omissions were now filed, and filing is what institutions do to things so that they can be found later by somebody who is not the person who wrote them.

She had known this would happen. She had said the words *I will not pass it to anybody who has to pay for it* on a piece of paper to a captain, and she had known at the time that the Council's slots were free and the yard's board was not, and she had chosen the board because the board had people on it.

The thing she had not known, and had not had any way of knowing at twenty-two years old when she started the page, was what it would feel like to be right five times on a piece of paper and to have it be correct.

Nobody had refused her. Nobody had argued. The list had been received, read, and correctly filed, and the cost of it was that it had left her hands, and she had paid the four hundred and forty people of Low Spindle nothing and had paid herself the entire thing, and she stood in a district office at ten in the morning on the twenty-fifth of the month and understood that the thing she had been calling collecting grievances had in fact been building a record, and that a record is a thing somebody eventually has to be responsible for.

---

She rang the Weatherglass at half past eleven because she wanted one thing and she knew exactly what it was and she had known for a week that she was not going to get it and was going to ask for anyway.

"I want a maintenance hand," she said, when the field office answered. "Not a reader. Somebody who knows a pump gland and a seat ring. I've got a third pump with a cracked gland and a full line and I want a second opinion on the line from somebody who is not in this argument."

"Vale," said the field office, without a pause, in the voice of a man who has been asked to fetch something from a shelf. "Marek Vale. He's off the field list but he's in the shop all day and he knows more about glands than anybody in the building including me. Are you sending a docket or shall I?"

"Neither," said Neris Kade. "I'll walk him down there myself. Tell him it's the ninth ward and tell him it's a hole and a lamp and about ninety seconds, and tell him he can say no."

---

He came at half past two and he came on foot, up Tannery Walk and down Sump Lane, in a good coat, with no docket, which she noted, and with a lamp in his hand, which she also noted.

They went down to the outfall first because it was on the way to the pump house and because the fourth block was holding.

"You've been in this hole," said Neris.

"On the Thursday," said Marek. "You weren't here. I was holding the lamp for a man with a bar."

"He told me it in a yard in ninety seconds on Saturday and my sergeant wrote it down." She stopped at the head of the steps. "I'm not asking you about the pipe, Mr Vale. I already know about the pipe."

"Then what are you asking me about?"

"The pump," said Neris Kade.

---

The third pump house was a brick hut at the end of a cut with a barometer on the wall that had a cracked gland and a new one in it that had been taken out of a shed in Ward Six in a hurry on the morning of the tenth, and the whole of it was doing the work of three and Ampow had it at a rate he refused to write down because he had no figure for it.

They stood in there for about eleven minutes. Marek went over the gland with his fingers and did not take anything apart. He put his hand in the discharge elbow and held it there. He got down on one knee and looked up under the volute by the light of the lamp, and he said one number out loud, twice, and asked her what it was against, and when she told him he said *right* and nothing else.

Then they went out into the cut and he stood with the lamp hooked on his finger and looked at the water in the street for a while.

"Ninety seconds," said Neris Kade. "I told them ninety seconds and you have already taken twenty-two."

"Right." He straightened up. "The gland is sound. I did not expect that and I would have told you in an hour if I had. The gland is sound and the pump is sound and the pump is making about a tenth more than it was making on the seventh and the volute is clean and the delivery is clean and there is nothing wrong with any part of this pump house that a wrench would fix."

"I know that."

"Then you have not asked me anything, warden, and you have walked me down a hill to tell me that."

"I have asked you," said Neris Kade. "And I have got the same answer, and I got it on Saturday from a man who is sixty-one and has been saying it for eleven years to anybody who would stand still, and my sergeant was sent to him through a market gardener because that is the only route by which anything gets to that man. And I have now had it twice."

"Yes," said Marek.

"So you can go."

"No," he said. "Wait. I am going to say the other thing, and then you can decide what it is worth, and I am saying it because you have walked me down a hill and because you asked me for a second opinion, which is not the same as asking me for an answer, and the difference is the entire reason I have come."

---

He said it in about ninety seconds, standing in eight inches of brown water in a cut off the fourth block, in a good coat, with somebody else's lamp.

"A full line is not a fault. I want to be exact, because this is the thing I get wrong and I have got it wrong in public twice. A surcharged line is a fault in the sense that it is a pressure standing in a pipe, and a surcharged line clears when the inflow stops. A full line is not that. A full line is a line that is at the crown from the ordinary run of the district and has been for days, and it will take nine days of rain and give nothing back, and the water is not in your catch pits and it is not under your pump and it never was, and every hour you spend clearing a grate is an hour you have not spent on the only question, which is where the water is *coming from* and when it stops coming."

"Yes," said Neris Kade. "That is what Marda's gardener's boy heard off your father."

Then she waited, and let it sit, and then asked him the question she had actually walked down the hill for.

"Mr Vale. If two men in this city say a thing in the same words, does it become a different kind of fact?"

---

Marek Vale stood in a cut off the fourth block of the ninth ward and did not have an answer ready, and the not having one ready took about four seconds, and she watched it happen.

"No," he said. "It does not."

"No," said Neris. "That is what I thought and I wanted it said out loud by somebody who is not me, because I have been sitting in an office for two days with eleven lines in a notebook and a reclassification, and I have got to the stage where I keep thinking that if the thing is written down in two places it is harder for a building to lose. And it is not, is it. Two copies in two buildings is two documents. It is still an assertion. There is nothing in this district and nothing in yours that can be shown, and if the man on the ridge has never had a branch in his life, then it is just a true remark by a man in a wet waistcoat, and the wet waistcoat is the problem."

"It is a very large part of the problem," Marek said.

"I know it is. I have a man on my own books who I cannot get to speak in a room." She took the lamp off his finger. "That is my problem and it is not yours and I have used about twenty minutes of your afternoon on it, and I am going to say thank you and mean it, and then I am going to ask you the last thing, and then I am going to let you go."

"Ask."

"You read. The office says so, and the ninth ward has been told so by four separate people in four weeks and has chosen to believe it, and I have a plate in a coat somewhere and I have never seen it, and I have not asked you for it and I am not going to." She looked at him steadily. "If I sent you a notice with a place and an hour on it, and asked you to put your name under it, would you do it?"

---

The rain came into the lamp and hissed on the flame for a moment and neither of them moved.

"No," said Marek.

"No," said Neris Kade. "Right. Thank you. That is the first straight answer anybody in that building has given me since the ninth."

"Because I have no chain," he said. "That is the whole of the reason and I am not going to dress it up."

"I know you have no chain. That is not why." She started up the steps out of the cut. "You have no chain and you also have a reason. You are the only person in that building who has ever said *I am not sure* out loud in a room with a witness in it, and if you put your name under a notice for me you would stop being able to do that, and Low Spindle would be worse off, and so would I, and I would have spent a morning of my own people's wages to get a glass in a tube that nobody can check. So: no. And I would have taken it, and it would have cost me less than the school morning did, because the school morning was obvious."

He did not answer that. There was nothing to answer it with, and she had not been expecting an answer, and she went up the steps and turned round at the top.

"Two inches," she said. "And it will not go. And the third pump is sound. And the line is full. And it has been full since the seventh. And I have known all four of those things since Saturday and the district has not moved a man."

"Then why not?"

"Because none of those four things is a forecast," said Neris Kade, "and this district has been waiting eleven years for somebody to send it one, and it has not occurred to anybody here — including me, most days — that a thing can be true and known and cost nothing to obtain and still be worth nothing at all."

---

She went back to the office and put the lamp down on the table in the district office on the corner of Sump Lane and stood for a moment in front of the board where Sable Orin's card was still pinned from the eighth of the month, with a fifth line on a ruled page in a locked drawer, and a reclassification in a building on the ridge.

The two inches was still in the street.

Somewhere out over the basin, forty miles west, a body of weather that was not a front and had no collision in it any more was sitting on a lake and pulling air, and the air it pulled was the air that would hold a corridor over the water, and the same air was the air that kept two inches of water in a district of four hundred and forty people exactly where it was, and the low ground had now had nine days of it and was going to have a good deal more, because the low ground did not care what anybody forecast and neither, in the end, did a full pipe.

Neris Kade wrote the twelfth line under the eleventh, and it said: *nine days. nothing has gone into any column. I am not going to be able to make anybody pay for this, and I am not going to be able to stop it either, and both of those are the same document.*

Then she stood the pumps, and cleared the catch pits on a rota with four slots in it and one man who filled two of them, and went out into the water in her own boots, and did not sleep much, and had not yet worked out that the worst of it was still four days away and would arrive in a night with nothing in the sky over the ridge to look at.
