# Chapter 5 — The Third Watch

What Anwen Sarr wanted, in the end, was a piece of paper with a number on it that she could hold against the Hearthward assembly on the following morning and say *the Office told us this and we acted*.

She had come up the Weatherglass steps in the rain without an umbrella and had said so, in the first thirty seconds, in a way that told Marek she had decided something about the conversation before she had arrived. She was forty-six and thin and had a way of standing that put her hip against the bench so that a person could not lean over her, and she had brought three people: a younger man with a twenty-year ledger, a girl of about nineteen with wet hair who turned out to be the terraces' water steward, and a fourth man who stood at the back with his cap in his hands and said nothing at all and turned out to matter most.

"I am not going to argue with you about the weather," Anwen said. "You work at it and I have a market to open in it. I want three things and I have until the middle watch. I want to know what you are going to publish. I want the thing you are least sure of, written down where I can point at it. And I want somebody's name on it that is not my assembly's."

"Your assembly's name is the right name on it," Marek said.

"That is the first thing anyone from the ridge has said to me in six years that I believe," said Anwen, "and I would still rather it was yours."

The rain had come on hard while he was on the tram and it was still coming. Marek had the strip of sequence glass out of the tube and no instrument chain to put behind it, because he had never in his life been to the Hearthward terraces and had no idea what the outfall looked like or what the ground did under a full surcharge, and he had spent the walk up the ridge constructing a version of himself who did not need one.

He was not going to get an unconditional claim. He knew that in his bones, and Malle had known it on the stairs on Wednesday, and the reason was structural rather than political: an unconditional claim is a promise about a system, and a promise about a system needs a chain, and he did not have a chain on Hearthward, and Malle would not put him in a room without one, and Malle was right, and there was no version of this that produced a paper with his name on it before the middle watch unless the paper had a door in it.

A conditional claim was a door. That was the whole difference and it was an enormous one. A conditional forecast said *if this is done, then that follows*. It was not weaker. It was a different shape, and it put the decision where it belonged, in the hands of the people who were going to have to live with the consequence, and it did that not out of generosity but out of arithmetic: he had nothing to promise, so he had to ask.

"Anwen Sarr," he said. "I can give you a condition and I cannot give you a promise. If your assembly keeps the terraces' upper spill gate shut and does not open the lower outfall through the leading rain, then the stored water holds to the third watch, the upper and middle rows stay dry, and the lower row drains clear. That is the whole of what I am prepared to put my name on."

"And if we do not accept it?"

"Then you get nothing, and you get it in six hours, and the ridge's published window for your water is the same as every other window in this city, which is a word with a number under it and no name on it."

The girl with the wet hair — the water steward — made a small sound. The young man with the ledger had it open and was not writing.

"Ask the last part again," Anwen said.

"The lower row drains clear."

"That is the part you are least sure of."

"Yes."

"Then say why you put it in."

Marek could have lied. He had a whole shelf of competent ways to lie about an instrument's reach and he was tired and it would have worked on anybody but a woman who had spent six years refusing forecasts. So he told her the truth, which is what he had not planned to do.

"Because the water has to go somewhere, and the only place it can go is the outfall, and the outfall's capacity is a published figure, and if you take the design load off the upper rows at full pitch the outfall clears it in about three hours. I have not been on those terraces. I have no instrument chain on Hearthward. I have got a rain figure off the tower feed and a catchment area off a ward sheet and a run-time I can do in my head, and every one of those three assumes the drains are clear, because that is what a run-time is. It is a calculation about drains, not about a district."

"Somebody clears them," the water steward said. "The rain-garden crew. Ferren's lot, and he is standing behind me, and he has not said a word in four minutes."

"And when?"

"Tuesdays," the steward said. "And Thursdays."

Marek wrote it on the pad on top of the tube. Tuesdays and Thursdays. It was the first time anybody had given him a piece of Hearthward's actual life and he wrote it down the way he wrote down plate numbers, and Anwen watched him do it, and he did not turn round, because there was a claim to get into a shape and a hall to read it out of and the man had not been introduced and there were a hundred other things to do.

"Well?" she said. "Now what?"

"Now you accept the condition or you don't, and either way I write it down and the bell goes and the tower sends a crew, and the condition is yours, not mine, and that is the only reason I can offer it at all." He put the pad down. "I will list the alternative, which is opening the lower outfall in the second hour of the rain and taking the market's ground floor instead of the terraces' lower row, and it is a worse trade and I would not advise it, and I am putting it on the sheet so that the choice is written down in both directions and nobody gets to say afterward that there was only the one thing on the table. I will put an expiry on it at the third watch, and the expiry is the end of my authority to send anybody anywhere, and it does not un-hold the water. And the name on it will be mine, and not the assembly's, because you asked for a name that is not yours and because the sponsor is the person who is answerable for having made the claim, and that is me."

The girl with the wet hair was looking at him as though she had not expected a man on a ridge to talk like that. The man behind her said:

"The alternative is the market."

"The market," Anwen confirmed, "which holds on a Thursday."

"It's Thursday," the man said, level as a table, "and the outfall's under the market, and if you put the terraces' water down there on a Thursday the market's ground floor is where it goes, and I have stood in that water twice."

"Then it's a worse trade," Marek said, "and I've said so on the sheet."

"You have." The man did not move. "You said it before I said the reason."

"Ten hours," Marek said, and heard himself step straight over the place where a question should have been. "Ferren clears them tonight, at the first hour, before the rain. Tell him the rain figure I've got, and if it runs long he'll have seen it coming."

"I'll clear them," the man said.

"I'll tell him," said the water steward, in a voice that was not unkind and was not forgiving either. "I have told him every Tuesday and every Thursday for three years without being asked, and before that it was the water board's standing order and not mine, and before that it was Ferren's father."

Anwen Sarr looked at the four of them and then at the window, where the rain was coming down in a sheet that made the yard crane disappear.

"The assembly accepts the condition," she said. "Gates stay shut, outfall stays shut, and Ferren clears the drains tonight at the first hour. And I want the alternative written down exactly as you said it, in that order, in the same hand as the rest, and I want the expiry in front of the signature and not underneath it." She was already unbuttoning the wet coat. "And you will stand in front of that hall and read it out, because if it is going to be in this assembly's name as well as yours, the people who are going to be standing in water at four in the morning are entitled to hear whose idea it was."

---

The public forecast hall smelled of wet coats, hot pipes, and eighty people who had come to be told something they would rather not hear. It was long and low and built for about forty, and the acoustics were such that a man reading at the front could be understood at the back in a dead calm and not at all in a storm, and there was a storm coming, and by eleven o'clock there were people standing at the back who could not hear and had to be shouted at by their neighbours, which is how a hall like that loses a room.

Malle was at the side with a folder under his arm. He did not stand in front. He had been in the field for thirty years and he had the particular gift of making it plain that a thing was happening without appearing to be the cause of it.

"Conditional," he said, when Marek had shown him the sheet. "Place, condition, effect, alternative, expiry, sponsor. That's a claim. I can't certify it and it doesn't need certifying, because nobody's paying for it. The steward accepted the condition, so it is live from the moment it is entered, and it will bind at the first hour tonight, when the gate is shut and the rain is on it and there is no third party left to refuse. If it's wrong, the sponsor's name is on it and the sponsor is you, not the ward and not the Deputy."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've watched a man's whole career go in the eight weeks after a claim of his goes bad, and the Office doesn't do anything to him, Vale, the Office just stops mentioning him." Malle took the sheet and gave it back. "It's a good claim. It's tight. The alternative's in the right place, which is more than I can say for most of what goes through this hall. Publish it and get out of the way. You've got four hours to be somewhere else."

Then he was gone, and Marek stood in the middle of a public hall with eighty people in it and the single most consequential piece of paper he had ever held, and the strange and unfamiliar relief of it came up through him like warm water: it was not his problem any more. Somebody else was going to act on it. Ferren was going to clear drains, the tower was going to send a crew, a water steward was going to watch a gate, and if it all went wrong it would be because the water was heavier than he had calculated and not because he had been slow.

He had never felt that before. In eleven years he had felt fear and irritation and the small clean pleasure of a plate that behaved. He had never felt the particular warmth of *this is now somebody else's to do*, and he noticed it, and he noticed that it felt good, and he did not examine it. He would not examine it for another two days, and by the time he did there would be six rooms under water and a man with a shovel on a step who could not account for his evening, and he still would not call it that.

He walked back down to the ward ledger bench at a quarter past eleven with the tube under his arm and the sheet in his hand, and Tovan Iles had the emergency book out of the cabinet, which she had not had out in four years, and the sight of it on the bench was somehow the most frightening object in the building.

"It's the first one," she said.

"Yes."

"There's a line in the old ink about a ferry."

"Leave it," said Marek. "We'll go underneath it."

The strip of sequence glass was lying on the bench where he had put it, and the lamp over the bench was the only lamp in the room, and as he put his hand down flat on the bench beside the sheet to sign the sponsor line, the glass under the light did something it had never done in three days of carrying it around.

The trace was the same. The order was the same. And across the middle of it, in the plain block letters that a plate makes when it is telling a reader the price of a thing instead of the price of an observation, five lines stood out.

```
CLAIM: the spill gate holds shut — stored water to the third watch, upper and middle rows dry, lower row drains clear.
ONE POSSIBLE TURN WILL CLOSE.
```

He read it twice. It did not say which turn. It did not say whose. It gave him the exact words he had just agreed to sign and told him, in the flattest possible voice, that one of the things still available in the world was about to stop being available because he was making this true.

"Mr Vale?" said Tovan Iles, with her pen up.

"Write it," Marek said. "Exactly as I read it to you. And put the emergency entry above it with my name as author, and the steward as the accepting witness, and the expiry in front of the signature."

His mouth had gone to metal. It was the first time the taste had come and it was not going away, a coin held between the teeth that he could not get rid of by swallowing, and behind his left ear a pressure that had been there for three days had come up half a tone, and it stayed there while he dictated, and he noticed it, and he filed it, because there was a bell to strike and an entry to make and a hall full of people who had come to hear a number.

Tovan wrote it. It took her ninety seconds because she wrote the way she was taught, in one hand, without lifting the pen except at the end of a line, and when she came to the sponsor line she turned the sheet so that it faced him, which was the last kind thing anybody did for him that day.

He signed it. She countersigned it as the entering clerk. Then she reached over and pulled the brass arm of the entry bell and struck it twice, once for the yards' ward head, because the upper tanks on those terraces feed the freshwater header at Kestrel, and once for Hearthward, and the flat sound went out into a hall full of wet coats and was swallowed whole, because a bell for a ward is an ordinary thing and happens about nine times a week and nobody in the building looked up.

The strike arm dropped. Along the wall, two ward heads came up and stayed up, one clear for Hearthward and one clear for the yards, and the ninth ward's head did nothing at all, which it had not done for a fortnight, because the ninth ward's pane was a temporary with a bubble in it and that ward had not been able to publish a legible state to anybody since the middle of the month.

Tovan opened the emergency ledger, found the page, and wrote the first line that had been written in that book in four years. Then she squared the book against the edge of the bench with the flat of her hand, which she had learned from a records clerk named Pettigrew, and which meant, in the grammar of that bench, that the thing was now in the world and could not be got back.

"The lower row drains clear," she said, reading it back. "That's the part you're least sure of."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Then I'd better be the one who copied it," said Tovan Iles, "because if it turns out to be the part that was wrong, I would like it to be in my hand and not a stranger's."

And then Marek went up to the hall and read it out, twice, to a room he could not see the back of, and the people at the back shouted the words to each other, and by the time he had read it the second time there were more people in that hall than had ever stood in it for a forecast, and Malle had gone, and the rain had started, and everybody in the room was satisfied, and every one of them went out into it and did something sensible.

He meant to go down and see it himself. He got as far as the stair, and then a man from the Kestrel wards wanted the wording of the condition because he had people with tanks on the upper header who needed to know whether to draw down before the first hour, and that was a real question, and answering it properly took eleven minutes, and by the time Marek was outside the door with his coat on it was twenty past twelve and the rain was coming down in earnest and there was no practical version of walking seven miles down to Hearthward and back in it to look at a gate he had just been told would hold.

He told himself that a man who could not trust his own arithmetic at half past midnight on a wet road was not much use to anybody. It was true, and it was the reason he did not go, and it was not the whole reason.

The relief of the evening was so enormous that Marek had to stand at the side of the hall with his hand flat against a stone column and let it go through him.

Because the trace on the bench had already finished. The whole of the second turn had completed and gone, and there was nothing left in the glass but the record of a movement that had stopped happening some time before he had started looking at it, and he looked at it, and he knew it was over, and he could not make his eyes leave it.

It took him a long moment to turn away. He put it down to the lamp.
