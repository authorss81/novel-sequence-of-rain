# Chapter 15 — Tannery Walk

The Ninth Ward tower is a brick shaft on the corner of Tannery Walk and Sump Lane with a copper tank on top that has been repaired eleven times, and it has a ladder in it instead of a stair, and the second landing is where the feed seat is, under a copper tank that sweats so badly in a cold season that there is a permanent brown stain on the brick shaped like the country it was imported from.

Marek came up the ladder at ten past nine in the morning on the thirteenth day with a tool roll and a sheet of Office stock wrapped in newspaper and a docket in his pocket, and the keeper came down the first two rungs to meet him, which is what a keeper does.

"Morning, Gant."

"Morning." Gant Ollis was sixty-three or sixty-four and had been the keeper of this tower for eleven years, and he had the particular calm of a man who is left alone in a building for long enough that he has stopped expecting the building to be fair. "You'll want the light off the stair."

"I want the book," said Marek.

"There's no book."

"Gant."

"There's the weather book and there's the glass book and the glass book's got nothing in it since March and what's in it from March is weather." Gant Ollis came down two more rungs and sat on the landing with his lamp between his boots. "You didn't come up here to look in a book. You came up here to put copper round a temporary and cut a plate I haven't had in two years, and I should be delighted, and I'd like to be able to say afterwards that I asked you a question about the plates on Friday morning and you answered it."

"I'd remember you asking."

"I know you would," said Gant Ollis. "That's why I have not asked."

They sat with that for about a minute and a half, and then Marek unpacked the tool roll on the second landing, and Gant Ollis got up and went down four rungs and stayed there, which is where a keeper stands while somebody works in a tower.

---

The surround was the worst of it and he had known it would be.

What a permanent pane needs is a bed. Glass this size sits in a rebate with a soft bed under it, in mastic, with a brass surround screwed to a frame that has been set true for four hundred years of weather, and the surround takes the load and gives the glass an even edge to die on. A temporary does not need a bed. A temporary wants a wooden batten, four screws, and a bucket of something out of a tin, because a temporary is a thing you fit in an afternoon in bad light in the rain so that the frame is not an empty hole for another month.

There was a batten. The batten had a bead of old mastic under it that had gone hard and black, and the temporary sat in it with a fingernail's width of daylight on the outboard side, which meant that in a westerly it would sing.

Marek took the batten off. He took four years of weather off the rebate with a chisel and a cloth and patience. He dressed the old mastic back to sound metal, one flake at a time, because the alternative is a new surround that fails in a year and gets blamed on the new surround.

Then he got his own surround out of the roll, which he had cut and threaded and bedded in the shop on the Monday night and had brought up the hill on a bus, and it was brass and it was the only good thing in that tower.

He set it in dry, and squared it, and it went square the second time he offered it up, which is not a thing that happens.

While he worked he looked at the frame's rebate, and the rebate told him the story he had already known. There were two sets of screw holes in the iron: an old pair, counter-sunk and rust-brown, and a newish pair from a temporary's batten. The old pair was the permanent's. The old pair had been in that frame since the tower went up, and the glass that had gone into it had been in it since the autumn, and the glass that came out of it had been, at the end of last year, quoted, ordered, and paid for by a ward that could not collect it, and had been sitting in a shop at Kestrel Yards ever since, and the Office had sent nobody, because sending a man forty miles with a crate of glass to a ward tower costs a day's wages out of a district's glasswork allocation, and the district's glasswork allocation does not cover that in a year like this one.

There was money-shaped reason and there was no reason at all and they were the same reason, and it was the most ordinary thing in the world, and it had a thumbnail-sized flaw in the middle of it, and there were four hundred and forty people in the lowest four blocks and four women with good handwriting.

"Mr Vale," said Gant Ollis, from four rungs down.

"Mr Ollis."

"That's the best piece of brass I have seen in this building and I've been in it eleven years." Gant came up one rung. "Where is it going to be in ten years' time?"

"That depends entirely on the water office," Marek said.

"Yes," said Gant Ollis, and went down the ladder to let him work.

---

The spare plate took the rest of the morning and most of the afternoon and it was the real work.

There is no spare in that tower. There has not been a spare in that tower for two years, and the reason there is no spare is that a spare plate is a nine-inch square of sequence glass sitting in a box doing nothing, which is the definition of a waste of money to anybody reading a budget, and so the ninth ward's glasswork line had gone to a nil return for two consecutive years and a tower that has had a feed go down in it once is a tower that is finished, because a feed that goes down takes the ward's own sequence with it and the ward's sequence is the only instrument in that district that has ever been able to tell the district anything at all.

Marek cut the plate on the second landing, which he had done four years' worth of times and which was the only flat surface in that tower above six feet, using a bench block he had carried up in a sack.

He marked the line in chalk on two faces and checked the marks against each other by holding the sheet up to the light from the stairwell, because a scribe that walks off a line puts a crescent through the work, and a crescent in a tower's feed is a reading that nobody can see and everybody has to act on.

He scored it. It made the wasp-in-a-jar sound for eleven seconds and then stopped, because the score was through. He turned the sheet over, scored the second face, and then wrapped it in the blanket from his roll and broke it, and it broke along the line on both faces, clean enough that it needed only a light kiss on the edge.

He ground it on the wheel block for an hour and ten minutes and it came up square under a thumbnail, and then he did the rest of a plate-maker's afternoon, which is the part that takes the longest and is the part that makes it worth anything: the edge run, the arris, the fine polish on the ground face, and then a slow anneal in a dish of hot sand with a lamp held at a distance and counted out loud, because counting out loud is the only way anybody has ever found to be honest with an anneal.

At about two in the afternoon he took it out of the sand and it was cool enough to hold and he put it in a wooden box with a folded paper and wrote on the paper, in his own hand, the tower, the date, and the word *spare*.

He did not fit it. A spare plate lives in a box in a tower for the day it is needed, and that was the whole point of it, and if you fit it you have spent it.

"You've cut one," said Gant Ollis from the bottom of the ladder.

"I've cut one."

"In this building. In eleven years." Gant Ollis came all the way up and looked at the plate in its box, and did not touch it. "They'll want it in the feed."

"They'll want it in the feed in about four years," Marek said, "and I'll have to be somewhere else by then, and somebody will have to have the skill." He put the box on the shelf under the seat. "That's not modesty. That's a real problem and I want it written in the book."

"Then write it in the book."

"I'm not writing anything in the book, Gant."

"No," said Gant Ollis. "You are, in fact, not."

---

He did the feed seat last, and he did it because a seat ring that has been wet for years and then dried has a habit of coming out of true, and a seat out of true is a feed that fogs at the wrong hour and gives a ward a plate it cannot read.

It was a small job. Thirty minutes with a spanner and a lapping plate and a smear of grease. He took the ring up, cleaned it, lapped it flat, and set it down, and the fact that it needed doing meant that somebody would have to keep doing it, and that somebody was going to be a man with a docket in a coat, and Marek stood in the middle of the tower for a moment and let the whole of his working life arrive at him without comment.

Then he was alone in front of the plate.

It had been in its seat since ten o'clock on Friday night. He had carried it up the ladder in the dark, put it in cold, written nothing, and said nothing, and inside twenty minutes the ninth ward's own feed had come back up on the Office's glass, and it had been running ever since, and it was now the only instrument in the city that had recorded the collision in full.

There it was.

He put his hand on the frame the way he would on any instrument and read it standing up, the way you read a pressure trace: down through the whole of it, then up through the whole of it again, and then once more with the frame cold, because a man who reads a plate once has read a mood.

Five days. Friday night's drainage, the whole of Saturday, the collision at about six, the eleven minutes of Release, the Witness out of order on top of the Break, the flat green light in the west, and then the drainage behind it — the basin emptying itself at the low end for two days and stopping on Sunday, which is when the low ground's drains and the low ground itself agree, and which is the single most useful line on the entire plate, and which nobody in the world could be told about by Marek Vale because he had a piece of glass in a locked room in a poor ward's tower and no chain of any kind.

He could have drawn it. He had a pencil in his coat and a folding rule and a sheet of bench paper in the roll, and there is no faster way in the world to make a copy of a sequence trace than to put a rule across it and copy the lines, and it would have taken him about forty minutes, and the copy would have been exact, and it would have been in his coat by four o'clock.

He did not do it.

He stood there for a while and let the not-doing be deliberate, which was new, because every other thing he had failed to do that week had happened to him. There was a version of this afternoon in which he went down the ladder and unlocked the tower's glass book and wrote the collision's order in it in his own hand, dated, with a plate number, and it would have been a record; it would have been the first instrumented record of a Bellweather collision's order in the history of the basin, and it would have been in a building with a door and a keeper and a date on it.

He knew exactly what that would look like to anybody who came along in twelve years.

So he did not do that either. And the reason he did not do that either was different from the reason he had not copied the trace, and the difference was that the first was about him and the second was about the ward, and both of them ended with his hand off the plate.

Gant Ollis was at the bottom of the ladder with a lamp when he came down, and the keeper did not ask him what he had read, and Marek did not say, and the arrangement held.

---

He put the tools away and had his coat on and was sitting on the bottom step with the roll across his knees when Gant Ollis said:

"Your father worked that water."

Marek stopped.

"Not the tower," said Gant. "The water. He was in the ninth ward's outfall run for about nine years, before I came, and I have the old run sheets up here because I keep everything, and half of them are in his hand and half of them are in the hand of a man called Ilves who was his mate and who was worse at it." He nodded up the shaft. "That's where the low ground goes, is what that is. Whatever's in the ninth ward's glass, it's reading the run your father used to be paid to look after."

"What is his name on the sheets."

"Oren Vale," said Gant Ollis. "And there's about a decade of a man's arithmetic in that book, and it's better arithmetic than mine, and I'm not going to be the one who tells him that."

Marek sat on the bottom step of a tower on Tannery Walk for about half a minute and did not say anything at all.

Then he got up, and put the roll under his arm, and thanked the keeper, and went out into a Wednesday afternoon in the eleventh week of the best autumn this city had had in nine years, with a tube in his coat and no spare in the tower and a brass surround around a temporary, and walked down Tannery Walk toward Sump Lane with no particular reason at all for walking that way.

He was two streets from a kitchen.
