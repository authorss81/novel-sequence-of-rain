# Chapter 24 — A Column of Differences

The column of differences was eight days old on the Sunday morning of the twenty-fourth and it had been chalked up, ruled, and copied out every single one of those days, and in eight days it had produced exactly one thing anybody in the trade had ever heard of.

They had it on a board by the route office door. Two columns, the Office's figure and the Line's, the hour of each, the date on each, and a third column headed *difference* which had been four seconds wide in one direction on the seventeenth and had been nothing at all on the twentieth.

"That day," said Ilva Sorn, tapping the third column with a pencil, "is the whole return on this board."

"The twentieth," said Nessa Tarrow, from the doorway, with two sheets in her hands. "They were the same. Both of them, same hour, and the Office's was late to the yard by about a quarter of an hour, which is inside their own margin."

"I know what it says. I have been looking at it since Friday." Ilva Sorn put the pencil down. "I want it on the record that I am not claiming it as a victory. It is one day out of eight, in a column, on a board, in a yard."

"It is the only day anybody in this basin can be shown the Office being right about a corridor," said Tarin Senn, from the window. "I have been waiting nine days for somebody to notice that we had accidentally built the Office's alibi and were charging them for it."

---

The yard had three ships out and two of them were the wrong way round a return, and there were four licensed carriers and two insurers holding paper that had been priced on the fifth day of the month against a corridor figure that the Office's own bulletin had made an hour and a half too long.

That is what the body of the front did to a yard. It did not close the trade. It made every instrument the trade was priced on a slightly wrong length, all at once, and then it did it again the next day.

Tarin put the sheet on the table and looked at it for a while.

The body had come off the lake at about four the previous afternoon and had been sitting on the basin ever since, and it was not a front any more. That was the thing that had taken the yard two days to say out loud and that nobody in the basin had said yet in a form a district could use.

A front arrives and it leaves. A front has a collision in it and the collision is the event, and everybody in the trade spends a fortnight preparing for the collision and a day recovering from it. What was sitting over Veyra on the twenty-fourth was the *body*: the wet, heavy, unglamorous remainder, hours and days wide, with a western draw in it that came off the lake in a long steady pull and did not let go.

And the western draw was the joint request.

She had read it on a board on the ridge on the ninth of the month and it had been two lines long and both lines had been correct, and the whole of the following fortnight had been an argument about whether anybody could put a claim on it. The lines said: the draw may be used either to hold the Kestrel corridor open or to ease the exposed floodplain of the lower basin, and these are the same air and they cannot both be had.

The body made that sentence into something a child could have worked out with a bucket.

The draw that opened a lane over the lake was the draw that lifted the low ground's water off its feet. The same pull of air came up the western side of the basin and went over the water and made a corridor, and the same pull of air was the thing that pushed water *up* out of a low reach instead of letting it run off, and the low wards did not care in the slightest about sequences or corridors or the six movements; they cared about the difference between an acre of standing water on the ninth day of a month and a week of it, and they got that difference by volume, and volume can be measured by anybody with a stick and a bucket and about forty minutes.

"Two things," said Tarin Senn. "One, this is not a sequence question any more and I am glad of it, because a sequence question needs a reader and I have a box that says how many hours of air there are in a lane. Two, and this is the part that matters: the low ground has been taking this for nine days. Nine days. It is not a channel and it is not a rainstorm, it is a basin with more water in it than it wants, and the thing that will take the water off the low ground is the same air that would hold my lane open, and the reason it is the same air is that it is the same basin."

Nobody in the route office needed that explained twice, and one of them needed it not explained at all.

"So the joint request is a bucket," said Ilva Sorn.

"The joint request has been a bucket since the ninth," said Tarin. "It just took a body of weather to make anybody else able to see it."

---

Maro Hesk came down the lane at about eleven with a carrier's man behind him and no case, which was new.

"I am not here to buy your board," he said. "I have read your board. I have had it read to me four times this week by four people who do not understand weather and every one of them came back with the same sentence, which is that the yard is publishing the Office's mistakes at cost."

"Your agents' licence does not require you to read it."

"My licence requires me to read everything," said Hesk pleasantly. "Here is the difficulty and I am going to state it plainly because you would rather have it plainly. A carrier's underwriter will not read a column. A carrier's underwriter reads a seal. I have a seal and it is the Office's and it is a quarter of an inch of stamped wax, and your eight days of beautifully kept arithmetic is a piece of paper with a date on it, and I cannot take it to an underwriter in Red Sill and get a premium back on the strength of it. If I tried, he would ask me what happens if the column is wrong, and I would have to say that the Line bears its own errors in public, and he would ask me by how much, and there is no answer, because *bear your own errors in public* is not an amount."

"That is the correct thing to say," said Tarin, "and I would like it written down that you said it in this yard in front of my route officer, and that it took you eleven days to work out."

"Eleven days and four carriers," said Hesk. "I am not a quick man, captain. I am a careful one." He turned round and looked at the board. "That is the thing I have been staring at since Friday, by the way. The twentieth. Two figures, same hour, no difference. Do you know what that is?"

"An accident."

"It is not an accident, it is a *day*," said Hesk. "You have found a day on which the Weather Office of this city said the right thing about a corridor at the right hour, and you published it, in a column, on your own board, on the day. Nobody upstairs has that. Nobody upstairs can have it, because the Office does not compare itself to a yard; it publishes a figure for a moving front and it does not keep a record of being right, because a building that keeps a record of being right has to keep a record of being wrong as well, and no institution on this ridge has ever voluntarily kept the second one."

Nobody in the lane said anything for a moment.

"So the yard has the alibi," said Hesk, "and I have a seal, and neither of us can use the other's thing, and four carriers have to decide this week whether to come back to a market that prices its weather off a board with no wax on it."

---

The *Petrel* went out at seven on the Sunday evening with the yard's three ships strung out behind her, and Tarin Senn took her because there was a lane to be in and the lane was hers, and because the four carriers' paper did not cover a *Petrel* at all and had never covered a *Petrel* at all.

She put the yard's own reading on the board before she went, with the hour on it, and Nessa Tarrow copied it out with the Office's figure beside it and the difference in the third column, and Ilva Sorn stood at the boards and watched her do it and did not say anything, because it was the third line and it was being honoured and there was nothing to add.

*Line's reading, western corridor, return: lane open from eight, closing by half past ten. Taken 18:40, Sunday the twenty-fourth, from the yard box, with the hour it was taken.*

Then she went out and got it.

The body had the draw in it and the draw came up the western side in a long band, and she could see the band from three hundred feet as a place where the lake went a different colour, and the *Petrel* went up into it at about a quarter past seven and sat in it, and the yard's three ships came round the point behind her and went down the lane one at a time at nine minutes' spacing.

The fourth ship — the one the *Petrel* was there for — came in at about six minutes before the hour Tarin's own box had said, and Tarin was under-sailed on the last two hundred yards because the wind that would have blown her mooring lines clear was not there.

She put that ship alongside by hand. It took her eleven minutes and it took the whole of the forward line hand to hand with the hull coming on sideways, and there is a seam in the *Petrel*'s after rigging that was not there on the Friday, and the yard's rigger looked at it on the Monday and did not say anything about it and wrote it down.

The other two were in behind her by twenty past eight. All four were alongside in the dark with the yard's lamps going and about forty people on the apron, and Tarin Senn came down off the *Petrel* with her own reading three hours earlier than the carriers' paper would have allowed, and every one of those people knew it, because it was on the board.

Ilva Sorn had the two figures written up before the last warp was off.

"Third column," she said. "Three hours and forty minutes."

"Three hours and forty minutes," said Tarin.

"You are uninsured."

"I am uninsured for the *Petrel*," said Tarin Senn, "and I have been uninsured for the *Petrel* since the fifth of this month, when I turned down a sealed window and flew it four hours early and lost a month of her overhead. What I have done tonight is put the yard's cover for the passage on the yard's own account in public, which is the only insurance product in this city that I have ever been able to control, and it is the reason four carriers have not walked tonight, and it will cost us one passage."

"And if the twenty-sixth goes like tonight?"

"Then we are wrong in public on the twenty-sixth as well," said Tarin, "and we will be wrong in public at some length over the next year, and that is what the proviso says and I signed it and the Line is the Line."

She stood on the apron in the cold with a rope in her hand and looked at the board, which was the only place in the city where the Office's figures had been checked, every day, for eight days, in public, by the people it was most convenient to be wrong about.

Then she went down the south bank, because there was a thing on her list that had been on her list for two years and had got longer.

The shed was open. The banded timber stack was under a tarpaulin that had not moved. The replacement dewatering pump was on its pallet with the Union guarantee on the drum and a cover over it. The notice board outside had been repainted four times and had been repainted in the autumn with a date on it, and the date was a week away, and there were about nine days a year when the low flat at the bottom of the reach would take a loaded flatbed and a pump.

Petrie was in the shed door with a lamp, as he had been on the Monday of this month when she had asked him the same question and been told the same answer.

"They're loaded," he said, before she asked.

"Still loaded."

"Still loaded, and nobody's stood them down, and nobody's cancelled it." He nodded out at the flat, at the mud with four inches of water standing on it. "It is not weather, captain. It never was weather. It is water, and water does not care what anybody forecast."

"I know," said Tarin Senn. "I have written it on a board."

---

She went back up the lane in the dark at about half past nine with her hands cold and a good day on the board and forty minutes of a corridor that had been there all along, and the thing she could not get out of her for the whole of the walk was not the wrist and not the three hours and forty minutes and not the fact that the yard had just paid, in public and on its own account, for an insurance product nobody sells.

It was that the basin had told her, in the plainest possible language, on the ninth of a month, in a two-line request that four people had signed, exactly what it was going to want in the last week of it.

And that the way to give it to the basin was to make a claim.

And that there was a good one available, and a captain on the west side of the lake who had put four conditions in chalk on a slate in front of a carrier's man to say exactly what a claim was allowed to do to her yard.

Tarin Senn walked up the lane and did not write any of it down, and the not-writing was the first thing she had ever done that she was going to be glad of.
