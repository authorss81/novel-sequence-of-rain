# Chapter 9 — The Arithmetic of a Correct Claim

At half past ten on day nine Marek Vale was in the chart room with a glazing block and a jar of warm gum, doing the only job in the building he was still trusted with, and doing it well, and thinking about the six hours in a way that was not the hours on a clock.

The figure had come up the ridge at nine: the front, nine hours. It had read eleven on Thursday and eight at ten o'clock last night, and it had moved all week, and Malle had stopped printing the range. Six hours was a number and he could do something with a number. Six hours meant the observation floor could be staffed, the low wards could be given a window, the yards could be given one, and he could be in a corridor with a tray of instruments looking useful at a time when the whole building was going to be looking for somebody to be useful.

What he wanted, and had wanted since six o'clock, was to be somewhere else.

The Weatherglass at ten forty in the morning is a place where a man in a wet coat can be extremely welcome for four minutes. Neris Kade was in the building. She had come up the hill with a warden and a satchel and a request, and she had been in and out of the records room twice, and the second time she had come out and looked down the corridor and found him, and she came straight at him the way a warden comes at a man holding something.

"Vale."

"Warden."

"You did the pane."

"I did the pane," Marek said, "and the crew have it on a docket in my name, and if you want to make a complaint there is a form and the form is short, and I would rather you made it than did not."

"I have made it." She was not angry. That was the difficulty. "I have made it in my own notebook, in my own hand, with the date, because I write everything down. Do you want to know what I wrote?"

"I would rather you did not tell me."

"I wrote: *Vale, M. — glass shop. Pane out since the sixth. Told the crew himself. Reason: own instrument. Did he know.*" She let that sit. "And then there is no answer under it, because I do not have one, and I have been a warden for five years and I have learned not to write down a thing as fact when it is a question. So it sits there being a question. You may go on with your day."

"Warden," Marek said, and stopped, because she had not asked for the pane. She had come three thousand steps up a hill to ask him for something else, and she was standing there waiting for it, and he had about two seconds in which to be a better man than he was.

"The low blocks," she said. "Four hundred and forty people, the front sitting on the ridge, and the Office's own number moved again this morning and nobody will tell me where it landed. I have a pump house and four crews and a school I emptied last night at my own cost, and all I have got of yours is the middle of a Bellweather bulletin that says *between the second and fourth watch*. I need a time."

Marek had a plate in a tube in the room behind him that would have given her a time.

He had cut it out of the crown of a pane her ward had paid for and never been given, on the Wednesday afternoon, nine inches square, ground edge, annealed, and it had been in the ninth ward's own feed seat since seven o'clock the previous evening, and it had the second turn on it — the collision, out of order, Release begun and not finished, and then two hours and a half of nothing in the middle of it, where the tower's own plate has the same nothing, and the tower's own plate is back up on Tannery Walk in its seat because a Deputy told him to put it back and say nothing. And if he put that plate on her district's glass and read her the low blocks' hour the way he had read it to himself two mornings ago, she would have had a number before lunch, and she would have had a number that was right, and the crews could have gone out on it.

"Why can't you?" she said, reading his face. "You have got something."

"I have a private plate with no chain behind it," Marek said. "If I give you a number off that and the number is right, then when somebody asks you in three weeks where the district's warning came from, you will have to say that a maintenance man read a piece of glass he cut out of your own notice, and the number will be worth nothing to you. And if the number is wrong, then it is a wrong number from an unlogged instrument, and you have spent your crews' night on it."

"That is the most careful thing anybody has said to me this week," said Neris, "and it is also an answer to a different question. I asked whether you have got something. You have not told me why you have got it."

"Because I have not got a chain," Marek said, and heard himself say the sentence he had been saying all week to people who were not asking about chains, and understood as it left his mouth that he had just described his entire career to a woman who had met him once at a flooded door and had never had any opinion about him at all.

Neris Kade looked at him for a moment longer than was comfortable and then took a pencil out of her coat and wrote four words on the cuff of her own glove and did not read them out.

"I have got a list," she said. "Four lines. It is going to be a great deal longer by the end of the week. When it is long enough I am going to send it to your Deputy, and it is going to say that the ninth ward cannot read its own notice. I am telling you that now so that you cannot tell me later that nobody warned you."

She went. Marek stood in the corridor with a glazing block in his hand and the taste of a coin in his mouth, and it was at that moment, in a corridor, on an ordinary morning, that he understood that he was afraid of something, and that the something was not a storm.

---

On the front steps of the Weatherglass a man in a mason's apron was on one knee with a shovel, clearing a blocked step.

It was not a strange sight on the ridge; the steps at the top of them backed up with leaf and silt every autumn and every office in the building walked over the resulting hazard for a fortnight. What was strange was that this man was not an office. He was a big man of about forty with the forearms of somebody who had moved stone for twenty years, and he had a paper folded inside his apron, and he had asked at the counter for the Deputy and been told that the Deputy was in a meeting, and had then gone outside and got on his hands and knees about four feet down the steps, where the water had got under the nosing and was working at the joint, and had gone on doing that for an hour without appearing to want anything.

"You've got water in a stone joint," Marek said. "That's a lime mortar joint and it wants pointing, not clearing. It'll take the water in again in a fortnight."

"Mm," said the man.

"I'm not telling you how to do your job. I'm telling you the step's going to fail at the nosing in about a fortnight because there's a void behind the stone, and the void's full of water now and it'll be full of ice in a week."

"Second time this year somebody's told me what my own step's going to do," said the man, and got the shovel under the stone anyway, and looked up. "You off the ridge?"

"Off the ridge."

"You'll be the one who put Hearthward's water in writing." Juba Tren said it without any particular emphasis, and went back to the stone. "That's all right. Somebody's got to. It's the worst job in the district and there's nobody else'll do it, and I'd rather it was a man off a ridge than it was nobody."

"I'm not anybody's. I'm a maintenance hand with a tube."

"Mm," said Juba Tren, and did not look up again.

Marek stood there a moment longer than he needed to. The man had come up a hill on the morning of a collision to say something to the Weather Office, and had been told to wait, and had waited, and had got out of the rain and done a job nobody had asked him to do, and had not once raised his voice or asked for anybody. There was a version of this morning on which the man said the obvious thing, and he was not going to get it, and something about the fact that he had made his own supply of it went with Marek up the stairs and sat down outside the glass room and would not go away.

That was the whole of his contribution to the morning. It was also, by a distance, the largest.

---

The assembly's clerk found him at eleven twenty and said that the chair of the Hearthward rain-garden assembly was in the public hall and had asked for him by name, and that she had brought somebody with her, and that she was not in a temper, which was worse.

Anwen Sarr had not slept and it did not show. She had a dry coat on and dry boots and a bundle of papers under her arm tied with a bootlace, and she stood in the middle of the public hall with the rain coming down on the skylight, and she did not sit down.

Ferren stood by the door with his cap in his hands. He looked like a man who had not understood what had happened to him and had now had a whole night to be told by other people what it was, and had believed about half of it.

"Mr Vale," said Anwen. "Thank you for coming. I am not going to make a speech. I have got five things and I want them answered, and I have written them down, and my clerk is writing this down, and I am going to read them out because I have read them out to myself four times and the reading is better."

"Please."

"One. The claim said the lower row drains clear. It did not drain clear for nine hours. The surcharge was above my ground floor from twenty-three forty, and there are six rooms on that row and I have been in all six and I know what is in them and what is not in them. And I would like somebody to explain to me, in a year, whether the words *drains clear* and *stops being under water eventually* are the same phrase, because my assembly agreed to the first one and got the second one, and I did not know at the time that there was a difference and neither did you."

"I know," said Marek.

"Two." She turned a page. "The claim was true. The gate held. The upper rows are dry. A hundred and sixty people went up those terraces before midnight on the strength of your sentence and they are all fine and the seed vaults on the high side are fine, and if you stand in my market hall and tell me the claim was a failure I will put you out of it myself."

"That is what I have been saying all morning."

"Then say it in a document, because I cannot say it in a document." She did not raise her voice and it was very clear in the hall. "Three. The drains on that row are cleared on a Tuesday and a Thursday by a crew of five and a foreman. They were cleared on Tuesday. They were not cleared on Thursday. My water steward told you that at eleven o'clock on the seventh day, in front of me, in a room you were standing in, and wrote it down, and you read it out to me from your own pad this morning when I asked you, and you did not tell me one thing about it until I had already worked it out from a man standing at the back of the room who had not said a word for forty minutes."

Marek did not answer that, because it was true, and there was no version of an answer that was not worse.

"Four." Anwen turned another page. "The claim did not say anything about the drains. It said: stored water to the third watch, upper and middle rows dry, lower row drains clear. And the lower row did drain clear, in the end, at eight o'clock, which is what you said would happen, and it drained in half an hour, and Neris Kade's warden on the low ground tells me that a surcharged outfall does not drain in half an hour. Not in any district. Not ever. It has got to be that the pipes were already empty when the water came up them."

The hall was very quiet.

"So either the drains were clear, and the crew I sent did clear them, and my water steward is wrong and Ferren is wrong and both of them have spent nine years on that row," said Anwen Sarr, "or the crew did not go out, and the drains were not cleared, and they could not have been, and I would like to know which, because one of those two is a lie about the weather and the other one is a lie about a man with a shovel, and I have spent five years in this district deciding which kind of thing I can live in the same room with."

Ferren said, from the door, in a voice with nothing in it: "I didn't go out."

Anwen did not turn round.

"Five," she said. "What did your claim require us to give up?"

The question came at Marek from six feet away with eighty people at the back of a long hall and a bootlace-tied bundle of paper and a man by the door who could not remember his own Thursday, and it was the correct question, and it was the question he had spent three days failing to answer, and there were two answers available to him and both of them were lies of a different colour.

The first was the mechanism. *A claim closes the turn that most directly contradicts it, and what it closed here was the weather outcome in which that row needed a crew.* And that was not only untrue, it was not even his to say, because it was a rule about sequence that he could not demonstrate, could not source, could not put on the ridge's own plate, and because a woman standing in front of him had a district behind her and was entitled to something better than a mechanic's theory.

The second answer was the one he was on the verge of giving, and he felt it forming, and it was smooth, and it was shaped exactly like the truth: *the claim kept the water, and the water was heavy, and Hearthward's terraces are the wrong place to hold a Bellweather, and the honest position is that you should not have accepted the condition and I gave you a door and you walked through it.* Which was a sentence about a woman's decision, delivered by a man who had spent three days privately believing that the door should not have been openable.

Marek Vale looked at the floor of the public hall and found the thing he had been doing all his life, which was to narrow a frightening problem until it became a procedure he could manage.

"Under the Charter," he said, "a claim of that kind is answered in the records. The district's right is to a copy of the entry, the emergency-ledger line, and the sponsor's name, and all three are on the fourth bench, and they are yours, and you may have them this afternoon. What the claim required you to give up is not a question the record answers."

"Answer the question, Mr Vale."

The entry was on the fourth bench. He could see the rack from where he was standing, twenty feet off through a doorway, and a clerk would have pulled the Hearthward tab out and turned it round for him if he had walked over there, and none of that would have helped, because the line on the sheet said *lower row drains clear* and it was going to keep saying it whether he looked at it or not, and there is no drawer in this building for the other version.

"It's a records question," Marek said.

Anwen Sarr stood absolutely still for about two seconds.

"Records question," she said. "Right." She squared the bundle of papers under her arm. "I have chaired an assembly for six years and I have refused four official forecasts on the ground that I was not prepared to take the cost of somebody else's certainty on trust, and I have done it four times, and three of those times I was right, and one of them I was wrong and I have never found out which." She looked at him directly. "And what I have learned, sitting in that chair for six years, is that it is not the forecasts I have any problem with. The forecasts are good. The forecasts are the only good thing this city has got. It is that every single one of them arrives with its consequences folded up inside it, and the folding is done somewhere I cannot get to, and by somebody who is very likely doing it honestly."

"You are owed the whole of it," Marek said.

"Yes."

"And I can't give it to you this morning."

"No," said Anwen Sarr. "I rather thought not. You could not do it in a fortnight, I expect, if it is the same difficulty."

"No," Marek said. "It is the same difficulty. And when I can, I will come to your assembly and put it in a document, with my name at the top of it and not the Office's, and you can put that in front of anybody you like."

"I will hold you to that in writing if you want it in writing."

"Yes, please."

She turned and walked out, and the clerk went with her, and Ferren followed her out into the rain and stood in the doorway for a moment with his cap in his hands looking at Marek as though he had been about to say something and had decided, on the whole, not to.

Marek stood alone in the public hall of the Weatherglass.

And the thing that arrived, unbidden, with no dramatic preparation at all, was his father's voice, from a kitchen in Low Spindle, six years ago, on the evening Marek had told him he was taking a post on the ridge: *You will end up doing it your way, Marek. You will end up holding it all yourself because it is easier than asking, and one day there will be a woman in front of you asking you the right question and you will give her a sentence about procedure, and you will go home afterwards and you will be proud of the sentence.*

He had told Marek it was a career. He had told him not to make a career of telling people what the weather would do to them.

And Marek had laughed at him at the time, because it was a father's remark, and fathers are allowed remarks.

He stood in the hall and found that he had no answer to it at all, and that the discovery was not shame. It was much worse than shame and it arrived in his chest like cold water: he had been counting, for eleven years, on not finding out. He had built a whole career on the expectation that he would be asked the right question by somebody competent and be able to answer it, and he had never once considered that the competence might be on the other side of the table and that the question might be one that deserved an answer and that he would hand it a filing system instead.

---

At ten past twelve he was sent for.

Malle's office had six rosters on it in the morning and four in the afternoon and one on the floor, and the Deputy was standing up, and there was a district superintendent and a Kestrel route warden and a man from the Storm Warden service in the outer room, and they all looked like people who had been in a fight that produced nothing.

"Sit down, Vale."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down." Malle put a sheet on the desk. "You know what that is."

Marek read it. It was a joint request, signed by two ward assemblies and countersigned by a Kestrel route officer, and it said that the yards required a passable corridor for the return lane and the low wards required rain to be let down on the floodplain, and that neither could be given by a party acting alone, and that the two requirements were physically incompatible, and that the Office was therefore requested to produce a single binding claim that covered both before the collision.

"Is that request correct?" Marek said.

"That request is *arithmetically* correct," said Corrin Malle, "which is the worst kind of correct there is. Read the last paragraph."

Marek read the last paragraph. It said that the Office's own instruments could not time the front, and that no district or carrier would accept a window without a sequence behind it, and that the Weatherglass was therefore requested to name the reader who would produce the sequence.

"They have asked for a man," Malle said, "and not for an instrument, and that is the correct request, and I have got nobody to give them."

"You've got the technical cell."

"I have got the technical cell, and the technical cell has got a hundred and forty people in it, and I have been in that room since eight this morning, and there is not one pair of hands in it that has seen the order those movements arrive in." Malle took the sheet back. "There is one, and he is sitting in that chair, and on Wednesday he told me the order of the six movements and could not tell me the arrival times, and on Friday morning he gave me an account of a claim of his own that was a correct description of a mechanism and not an account of anything that happened, and I have not had time to be angry about it because I have a basin to put under a roof."

Marek felt the floor of the room change its texture and did not look down.

"The Bellweather watch is yours," said Corrin Malle.

"The Bellweather watch is the low ground tonight."

"Yes."

"Nobody will take it. It's a wet stone floor, four crews, no public glass, and a district that has already emptied its school at its own cost. Nobody with any standing will take it, because being on that watch tonight means being the Office's man in Low Spindle when the water is in the doors, and that is a thing a man's career does not survive." Malle sat down for the first time since Marek came in. "Except you. You have a docket on the ninth ward tower's glasswork, an unpaid account, and a sequence plate in a tube that you have had in your coat for four days, and every single reason in the world to be on that watch, and none of the others apply to you. So I am going to put you on it, and you are going to take it, and I am not going to discuss it."

"And if the collision comes out of order?"

"Then the low ground will be under water four hours before we can time the drainage, because the drainage depends on the order in which the movements release, and the order is the only part of this that we cannot measure." Malle's pencil went behind his ear. "So you will read the order off your plate, out loud, on the ward floor, at the time it happens, to whoever is standing there, and they will do with it what they can, and if you are wrong then Low Spindle will know that the man from the ridge was wrong, which is the first useful thing that district has had off the Office in a fortnight."

Marek thought about the plate in the room upstairs with the second turn on it, and a two-and-a-half-hour absence in the middle of the Release, and a dry stone throat at the bottom of a market hall, and about two days of tower glass sitting in a seat on Tannery Walk where he had been told to leave it.

"All right," he said.

"Say the other thing."

"There is a sequence fault on that plate," Marek said, "in the second turn, and it is not a plate fault, and I can see why it is not, and I cannot prove it, and I told you about it on Friday in the terms you set for me, which were to bring somebody else's plate, and there is no somebody else's plate, and I should have said that then and I did not, and I have been carrying it for two days and it is heavier than I can carry, and it is not the weather, it is a fault in the record, and I would like it minuted."

Malle wrote one line on the pad in front of him and did not read it out.

"Minded," he said. "And Vale — the collision is confirmed. Six hours off the top of the hour, and it read nine at ten o'clock, and it has been coming in on us all week. Get some food and then get a coat, because you are going to be out there in it, and if the low ground wants a man from the ridge then you are the man from the ridge, and the only thing I am going to ask of you is that when you say a number to a crew you say it as a number and not as an opinion, and if you are not sure, then you say *not sure*, out loud, in those words, to their faces, and I do not care what it does to your standing. I have a basin to get under a roof in six hours and I do not have another six hours after that."
