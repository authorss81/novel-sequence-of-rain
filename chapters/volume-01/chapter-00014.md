# Chapter 14 — A Number With No Place On It

Sable Orin had been on the stone floor of the ninth ward on Saturday night with a strip of glass in her hand and an aneroid under her coat, and it had given her a number, and the number was right, and on the Tuesday morning she went out and told the world so, and the world declined.

She had also, by twenty past eleven on the Tuesday morning, been told no four times by people who were not being rude, which was the part she was not prepared for.

The sheet went up on the drying-room wall at eight, at the same place as always, with the same two hooks, and the room was warm and smelled of hot linen and the damp of a ventilation system shut against the coal. There were seven people in it, four of them old and out for the warmth and one of them asleep in the chair by the door, and none of them read the sheet, and that was fine, because the sheet had never been for the people in the room. It was for the people who came in.

*VERIFIED. Western turn off the basin, the draw backing and re-drawing, low corridor, from 04:00. Instrument below. Basis published. I cannot say why it is right.*

*Instrument: one aneroid, mine, eleven years, re-caged in March. One wet cell, mine. One strip of sequence glass, bought, not cut by me, three years in a drawer. One barometer from a stall at the foot of the Weatherglass steps, which is Feth Raal's and which she has agreed would be read in a public room and not copied.*

That last line was new. Sable had never before printed the name of a person whose instrument she was using, and she had thought about it for two days, and the reason she had done it was that she had spent the weekend watching a man on a stone floor say a true thing out loud with no instrument behind him at all, and had come back up to her own room and understood for the first time in her working life what it felt like to be the man on the stone floor.

Below it she put the basis in the flat form she used, which was deliberately the dullest prose she could produce:

*Time of observation: 17:40, Saturday, the ninth day. Place of observation: ninth ward, the low floor, at the foot of the outside steps, with the district's own written record of the night within thirty feet of me. Height: no fixed station, the glass was in my hand, which I state because it matters and because I have read other people's books.*

*What I am publishing: a turn, a corridor, an hour. What I am not publishing: a place. I have no place. I have a corridor and a direction and a time and I have said so every time I have ever printed anything, and I am printing it again today because a great many people are now convinced that a number without a place is a number that is not a number, and it is a number, and it is right, and the absence of the place is the instrument's limit and not the reading's.*

She stood back and looked at it.

It was, she thought, the most honest sheet she had ever put up, and she was reasonably sure that it was also the least likely to be used by anybody.

*What I cannot say: why it is right. I will not put a mechanism on this sheet. I have a mechanism. The mechanism is a wet cell and a glass strip that I bought off a man who was not supposed to sell it, and the two of them agree with each other, and neither of them agrees with anything anybody can send for. If you want a mechanism you must go and find somebody who has one.*

---

The first refusal came by the ten o'clock post and it came from Ternway, and it was the best of them.

*Ms Orin — Thank you. The turn is accepted as read and cannot be acted on. Your number names a corridor and a time. It does not name a field, a holding, or a road. To move the Ternway harvest crews on it we would be required to translate it ourselves, in writing, with a name attached, and to be answerable for the translation. We have done this before, on a smaller number, and the district lost a morning's cutting and one flooded wagon of seed to a translation that two of our own men disagreed with at the time. We are not declining your reading. We are declining to be the instrument that makes it into a place. — M. Arel, district secretary, Ternway lower holdings.*

Sable read it twice and put it on the wall, because it was the best thing anybody had written about her in two years, and because it was, as far as she could see, correct in every particular.

The second was worse, because it was not a refusal. It was a thank-you.

*A district clerk, Kestrel low ward: Thank you for the sheet. Noted and filed. We are at present able to do nothing with it.*

*Nothing*, and not *no*, and Sable knew the difference, and it is the difference between a district that has considered a thing and a district that is waiting to be told what to do with it.

The third came at eleven and it was an assembly, and the word it used was *declines*, and it declined to move a shelter rota on a number with no place on it, and the reason it gave was that the rota had already been run twice in nine days at a cost to the ward in wages and in goodwill, and that running it a third time on an uncertified number would be the third time in a week that it had asked its people to move on the strength of a stranger's glass, and it was not going to ask them again until the stranger had a chain.

That one hurt. Sable could see where it hurt from, because it was the same place her own ribs hurt, and she wrote it down in the notebook with the rest and then sat on the edge of the table for a while with her hands hanging.

The fourth came in person, and it came at twenty past eleven on a Tuesday, and it came down the outside steps and into a drying room above a public bath, and it was a warden of thirty-seven who had a strap satchel and a chalk mark on her trousers and who had not come for a fight.

"You know I can't act on it," said Neris Kade.

"You came to tell me that in person."

"I came to tell you that in person," said Neris, "and to take the last of the tea, and because you put your basis on the sheet, which nobody has ever done."

Sable put the kettle on.

Neris Kade stood with her back to a wall of glass hooks and looked at the seven people in the room, four of them old and one asleep, and did not sit down.

"I did it again," she said. "The translation. On the Friday night I pinned your card to a board and I wrote a pump order off it, and four hundred and forty people moved at half past eleven at night because I turned a corridor into four blocks and a school and two pump houses, and I did it in about eleven minutes, and I did not ask anybody." She said it evenly. "It cost us a morning of wages and a day of a schoolmistress's life and it was the right thing to do and I have not slept properly since, and if I do it again off a Tuesday sheet I will do it because I have a practice now. I have got a habit. That is the most dangerous instrument in this city and it is mine."

"You did it because it was right."

"I did it because it was right on Friday," said Neris. "What I am telling you is that *right* is not the test. A number with an hour on it and no place on it is not a lesser claim. It is not a claim at all. I cannot be answerable for it and neither can you, and if the drain backs up at four o'clock on a person who acted on it, one of us has to have said *yes* in writing, and neither of us is going to, and so it does not happen, and that is not a failure of the system. That is the system working exactly as it is designed, in a room with a kettle and a barometer in a bag."

"The barometer is Feth's," said Sable.

"I know whose barometer it is. That is why I came and not the Office."

They stood there.

Neris put her satchel on the table and unbuttoned it, and took out a folded sheet of a different shape from anything Sable had seen in a drying room, and pushed it across the table.

"I have written to a yard," she said. "To a captain. I have asked her to publish the western turns, over the low ground, with the hours on them, on a board that a warden is allowed to read. Not for nothing. Not for a fee. I put it on our slate and I said I would not pass it to anybody who had to pay for it."

"That is not a number with no place on it."

"That is a number with a *district* on it," said Neris Kade. "It is the worst number in the world. She will publish it and I will put it up and it will be about my low ground, and if it is wrong then it is wrong *on purpose*, by two named people who can be brought to a council meeting and made to answer for it." She squared the sheet. "You cannot do that. You have never once been able to do that, and it is not because you lack a chain, Sable, it is because you have never been willing to have a name at the bottom, and you have made an entire practice out of that and it is a good practice, and it is the reason a great many people are alive who would otherwise not be, and it is also the reason that nothing you have ever published has ever been able to be answerable to anybody."

She picked the barometer up out of her bag, unwrapped it, checked the reading off the wall against the slate, and put it back.

"Right," she said. "I have to go and stand a pump house."

---

At two in the afternoon a woman came into the drying room and asked for the number, and Sable Orin, who had been putting readings on a public wall since November and had never once had somebody come and ask her for one, found that she had to say no.

The woman was about fifty and she had a fisherman's hands and the end of a nightgown showing under a coat, and her name was Enid Fisk and she was not from the ninth ward, and she had come four miles because somebody in the baths had said that the woman above the drying room had the number for tonight.

Sable Orin read her the sheet.

She read it aloud, all of it, the way she read everything, because being read a number by somebody who means it is worth a great deal to a frightened person. And the woman listened, and looked up at the word *corridor*, and asked, twice, what corridor.

And Sable told her the truth, which was: I have a direction and an hour. I have not got your street. I have got a body of air and it comes off the basin somewhere west of here and it will turn, and I can put a clock against it, and I cannot put a house against it. And if you go up on it, and I am wrong about where it lands, then you are the one who is standing in it.

"Then I could ask somebody," said the woman. "Somebody who knows. I could take it round to Ampow and he'd—"

"You could," said Sable. "And you would be the one who did the translation, and Ampow would be the one who sent the crew, and if it went wrong there would be three people on the ground who had acted on a number out of a drying room above a bath, and I would be upstairs here, saying that I told them the direction."

"You'd say you told them the direction."

"I'd say I told them the direction," said Sable Orin, "and I would be telling the truth, and it would be no use at all to anybody."

The woman stood in the middle of the drying room with her hands at her sides for a while.

"My mother-in-law's on the Quay Steps," she said. "Ground floor. She'll be seventy-eight come Michaelmas and there's no stairs."

"I know," said Sable, which she did not, and which was the first lie she had told a stranger in a year and which she noticed, and did not enjoy noticing.

"Could you write it on a card? Just the hour. I'd take it to the district."

"No," said Sable.

"All right," said the woman.

And she went, and Sable Orin stood in a warm room above a public bath with seven people in it and one of them asleep, and looked at a sheet on a wall that was on its own there and had been put there for the express purpose of being useful, and understood for the first time in her working life that usefulness and being used are not the same shape, and that she had been handing out the second one and calling it the first, and that the price had been on every sheet she had ever put up and had been invisible because she had never been the one paying it.

She did not sleep well that night. She got up at about two, and did not go down to the wall, and instead took out the notebook she used for readings, and the notebook she used for instruments and where she had got them, and the notebook she used for people who had asked her for something and what she had said, and she counted, and found that the third of those had eleven names in it and that she could remember asking five of them anything at all.

Then she took out a fourth notebook, which was the cheapest one in the shop, and ruled a page, and at the top of the page she wrote:

*Declined. Who. Why, in their words. Date.*

And she wrote four lines before it was light, and there were to be a great many more, because there would be other people turning things down in rooms, with reasons that were correct, for as long as there were offices like hers and sheets like that one on a drying-room wall. She did not know what the book was for. She was not going to decide it that night, and she wrote that down too, in the same hand, on the line under the heading: *not yet.*

She did not take the sheet down off the wall.
