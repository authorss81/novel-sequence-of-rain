# Chapter 37 — The First Sentence

Anwen Sarr had a rule about Thursdays, which was that she did not read anything anybody had sent her until she had been in the building for an hour, and which had been formed on the ninth of a March in a different decade and had never once been explained to anybody, including Kell Bray, who had learned to work around it the way you learn to work around a tide.

She got in at half past seven on the Thursday of the thirty-fifth day and did the first hour with the market hall, which meant standing in the upper room looking at boards across the doors of a hall that had not been open since the eleventh of the month and would not be open for another three weeks, and would open, when it opened, onto a market with a season's seed stock in the lower six drawers of the vaults standing in water eighteen inches deep, because the lower six are the six that sit eighteen inches off the market floor and the water came up the retaining wall at twenty-three forty on the seventh and stood until eight on the morning of the eighth.

Nobody brought either of those up.

On the morning of the thirty-fifth day of the month, in a chamber with the windows shut, a woman was going to have her clause examined in front of a clerk and a Council and a Director and a yard, and nobody was going to mention the market, or the six ruined drawers, or the four men and a foreman who were on a Hearthward row on the Thursday and were not on it, and the reason nobody was going to mention it was that it is not a weather event and there is no column for it, and that has been true since the seventh of the month and will be true in a year.

She had thought about the fourth clause for three weeks, which is a long time for a sentence of forty-two words, and she had got it down to a shorter sentence and then a longer one and then back.

---

Ferren came in at nine with his cap in his hands and stood by the door, and he had been in this room before, and he had stood by that door before.

He did not say anything for about two hours and forty minutes.

He had not been asked to say anything, and that is the correct arrangement, and Anwen Sarr had gone to the trouble of explaining it to him on the Wednesday evening in a kitchen above a market hall, in about four sentences, and one of the four had been *you are not on the list and I have got you on it as an attendor and not as a witness, and if anybody asks you a question today, look at me and I will answer it before you open your mouth.*

He looked at her four times in two hours and forty minutes and did not open his mouth.

---

The item came on at eleven, and Sabin Orle did it in the flat voice of a woman reading a form, and she did not do it unkindly and she did not do it as an accusation, and Anwen Sarr has said since that of the four people in her life who have handled her badly, two of them were in that chair.

"Assembly, clause four of the refusal of the eighteenth is before us. It has two sentences. I am going to read them and then I am going to ask this room which of the two it is, and I want to say before I do that I have read it four times and I think the room will find it more useful than I do, and I would be grateful if somebody would tell me which sentence I am looking at, because I have not been able to."

She put a single sheet on the table. It was a copy, not the original, and the copy had a violet square on the corner of it and a records stamp, and the stamp said that a district's standing requirement submitted in a refusal was a local record and was filed in the technical cell's file and was not a public notice.

That was also correct.

*That no claim certified under the Charter shall be entered without the cost of that claim being published at the point of entry, or the sponsor recording in writing that the cost cannot be made public and why.*

"First sentence," said Sabin Orle. "*No claim certified under the Charter shall be entered without the cost of that claim being published at the point of entry.* That sentence has been satisfied. It was satisfied on the twenty-second, in the Council's report, and the report went to nine seats and three regional seats, and the report's annex carries a reader's statement which is bound separately so that it cannot be missed, and the annex names the reader and states the cost and states that he can produce no district and no person and no instrument.

"It was satisfied a second time on the twenty-ninth, in a way that nobody designed and everybody has since been grateful for. A claim was entered on the ward ledger bench under a Director's authorization. The sponsor named every cost he was able to name at the point of entry. There was one and it was published and it was on a yard board in chalk with the hour on it, and the yard published it against their own interest, under a heading which says *not the yard's error*, and that cost was a mooring hand's wrist at eleven minutes past eleven and it is on the Office's own day sheet for the thirtieth in four words.

"So. The first sentence is satisfied twice and I am not asking anybody to do anything about the first sentence and I have written it off."

She put her hand flat on the copy.

"The second sentence. *Or the sponsor recording in writing that the cost cannot be made public and why.*" She looked up. "Nobody has answered that sentence. Not the Council. Not the Office. Not the Director, and the Director's authorization is on the file, and the authorization is three lines and does not touch it. Not one sponsor has ever recorded it, because it cannot be recorded, and I would like the room to understand that I am not saying nobody has tried."

"Ma'am," said a man at the far end who was not named in anything Anwen Sarr had been sent, "a sponsor who cannot publish a cost may record the fact."

"He may," said Sabin Orle. "That is what the sentence says. And what has never happened, in the whole of the history of this instrument, is a sponsor who has recorded the fact. So either nobody has ever been in that position, or the position is not one anybody can write down from, and I have read this Council's files going back to the beginning of the method and I am prepared to say publicly that the second sentence has never been answered, and I would like to know why, and I would like to know it from the author."

---

The room turned round.

Anwen Sarr had thought about this for three weeks. She had a hundred and forty words ready and she did not use any of them, and the reason she did not use them is the reason she has been a difficult woman to a series of careful men for six years, and it is this: she had looked at the room and there were eleven people in it and six of them were waiting for her to be impressive.

"Right," she said. "You want the why. I have had three weeks to write it and I have not written it, and I am going to tell you why I have not written it, and then I am going to give you the best version of it I have got, and it will be shorter than the one I have been carrying around.

"Clause four asks for a record. It asks a person to write down why a thing cannot be written down. And that is a request for a sentence, and the only way to write the sentence is to say what the cost was, and I cannot say what the cost was.

"That is the whole of my answer and it is going to sound like an evasion and it is not one, and I am going to give you the three sentences that make it not one, and then I am going to sit down, and the Chair may tell me that I have wasted the morning, and he would be entitled to."

She turned to the Chair. Rook sat at the end of the table with his hands where they always were, and he said, "You have four minutes of my estimate and I have been miscounting you for six years. Go on."

"The first is this. A claim's cost is a turn in the air. Not a number, not a figure, not an inch on a staff. A turn — a thing that might have happened and does not now happen, in a district, for about forty minutes.

"The second is this. There is no instrument in the Weather Office of this city that has ever looked at a turn. I know this because I have spent six years asking, in letters, in this room, in a corridor on the ninth of the month in the rain, and I have been told by three separate competent men in three separate offices that there is no such instrument and that if there were one it would be in a tower the Office locks, and that I would not be shown it. I have never been told which tower. I have never been told whether there is one. I have decided that I do not want to know, because if I know there is one then the next question is why a district chair is not shown it, and I am not ready to have that argument in a room, and I have not got the afternoon for it."

Somebody at the back said something that was not a word.

"The third is this, and it is the one that matters, and I have got it from your own assessor and I am quoting her and she will not mind." Anwen Sarr put two fingers on the copy on the table. "*It is not accidental that a claim's cost cannot be documented; it is the design.* That is a sentence said out loud in a chamber on the nineteenth of the month by the woman sitting in that chair, and it went into the minutes, and it is true.

"So the second sentence of my clause four asks a sponsor to write down why a thing cannot be written down, and the answer, when it is honest, is *because the mechanism does not report itself and there is no instrument in this city that would show it, and I am not permitted to be shown it and I have not been able to find out whether it exists.* That is the sentence. I have had it in my mouth for three weeks. It is not a *why*. It is a *whereabouts*, and whereabouts is not a category the Charter recognises, and if I write it, then the paper I hand to this Council is a piece of paper saying *I know something happened and I cannot tell you what it is and I cannot even tell you where to look*, and a district that files that paper goes on being a district that files papers.

"That is why the second sentence has never been answered. Not once in the history of this instrument, and I have looked." She sat down. "And I am not going to answer it now, and I want the minutes to say that I declined to answer it and that I gave reasons, and I want the reasons minuted in full, because the reasons are good and they are the only good thing I have got."

---

There was a pause of about four seconds, and then Lior Venn, who had been sitting with his hands flat on a folder he had not opened, said:

"Chair, may I?"

"You may."

"I want to say one thing and I want to say it in a records voice so that nobody mistakes it for a records opinion." He did not open the folder. "What the assembly has just described is not a gap. It is a design, and the assessor named the design, and the design is older than every person in this room, and the design is *right*, and I would like it minuted that the Deputy for Records and Public Notice does not think a single word of that has been answered by what the chair of the Hearthward assembly has just said, and that I have not argued with any of it, and that I am going to vote with the Council on the whole of this item when we come to it, and that I would like it recorded that I said so at the time rather than afterwards."

"Then that is minuted," said Sabin Orle, "and it is the first time in eleven years that Mr Venn has volunteered his position in a room and I would like that minuted too."

---

They gave her the soft landing at about half past twelve, and they gave it well, and it was the kindest thing that had happened to Anwen Sarr in six years and she has never been able to decide whether she was the beneficiary of the kindness or its object.

The proposal came from the assessor and it was, in the flattest possible summary: that the Council recognises the standing requirement in the second half of clause four in a form the Council would honour going forward; that is to say, that where a claim's cost cannot be published at the point of entry, a sponsor **may** record a statement to that effect, and the statement shall be lodged with the ward ledger, and the statement need not set out reasons; and that a district's standing requirement in this form is **not** required to be refused before a claim may be certified, and a district's absence from a seat is not a bar.

"Right," she said. "Kell."

He had the book open. He had had it open for about an hour and a half, and he had not written anything, which for a clerk of twenty years in a ledger is a thing that is visible from across a room, and Cassian Rook had looked at it twice.

"Read it back," she said, and Kell Bray said, "There is nothing to read back, ma'am. I have not taken a line down since the assessor started."

"Then take this one down and take the rest of what I am going to say and do not stop for the page."

He wrote four clauses in one hand without lifting the pen except at the ends of lines, and had to be given the second page, and did not have to stop for it.

She dictated for eleven minutes, and what she dictated was this, and it is the fourth thing the Hearthward rain-garden assembly has put in front of this Council in six weeks, and the first thing it has put in front of one that has read it:

"Clause one. The assembly declines the Council's form of standing requirement as insufficient, and declines it for the reason that it is a permission where the assembly asked for an obligation, and because a permission may be exercised by nobody at any time and cannot be argued with.

"Clause two. The assembly is aware that the second sentence of its original clause four has not been answered by anybody, and has never been answerable, and records that it does not regard the Council's form as an answer to it.

"Clause three. The assembly **concedes**, and does so freely and on the record and without any view as to whether it is a victory, that **the cost of a claim may be published as what the claim closed, by no means naming who chose it.** The assembly does not ask for a name. The assembly has never asked for a name, and if it had asked for a name the answer would have been that a name cannot be given, and the assembly does not spend its afternoons asking for things that cannot be given."

She stopped there and Ferren, who had been standing by the door for two hours and forty minutes, said, very quietly, "That's the first sentence that's a road."

"Thank you, Ferren," said Anwen Sarr, without turning round, and went on.

"Clause four. The assembly's standing requirement, submitted separately and in addition, and submitted in substitution: that no claim entered under the Charter or under an authorization of the Director be certified, authorized, or acted upon unless the **place, the stated effect, the trigger, the condition, the expiry, and a line headed *turns displaced*, in which is set out what that claim closed, in the ordinary language of the district affected, and by no means who closed it,** be entered on the ward ledger at the point of entry and published in the next return.

"And the assembly will accept that the line headed *turns displaced* may name no person, and will accept that for ever, and gives notice that it will spend the rest of its life defending a column that will never say who did it, and does not tell this Council that that is what the assembly would have chosen."

Kell Bray read it back, and the room was extremely quiet, and Sabin Orle looked at the clause for a while and then said:

"Chair. I want to say three things about that and only one of them is procedural.

"Procedurally: that is a well-formed requirement, it is within the powers of the assembly to submit it, and it is the strongest thing anybody has put in front of this Council in a year and a half.

"It is going to be adopted. Not today. It will go to the technical cell and come back with recommendations and it will come back with two changes in it and both of those changes will be improvements, and the reason both of them will be improvements is that a Council which is going to have to sit behind a column of what claims closed will want the column legible. So I am telling you now that the first two days of this review have not been wasted on a district that is being difficult. They have been spent on the only piece of drafting anybody is going to do in this building for the next three years, and it is going to be on a public table, and the assembly wrote it, and the assembly should write on it rather than be written on."

"Assessor," said Anwen Sarr, "I have been doing this for six years and I am going to be honest with you, which I have not been in this room, and if I am honest it is going to be expensive for me, so I am going to do it quickly. That is not a victory. It is a defeat that has a clause in it. I came in here this morning with a demand that a thing be named, and I am going out with a form that says a thing is not to be named, and I put the second one there myself, at about twenty past twelve, and I did it in one sentence, and I did it because the first thing you said to me this morning was the truth and I have not got a second morning.

"**You are not going to name who closed it.** Not in the column, not in the return, not on a notice, not ever, not while I am alive, and not after. And you have just told a room of eleven people that the Council is going to print what closed and is not going to print who, and that is going to happen, and it is going to be the only honest public record of what a claim costs that exists in the history of this basin, and I am going to have to defend it against every district in this city that has ever lost a thing, including the one I chair, for the rest of my life."

"Then say it in the minutes," said Sabin Orle.

"Write it down, Kell," said Anwen Sarr, "and write the whole of it and do not tidy it, because in four years somebody is going to want to know that I understood what I had done on the thirty-fifth day of a month in which nothing whatever was happening in the sky."

Kell Bray wrote it down and did not tidy it, and it is in the minutes of the thirty-fifth day at the third item, in a clerk's hand, four clauses and a paragraph, and the paragraph is the only place anybody has ever written down, in advance, in a fair copy, that they are going to lose an argument on purpose.

---

The room read it back, which is the practice, and the clerk who read it was on the far side of the table with his back to the north wall, and Marek turned his chair to take it off the clerk's shoulder instead of off the page in front of him, and put his weight on his right hand, and nobody in the room noticed that he had done either of those things, because eleven people in a chamber with the windows shut are all leaning on something.

So she had come in with a second clause behind the first one. And she had got it in, and the cost of getting it in was the name, and she had chosen the column over the name on her own authority, in a room, on the Thursday morning of the thirty-fifth day, in about eleven minutes of dictation, and she had done it on the record and in front of a clerk.

And she was not soft about it. That is the part of this that people who were not in the room get wrong, and the part that the Hearthward assembly understood and the rest of the chamber did not: she had not been softened, and she had not been bought off, and she had won nothing, and she had spent the whole of her standing requirement on a form that gives a city a way to be honest about its own losses without ever having to say whose they were, and she knew exactly what that was worth, and she said it out loud at a table in front of a Council and asked for it to be minuted, which is the single most expensive thing anybody did in that month.

And she stayed for the whole of the rest of the morning, and did not leave early, and did not thank anybody.
