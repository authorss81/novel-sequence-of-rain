# Chapter 22 — The Copy Is the Original

At twenty-six minutes past three in the morning on the twenty-first day of the Bellweather autumn, Nell Cray stood on the Quay Steps in a coat over her night clothes with a lamp in one hand and a district book under her arm, and there was about an inch and a half of water in the low side of Ferrygate and it was not going anywhere, and the release was in an hour and fourteen minutes and going to do exactly what a Council assessor had certified in a room on the ridge four days ago.

She had been awake since two. Nothing had gone wrong, which was the difficulty.

The water was not the flood water. That was the thing she kept having to explain to herself while she walked the low lane with the lamp held low. This was the low side, which floods twice a winter by design, and what she was standing in was the low side doing what the low side does, and the reach above it had already come down four feet by the Friday afternoon and taken the causeway with it, and the cart marker at the turn was standing out of the mud like a bad tooth, and everything the market needed was dry above the marker and everything under the marker was wet and both of those facts were on the claim.

*the Ferrygate low side drains clear to the second watch*

It was a third of a way through the second watch and the low side was as clear as it had ever been, and the channels were running free, and the two rain gardens were taking it, and she had walked the lane at three and there was nothing in the channels but a slight brown coming down from the top of the steps and going out again.

The claim was true. She had been telling herself for four days that she did not believe it would be.

---

The release came at 04:40, and it came at 04:40.

Nell Cray was standing at the gate road end of the Quay Steps with a watch and a lantern and two men from the water office's district crew who had turned up at four because she had rung them at eleven the previous night, on the strength of a sheet of paper in a sealed folder that said *probable, within fourteen days, size not stated, hour not stated*, which had told her nothing at all, and which had been overtaken by a claim with an hour on it, entered under the Charter with a named sponsor and a published alternative and one regional seat's mark on it, and which was now doing what it said it would do to the minute.

The gate let go. The sound came up the cutting and the low side took it, and for about eleven minutes the whole of Ferrygate's low ground was a sort of administered event, and two men walked it with poles and a third man stood at the third step with a board and called numbers, and at 04:52 the low side was clear to the marker and at 04:55 it was clear past the marker, and the water in it was going down the Ferrygate side of the basin at a rate that a man with a pole could describe, which is a sentence that means nothing until you have spent nineteen years in a district where it means everything.

"Right," said Nell Cray, out loud, to nobody. "There we are."

She wrote it in the district book. She wrote the time and the fact and the fact that the claim said *to the second watch* and that the second watch had not ended and that the district was therefore inside the certified effect and not outside it, and she underlined the word *inside*, because a clerk who underlines a word has said something, and she wanted to have said it.

Then, at five minutes past five, the lamp at the bottom of the Quay Steps stopped being a lamp.

---

She had walked the lane at three and again at half past four and there had been two inches of brown water in the channel and a smell off the yard drains that she had noted and dismissed as a blocked gully she would chase on Monday.

At five past five the water was coming in under the door of number four.

Not over. Under. Through the gap where the flags had come up a quarter of an inch over sixty years, the way they do on the low side of Ferrygate, and then across the floor of a ground-floor room in about the time it takes to make a decision about a kettle.

Nell Cray was at that door in ninety seconds. She had a key ring on a chain and the key for number four was on it because the district has held keys to ground-floor rooms on the Quay Steps since before she was a clerk, and she got the door open against the water and put herself in it up to the shin.

"Abel," she said. "Abel, are you up? Abel."

There was no answer, and then there was a noise behind the door, not words.

She got the door open another four inches and went through it sideways with a lamp above her head, and Abel Fisk was sitting up in a chair in his night shirt with a blanket round his shoulders and his feet up on a deal table, perfectly dry from the waist up and about to be nothing like dry from the waist down, and Ottilie Fisk was standing on the table. She was standing on the table in the dark in a cardigan over a nightgown with her arms out for balance and a boat hook in her right hand, which she had taken off the wall without being asked by anybody.

"Get off the table, Ott," said Nell Cray.

"I am not standing in it."

"You are standing in a table. Get off the table and go up the step to the landing. Both of you. Now, and do not stop for the chest."

"The chest—"

"Abel."

Abel Fisk was eighty-two and said he was seventy-seven, and he came off the table with his blanket round him and got to the step and stood on it, and Ottilie Fisk got down off the table on the landing side of the room with the hook, and looked back once at the floor of her front room, and said, in an entirely ordinary voice, "*Well.*"

Nell Cray got out and shut the door and stood in the lane in six inches of water with a lamp.

That was the whole of it. Nobody was hurt. It was the smallest emergency of the four Nell Cray had run in nineteen years, and it took four minutes from the door to both of them being upstairs, and the water in that room was ankle deep by six o'clock and knee deep by eight, and it was eleven inches at the turn of the chair, and it stood there until about ten, and then it went.

---

The rest of the night was the part that took until two in the afternoon.

There were forty-one other ground-floor rooms in the low side of Ferrygate. Not all of them went under; the ones on the Quay Steps and the two closes behind them did, and nine of those nine were on the district's ground-floor book and eight of them answered the door. The ninth was empty because the family were up the lane at a cousin's, which was the single luckiest thing that happened in Ferrygate that day, and Nell Cray noted it as luck and did not allow herself to build anything on it.

The district improvised. That is the phrase she used in the book afterwards and it is the correct phrase and she has been careful since to make sure that other people use it too.

The improvised part was this. There is no sheet anywhere in the basin that says two people on the Quay Steps ought to be somewhere else at five in the morning on the twenty-first day, and there is no warden in Ferrygate, and there is no rota for a night when a night is the right answer, because a rota is a thing you run in the ordinary course and in the ordinary course the low side does not take a ground floor at five in the morning while the claim that was supposed to guarantee it is *working*.

So Nell Cray did it with a lamp and a key ring and her own two hands, room by room, and the district's own water crew did the rest with poles, and at about ten o'clock she went and knocked on the door of a house in Chandler Row whose occupant was an old man she had never had cause to speak to, and asked him, without preamble, whether he had a room.

He had a room.

At about eleven she sent a runner to the assembly rooms for blankets, and the assembly rooms gave her four, and the four were a quantity that would have embarrassed a ward of any size, and by one in the afternoon the eleven people out of the two ground-floor rooms on the Quay Steps were in Chandler Row and in the back room of a chandler's, and Abel Fisk was asleep in a chair, and Ottilie Fisk was arguing with somebody's sister about the composition of tea, and nobody was hurt and nobody was drowned and no ambulance was called and there is not one line about any of it in any notice in Veyra, and there never will be.

---

The thing that stayed with her, and she understood by about two in the afternoon that it was the thing, was this.

At about six she went back to the district book, because she wanted the times, and she wanted to write the times while she still had them, and she opened it on the shelf in her own office under the window and turned to the low-ground section and could not find the sheet.

She found it in about ninety seconds. It was in the district book. It was where it had been for six years, between the return for the spring boundary stone and the return for the lamp glass, because that is where it had been filed the first time and nobody had ever moved it, and there were two lines on it in her own hand:

*Ottilie Fisk, 4 Quay Steps, ground floor.*
*Abel Fisk, 4 Quay Steps, ground floor.*

And that was all of it. There was no date on it. There was no reason on it. There was no box on it for a reason, and she had never wanted a box for a reason, because the reason had been a boat put in for three days in the winter six years before and a woman who had turned out to be perfectly well.

She stood and looked at it for a long moment.

Because that was the sheet, and the sheet was still perfectly good, and Nell Cray herself had written both names on it in the afternoon of a wet Thursday in boots, and every single thing about it was correct.

And there was nothing in the world connected to it. Nobody had withdrawn it, because you cannot withdraw a piece of paper that nobody sent anywhere. Nobody had cancelled it, because it had never been booked. It was not in the water office's records and it was not in the Council's and it was not in the market factor's and it was not in the four-line letter the district had written on Monday thanking the Council for a certified claim, and the four-line letter had withdrawn the rain-garden clearing rota for the twenty-first and cancelled the ferry's extended night hours, both of which had been on the agenda of a district meeting, both of which had been *decided*, and both of which were gone.

The rota had not been decided about. It had simply stopped being a plan.

---

She did the thing that took the rest of the day and she did it properly, because that is what she was.

She went to the water office's district desk at about half past seven in the morning and asked for the certified claim, and they gave her a copy of it, and it was correct on every part — place, trigger, effect, listed alternative, expiry, sponsor, the regional seat — and the effect line said what the effect line said.

"Was anything withdrawn?" she said.

"Not that I know of," said the man at the desk, and he was not being difficult, he was a district clerk doing a district clerk's job, and he was about forty and he had a good hand. "They'll have withdrawn the causeway clearing, in the ordinary course. Nothing against the claim."

"Right," said Nell Cray. "Thank you."

And then she went back to her own office and sat down and did the arithmetic that nineteen years had taught her to do before it needed doing, which is: *what has stopped being true since yesterday that I did not decide?*

The rain-garden rota, decided, Monday, in a letter, correctly.

The ferry's extended hours, decided, Monday, in the same letter, correctly.

And the sheet in the district book with two names on it in her own hand, which had not been decided about by anybody and could not have been, because there was no notice in Veyra that had ever mentioned it, and a thing nobody has been told about cannot be responded to.

That is the whole of what a good claim costs, and it cost it in a district that had done nothing wrong, by a clerk who had done everything right, in a claim that was entirely true and entirely certified and entirely discharged.

---

Here is the part she would think about for a long time, and the part she never said out loud to anybody in Ferrygate.

If she had put the rota in front of the district on Monday, at the meeting where the four-line letter was agreed, somebody would have asked what it was for. She knows that. She was the clerk and she was the one who read the four lines out. Somebody would have asked what it was for, and she would have had to explain that it was a piece of paper about two old people on the Quay Steps with no stairs, in a district where the ground floors flood twice a winter by design, and somebody would have said *that is not this*, and she would have said *it is not this and it is cheap and it is in the book and I move it myself*, and somebody would have said *very well*, and it would have been minuted, and it would have been a decision, and a decision can be responded to.

A decision can be responded to. A piece of paper in a book cannot.

That was the mechanism. It was not anybody's. It was a piece of Office stationery about a water gate, and a market that opened on time, and a district that thanked the Council in a courteous letter, and two names in a clerk's hand with no box on the sheet for a reason.

---

She finished at about two in the afternoon. She got the boat hook out of number four's ground-floor room, which was the only thing in it anybody wanted, and which came out of the water about eight inches under. She put it against the wall of the district office to dry because there was nowhere else for it, and it stood there for nine years, and it is a boat hook.

Then she took a clean sheet of the district's own paper and she copied the rota out.

She copied it exactly. *Ottilie Fisk, 4 Quay Steps, ground floor. Abel Fisk, 4 Quay Steps, ground floor.* And she wrote the date on it, and she wrote *the twenty-first day, the low side, 05:05* at the top of it in her own hand, and she signed it, and it is a good copy. It is better than good. It is the clearest, plainest, most correct piece of paper in the Ferrygate district records and it is a perfect record of a piece of paper that cannot be produced, because the piece of paper it is a record of was the copy, and the copy is the original, and a sheet of paper does not carry a reason, and the reason was a boat in the winter six years ago and a woman who had turned out to be perfectly well.

There is no authority in the new copy whatever. It could be produced tomorrow to anybody in Veyra and it would prove that Nell Cray wrote down two names. It would not prove that anybody was under any obligation of any kind to be upstairs at five in the morning.

She looked at it for a while.

Then she copied it three more times.

Not because anybody had asked. Because the first copy was the same mistake twice.

At half past four on the twenty-first day of a Bellweather autumn, with nine inches of water in two ground-floor rooms on the Quay Steps and eleven people in Chandler Row and a claim in the district book that was entirely true, Nell Cray filed four identical copies of a two-line shelter rota, and sent one up the hill to the warden's council with the ordinary Friday post, and wrote the covering note, and the covering note said:

*This is a district record and not a public notice. It is not a forecast and there is no claim in it and I have entered no claim. I send four copies because I have learned this month that a thing kept once is a thing that can be lost. — N. Cray, clerk.*

Then she locked the drawer, and put the key on the ring, and went down to the Quay Steps because the water was still in number four's yard and somebody had to be able to say that it had been looked at.

And the thing that she could not get out of her, the whole rest of the afternoon and the whole of the evening, was not grief and was not self-pity and was not any kind of vindication, and she was careful about that later, because she had a career and a reputation and a district.

It was the specific exhaustion of a competent woman who has spent nineteen years being right, discovering that being right was the mechanism, and that it had never once been enough, and that there was no room in the entire apparatus of this city for the sentence *two old people were standing in a foot of water because a piece of paper about a water gate was completely correct.*

She had no instrument. She had no sequence and no chain and no bell. She had not spent a single possibility of anything and nothing whatever had been taken out of the world to put a foot of water in a ground-floor room on the Quay Steps, and there is no column anywhere for her to be paid in, and she wrote nothing down at the end of the day that anybody will ever read.
