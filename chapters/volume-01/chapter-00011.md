# Chapter 11 — Two Truths in the Rain

The stone floor of the ninth ward was the floor of an old rope works that had stopped being a rope works before Marek was born, and it had been chosen for a ward floor because it was flat, it was stone, it had a drain in the middle of it, and it was four feet below the level of the street, which meant a man standing on it could see the whole length of the lane and a man on the street could not see his feet.

That last property was the reason Low Spindle used it and the reason every warden in Veyra who had spent a winter in a flooded district understood its value. You could stand on it in the dark and not be seen from the road. It was, in the terms of the district, honest weather work.

Marek came down the outside steps at ten past six with a cardboard tube under his arm and a broom in his hand and thirty minutes on him that he had not spent, and there were nineteen people on the floor.

He had expected four. He had been given four crews on the roster and there were two pump houses and a school and four hundred and forty people in the lowest four blocks, and the arithmetic of that had seemed to him at four o'clock to produce a floor with about nine men on it, and it had produced nineteen, and eleven of them were not in anybody's crew and had come because a runner boy had told a corner shop.

"Warden," he said.

"Marek Vale," said Neris Kade. "You're late and I'm glad, because I've had this floor for two hours with nothing to say on it."

"You could have started."

"On what?" She had a lamp in her hand and a board under her arm and, Marek noticed, wet trousers rolled to the knee and a chalk mark on the door frame behind her that he had not known about. "The Office put the window at an hour as of five o'clock. It's an hour. It's a number, and a number's a number, and my pump order's built on it, and my two crews are standing by. What I have got at six o'clock is an hour and a wet floor and a man from the ridge with a tube, so I have got about you. Say something."

Marek put the tube down on the stone and took the plate out.

He had thought about this for two days. He had thought about it the way you think about a thing you have rehearsed until the rehearsal has gone smooth, which is exactly how a man thinks about a thing he is frightened of, and he understood that about himself at the time and did not stop.

Here is what he had.

He had a plate. He had read the order off it four times, cold, and the order was the same every time, and there was no instrument in Veyra that could confirm one syllable of it, and the six movements that were still coming down on the low ground of the ninth ward that night were going to arrive in an order that no man in this building, and possibly no man in this city, was presently able to write down.

He had no chain. He had a piece of glass cut out of the crown of a pane this district had paid for and never been given, which was sitting in a tube in his coat because he had made it in a glassblower's shop on a Wednesday afternoon on a private arrangement, and a two-year docket in the name of a maintenance hand that said he owed the tower glasswork and that nobody in the district had ever read and nobody in the district ever would.

He was, he understood, about thirty seconds away from being a joke.

"Listen to me," he said, "and then decide what to do with it, because that is the whole of my standing and I am not going to improve on it while we are standing here."

The floor got quieter in a way that people do not usually manage on a stone floor with nineteen of them on it.

"Nothing I say tonight costs anything," Marek said. "I want that said first, out loud, in these words, because it is true and because I have got a bad habit of not saying the true things first. There is no claim. There is no entry. Nobody has to write this down. If I am wrong, the only thing that has been spent is my standing."

"Which is not nothing," said Neris Kade.

"It is the only thing in this room tonight that is mine to spend," Marek agreed. "All right."

He got the lamp turned so it came across the glass at an angle, and he held it up, and he waited for his own eyes to stop arguing with him, which took about four seconds and which he was extremely aware of doing in front of nineteen people.

"The front is on the ridge," he said. "It is coming over the Nacre side and it has three different airs in it arriving inside one cycle, and I want you to understand what that means before I give you the order, because the order does not make sense on its own and I would rather you had the reason. It means the six movements do not arrive in the order they arrive in normally. Not off today. Not in a slightly different order. The ordinary order is Gather, Weight, Break, Route, Release, Witness. Tonight it is not that, and the Office's six best instruments cannot tell you that it is not that, and I can, off a piece of glass with no instrument behind it, because the only thing those six instruments measure is *when one movement gives way to the next one*, and when the movements turn up in the wrong order that is the one question they are built to answer and it is the one question that has stopped meaning anything."

"That is a hell of a claim to make on that floor," said a man in a wet coat near the drain.

"That is the true part. Here is the order. I am going to say it as numbers because numbers are what you can carry."

He held up a finger.

"Gather. Alone, and early, and by itself, and it does what it always does — it pulls the moisture and it pulls it hard, because it is doing the work of a movement that is going to be short of air for the next four hours."

Second finger.

"Then Weight does not finish. And Break comes in on top of it, early, and hard, and takes the ground the Weight should have kept going in. That is not a small thing. That is the movement that decides whether a cloud is a cloud or a wall, and tonight it is going to decide it early and decide it hard."

Third finger.

"Then Route, and Route comes twice. A short hook, and then a long one on the top of it, and they are not the same route, and the second one is the one that puts air over the top of you rather than through you."

Fourth finger.

"Then Release starts. And here is the part I cannot time, and I am telling you now rather than at two in the morning. I can see that it starts. I cannot tell you the hour, and the reason I cannot tell you the hour is that on a night like this the Release stands on whatever is behind it, and what is behind it is a set of preparations that somebody has either made or not made, and I have not got an instrument in this city that can see which. It may be quick. It may be long."

"And which is it?" said Neris Kade. "Give me the two words."

"I am not sure."

That landed harder than he had expected it to. He had said it as a piece of housekeeping.

"Say it again," said Neris.

"I am not sure," Marek said. "That is twice, and if anybody here wants to hold me to it, hold me to that, and I would rather be held to that than be found out at four in the morning." He put his hand flat on the stone. "Now listen. The order is not the instruction. The order is the reason your pump order was worth having. If the front arrives in the ordinary order, the water and the weight arrive in a shape a plan can be built on. Everybody who has ever lost a district to weather has lost it to a front that came in the wrong order, because a plan that assumes Weight finishes cannot be run at all once Break has already taken the work."

He put the plate down on the stone, flat, where anybody could come and look at it, which was the single most frightening thing he did all night and which he had decided on the steps.

"Watch," he said. "That is all of it. Watch, and run the order you have got. And if the water comes early and hard and does not stop when it should, that is not a failure of your plan. It is the Release taking the whole front at once because there is nothing behind it. And I have told you now so that nobody can say afterward that they were not told."

"And if it comes ordinary?"

"Then I am wrong, and it will be an ordinary Saturday, and I will buy the ward a drink and never mention it again."

Somebody laughed, which was a good sign, and which Marek found he minded more than the silence.

"Copy that," said Neris Kade, to the floor. "Ott. Write it as he said it, and put the times against it, and do not tidy it up."

A young man of about nineteen on the far side of the drain got a wet notebook out of his coat and wrote without asking a single question, which was the single most encouraging thing that had happened to Marek in nine days. His name was Ott Belm, he was the ninth ward's district copy, and on that evening he was a boy in a wet notebook, and it was going to be the only document in Veyra that said any of it.

"Right," said Neris. "Ampow. Repeat it back to the second pump house crew."

Sergeant Ampow had been a warden for twenty-two years and had spent a week being handed notices that were true and useless, and he came forward out of the wet, and listened, and then said:

"He says we're not sure. Gather, then Break instead of Weight and it comes hard, then Route twice — a short one and then a long one — then Release starts and nobody knows how long, then Witness."

"Close," said Marek. "Very close, and it is me who is not sure and not the weather, and say it that way to the crew or they will think the weather has stopped being sure. Ampow — Break does not come *instead of* Weight. Weight starts and it does not finish, and then Break comes in on top of it part of the way through. It is a different shape, and the different shape is the whole difficulty, because the two of them together are holding the air, and if you have planned for Weight alone then everything you have planned is going to be a hand's breadth off."

"Say it again," said Ampow, and Marek said it again, and Ampow said it back again, and this time it was right, and he looked slightly annoyed about having got it wrong the first time in front of nineteen people, which Marek could entirely sympathise with and was grateful for.

"Again," Ampow said, to the crew on the steps. "And I'll take it from the pump house end at eight, and I'll say it there the way he said it and not the way I first said it, because I got it wrong the first time and there's no sense in me carrying my own version about the district."

"That," said Marek, "is the only use my job has ever had."

---

It came at about six.

Marek felt it in the plate before the floor did. The Gather went up off the glass in a single breath, hard and fast, and the moisture came on the film like somebody wiping a window from the far side, and the temperature traces folded over each other as the cold air came down off the Nacre side into air that had been sitting in the basin all week and had no idea what was coming.

It did not feel like weather. He had been half expecting it to feel like weather, in the sense of a man feeling a door open, and what he got instead was a pressure in his left ear that came up half a tone and stayed there, and a taste like a coin laid between the teeth, and the ordinary animal dread of nineteen people in a stone room when the sound goes up all at once.

He watched the plate, and the plate did what it had done on the bench nine days earlier, and the order was the order, and for about four seconds he had the specific, cold, entirely professional pleasure of a man whose instrument is doing precisely what he said it would do in front of a witness.

It was the best he had felt in a fortnight. He noticed it happening, standing on a wet stone floor with his hand on a piece of glass, and he had the thought in the middle of it — clear, complete, and about himself — that if this was the good part, then he had been living for nine days in a job with no good part in it, and that he had chosen it.

Then he went back to the plate.

---

Ampow sent the second pump house crew out at twenty past six, in the middle of the wind, with the Route doubled, and they went along the terrace end of the drain run with a line between them and came back an hour and ten minutes later with two men shaking and one ankle wrenched and the pump at the second house running at a rate nobody had a figure for.

"Sump Lane grate crew, terrace and school undercroft, before eight," said Neris.

"Done at half past seven," said Ampow. "Undercroft's dry. The whole lot's on the high side."

"Lower blocks?"

"Lower blocks are holding," said Ampow. "Nine inches at the corner of the fourth and not moving. The first pump house is doing what it can with what's going in and I'll tell you honestly, warden, it is not enough, and if it comes up another two inches those rooms stop being a drainage problem and start being a rescue problem, and I want that written down now, at seven o'clock, while the person writing it is me and not you."

"Written," said Neris Kade. "Ott. Seven o'clock, nine inches at the fourth, and Ampow's words."

At nine the water stopped coming, all at once, without any warning at all, in the way a tap stops.

It was the Release. Marek watched it go on the plate and had to put his hand on the stone to keep it under him. Moisture came on the film at an angle rather than from underneath, and the traces ran, and it ran for eleven minutes and stopped, and the whole of Low Spindle stood in a lane two hundred yards long and listened to a wind going through it and then not going through it.

Then the Witness came on. That was the cold and the calm and the light going flat and green in the west, and it came on top of the Break rather than on top of the Release, which was the last part of the order and the part nobody upstairs would have believed, and Marek said it out loud to an empty stone floor with the lamp still on it and nobody in the room but Ott Belm and a broom.

"Order's done," he said. "It came in that order. It will not do it again tonight."

"Warden says the lower rows are draining," said Ott Belm, from the steps.

"Good."

"Should I tell her you had it right?"

"No," Marek said.

"Why not?"

"Because she has got four hundred people to get to bed and I have got to go and find out whether a bell went anywhere," Marek said. "And she will know by morning."

It was in the small hours, sitting against the warm brick with his back to the drain, that the thing arrived.

It was not elation. He had expected elation, in the way a man expects a thing that has been promised to him, and there was none of it, and what there was instead was a kind of flat, grey, bone-deep exhaustion, and a very specific kind of tiredness that had nothing to do with the hour: the tiredness of a man who has been right in front of strangers for the first time in his life and has found, on the far side of it, that it has changed nothing whatsoever about the way the room is organised.

Ampow had got the order right by the third telling, which was excellent, and which had also made the Deputy's job on the watch about a two-minute job, and the district's pump order had been written on Friday afternoon off a number out of a drying room, and four hundred and forty people had been moved by somebody else.

He had been, for about nineteen minutes, the reason a street was dry.

Then it had been Tuesday again.

He had said the whole of it out loud, including the part he could not time, which was the thing he was worst at and the thing he had spent nine days promising himself he would do, and it had bought him nineteen strangers' attention for a night and a boy with a wet notebook and a sergeant who had carried the sentence to the second pump house and got it right.

He was not sure that was worth the taste of a coin.

What made him sit there, in the cold, for longer than he needed to, was the other thing. It was that in nineteen heads and one wet notebook and one district, the order of the six movements was now a thing people knew. It was out. It could not be got back. He had said it out loud to people who were not his, and it had not been a claim and it had cost him nothing, and the cost of that was that the one private thing he had left in Veyra was now in most of a district's heads and in a boy's notebook, and it belonged to everybody, and the only man in the city who could read it was an instrument nobody could check.

Somewhere between two and three, when the wind had gone round to the west and stopped, a woman he did not know came down the outside steps with a lamp, looked at him, said, "They've had it off the ward pipe," and went away again, and he understood that the district had gone back to its ordinary water because the pipes were clear, and that nobody was going to know for six years that the pipes had been clear on the Friday night as well, and that if he had asked a question on Tuesday about Hearthward's drains he would still have been sitting here.

At four o'clock the sky over the Nacre Range began to lighten in a way that had nothing to do with the day.

At twenty past five he did the thing he had been putting off since midnight. He got his coat off the wall and unbuttoned the left cuff, and took the pencil out from behind his wrist, and read what was on the lining in a hand that had got worse in the last nine days: a plate number and an order, a time and a fault he had told nobody about, and a filing mark in a dead woman's hand with a piece of paper missing beside it.

Three blocks. There was no room, and that was a reason, and the reason was good, and he wrote nothing, and he buttoned the cuff, and he went and shook an old man awake on a floor of the ninth ward and asked him whether his people had got to where they were going.

---

The Office's bulletin for the collision came down the ridge at about eleven and was put on the board outside the assembly rooms and copied onto the ward slate and read out on the steps.

*BELLWEATHER COLLISION, NINTH DAY. Cold air from the Nacre Range, warm draw from the lake, and a fast western turn inside one cycle. The basin stood at eighteen hundred on Saturday evening. The turn behind the collision passed the northern wicks at ten o'clock. Standing water in the low ground of the ninth ward to the depth of nine inches, easing after the third watch. The following day: settled, cool, light airs.*

That was all of it, and every sentence in it was true.

It did not say that four hundred and forty people had moved on a number set in a drying room above a public bath. It did not say that a school had been emptied on a warden's own authority on the Friday and cost the ward a morning of wages. It did not say that the six movements had arrived out of order, which was the single most important fact about the collision, and which was in most of a district's heads and one wet notebook and the private recollection of a dozen adults and a boy.

It did not say the word *reader* anywhere. It did not say that the man who had said it had a plate with no chain behind him in a tube under the bench and had said so himself, out loud, on a stone floor, to nineteen people, in the correct terms, before he had started.

Neris Kade read the whole of it twice, standing in the street with the damp coming up round her boots, and then she took it inside and put it in the district records, and she got a fresh ruled page out of the drawer, and she sat down at the desk she had been sitting at for four days, and she wrote a fifth line.
