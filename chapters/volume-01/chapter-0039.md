# Chapter 39 — The Same Answer

They gave him the Saturday, which nobody had expected, and the reason they gave him the Saturday was that the review had eight citations and five days and two of the citations were a Director and a Deputy and could not be heard on a weekday, and the assessor said so out loud on the Thursday afternoon in the flattest possible voice: *the review will sit on the Saturday because the review has a budget of days and not a budget of convenience, and because the Office's own reader is the only person in this basin who can be asked to describe what a claim displaces, and I do not intend to keep him for a fortnight.*

So the Council chamber on the second floor of the basin house had nine people in it at eleven on the Saturday of the thirty-seventh day, and there was no ventilation worth the name, and the table was the length of the room, and Marek Vale was at the end of the right-hand bench with no plate in front of him, no folder, and nothing at all in his hands, which he had arranged deliberately and had arranged badly, because a man with his hands behind his back looks like a man with something to hide and a man with his hands on a table looks like a man preparing to speak.

He decided to put them flat on the table.

---

The assessor had put the terms of reference on the wall of the room on a sheet of paper, at a height where a seated man could read them, and that was the first thing nobody expected her to do and it was, by a distance, the most frightening thing she did all month.

"Right," said Sabin Orle. "Mr Vale. This review has two questions. The first is whether the effect of a claim on a prepared district can be demonstrated from the record. The second is what happens if it cannot.

"A claim holds its stated route by displacing the turn that most directly contradicts it. On the thirtieth, what did that claim displace?"

Marek Vale put both hands flat on the table and answered the same answer he had given in a shuttered room on the nineteenth of the month, and it is worth setting down that he did not change it, because a man who has been caught out once in public and then puts the same sentence up again in front of a different room is a rarer thing than this volume has any right to assume.

"A turn," he said. "Not a person and not a piece of property. What the claim of the twenty-ninth displaced was the ebb under a long band in the western draw.

"The long band is the corridor. The corridor was the claim. A claim that holds an hour does not improve the hour, it makes the hour dependable, and a dependable hour is not a variable one, and the sequence does not distinguish between a thing that is probable and a thing that is fixed, and a fixed turn does not have a bend in it. The bend is the turn that most directly contradicts a long band held to certainty, and under that long band there was a hook of about a quarter of an hour, and the hook was the ebb, and the ebb is when the basin's outflow runs free and the bottom of a reach drops to the lowest point of the month.

"The convoy's repair window at the Lower Sill was on the bottom of the reach. It was about forty minutes wide, on the morning of the thirtieth, and it was the only morning in about nine days, and there is no instrument in the Weather Office of this city that has ever looked at a reach, and there is not one word in the Charter or in the rules of any ward or in the mouth of any Deputy that can be caused to move by the fact that fifteen minutes of air has stopped being available on the last morning of a month."

He stopped. He had gone about fifty seconds and the room had not made a sound.

"Assessor, that is the same answer you gave in a statement on the nineteenth," said Sabin Orle, "and the statement is in the file, and the statement is four lines long and it is stamped *an assertion not supported by a document is not compellable and is not evidence of a cost*, and I have read it about nine times this month, and I have read it in four buildings.

"What I would like from you now is the part you did not put in the statement, because the statement was four lines and you were in a room with eighty people in it and you had decided in advance that you were going to be asked for paper. So."

"I have no paper."

"I know. What I am asking is what the *thing* was, and I want to be very precise about the question, because I have asked it badly twice already today and I would like to ask it once properly.

"**Which instrument showed you the bend.**"

The room did not move.

Marek Vale did not move either, and he had about a second and a half in which he could have said a great many things, and the reason he said the thing he said is that the Director of the Veyran Weather Office had told him on the Wednesday of the thirty-fourth, in a bare office, three days earlier, exactly what would happen in this room, and had told him what to do about it, and had not told him to lie.

"A plate with no chain behind it in my coat," he said.

The clerk's pen went into the minute.

"That is a plate," said Sabin Orle, "and I am going to ask you the next question and then I am going to stop, and I want you to know that I am stopping on purpose and that I am sorry.

"Where does the plate come from?"

"Assessor—" began Malle.

"I am not asking it," said Sabin Orle, and she did not turn round, and Malle stopped.

The room waited, and Marek Vale waited, and what went across Cassian Rook's face was nothing at all, and what went across Corrin Malle's face was nothing at all, and both of those silences went into the minutes of the thirty-seventh day as silences, in the sense that the minute for that item reads *the witness having stated that the reading was taken from a plate without a registered chain, the assessor declined to pursue the provenance of the plate, and no further question was put.*

Rook did not ask it because he had decided on the Wednesday that the provable wins, every time, and he was not going to let a room put a document next to a smear.

Malle did not ask it because he had made a man give up a reading in a corridor on the Tuesday of the twenty-sixth and had not slept properly since and had a form with one line on it in a building two floors away.

And Sabin Orle did not ask it because she had certified four claims in eleven years on the strength of a rule about chains and she was not going to be the woman who broke the rule in public to win an argument she did not need to win, and because she was, in a chamber on a Saturday morning, doing the fairest available thing, and the fairest available thing was to let a document stand next to another document and not to bring in a third one that neither building owned.

"I am going to record," said Sabin Orle, "that the plate is not a plate the Office owns, and I am going to record that I have said so, and I am going to record that I have not asked the next question, and the Chair and the Deputy have each declined to ask it also, and that is three separate decisions by three separate people in one room and I would like the record to show the number and not the reasons."

"Minuted," said Mrs Ireson, at the end of the table, and Marek understood at that moment, with a clarity that arrived about four seconds late, that the number was going to matter and that nobody in the room would ever know why.

---

The room then did something that Marek had not expected and that he would think about for a long time afterwards, which was that it stopped, and that it was a long watch covering the body, and its reader is **Aldis Prynne**.

"Mr Prynne," said Sabin Orle, "is not a citation. He is a witness of fact and the Chair has admitted him, and I am going to explain to the room why, because I do not want anybody to think I have found a reader to contradict Mr Vale. I have not, and I would be astonished if I had.

"Mr Prynne read the order of the movements on the ninth of this month in a certified run, in a room, on the nineteenth, and he got it right, and he is the only other man in this basin who did. That fact is not in dispute. I want to know where he got it from, and the answer will be dull, and I want the room to hear how dull."

---

Aldis Prynne was about fifty and had been in the building for thirty-one years and had the particular stillness of a man who has spent three decades being careful in a job where being careful is not rewarded and being wrong is remembered. He had read the order in the Ternway reversal in the spring and had got it wrong by about forty minutes and the forty minutes had cost a ferry, and nobody in the building had forgotten it and nobody had ever once said so to his face.

"I had three places," he said. "That is the whole of it and I want the room to have it in one sentence because it is going to sound like more than it is.

"I had the technical cell's file. I had the annex. And I had the ridge's own day sheet for the ninth, which is the third of those and is not the same as the other two and is the only one of the three that is a record of the day rather than a record of what somebody concluded about the day."

"Where in the cell's file?" said Sabin Orle.

"Day file, fourth item, a slip the size of a stamp docket with a date on it and no name on it, initialled, and it says the order in nine words."

"And the annex."

"The annex is the reader's statement bound separately, and the order is not in the annex, and the annex is bound separately because the assessor ordered it on the afternoon of the nineteenth so that a district reading the file could not miss it and could not mistake it for an objection." He said it entirely without any expression. "I read the annex about four times that week and it told me the order was true, and it did not tell me what the order was, and I had a claim to certify in the spring and a Ternway reversal in my own history and I am not a stupid man, so I went and found the order somewhere it was written down."

"And the day sheet."

"The day sheet for the ninth is a printed sheet with a seal on it and it is the Office's own published record of the day, and the order is in it, in the ordinary course, in the sequence column, in the ordinary hand that a day is written up in, and it is in it because that is what a day is."

He stopped, and then he said the thing that made the room go very quiet, and he said it in the flat voice of a man reading an instrument.

"Which is the thing I have never been able to put down in thirty-one years of keeping myself to myself about it. I could not tell you where any of those three came from. All three of them are copies. Every one of the three is a copy of a thing somebody said out loud on a stone floor in the ninth ward on the evening of the ninth of the month, and the Office's own record of the order of the movements on that night is a copy of a copy of a man's voice in a room, and this building has never once been able to say that sentence, and I have been in this building for thirty-one years."

Nobody in the room moved.

"And I want to say the second half of it, because the first half is the sort of thing a man says at the end of a career and I am not at the end of my career, I am on a long watch and there is a water in a basin.

"I did not ask. That is the thing. I did not ask where the voice came from, and I had no reason to, and I have understood in the last thirty seconds, sitting in this room, that if I had asked it, this room would have had to answer it, and I would have found out. And I did not ask, and I would like that minuted, and I would like the Chair to understand that I am not blaming anybody and I am not making a point, I am a reader, I have been a reader for thirty-one years, and a reader's job is to work from what is on the table."

"Minuted," said Mrs Ireson.

---

And then Aldis Prynne turned his head about thirty degrees and looked down the table at Marek Vale, and the two of them had not exchanged a word in their lives, and neither of them ever did again, and this is the only time either of them looked at the other.

"You said Gather alone," said Prynne. "That is what is in the day sheet."

"Yes."

"And Break standing in for an unfinished Weight."

"Yes."

"And the Release is on the sheet as completing, and I have always found that the odd one," said Aldis Prynne, "because a completing Release in a collision is unusual, and I have got a theory about why it is on there, and I have had it for a month and I have not put it in a drawer, which is the only thing about me that anybody in this room would call a fault."

"What is your theory, Mr Prynne?"

"That somebody wrote it down in the room in the wrong column," said Aldis Prynne, "and that a clerk took it out of his hand and copied it into the sequence column at the end of the day because it was the correct order and it was not going to be corrected."

Marek Vale sat at the end of a bench in a chamber with the windows shut and heard a man he had never met describe the accident of his own mouth — the fact that he had said the whole order out loud, in the wrong order, into a room full of strangers who were writing it down, and that a clerk had then, in the ordinary course, put it where it belonged — and understood, entirely, with no argument available to him at all, **that the only corroboration he will ever have in his life is a stranger's, and that it is worthless, and that it is real.**

"Thank you, Mr Prynne," he said.

"That is all right," said Aldis Prynne.

---

Then the assessor asked him the last question of the morning, and she wrote the answer down as fast as she said it, which Mrs Ireson has never once done for anybody in that room.

"Mr Vale. The attestation of the order of the movements on the ninth of the month is in the day file with your name on it, and there is a motion available to a member of the witness to ask that a reader's name be removed from an attestation in the ordinary course, and it is not an accusation, it is a housekeeping thing, and two of them have been brought in eleven years. Do you want it?"

He had about four seconds. It was a sentence he had heard a Deputy say to a copy clerk in a corridor on the third of the month, in ninety seconds, and the clerk had been on a copy desk ever since on a form whose ground was one line and true.

"No," said Marek Vale, "and I want the refusal minuted, and I want the ground of it minuted, because the ground of a thing is the only part of it anybody ever argues with.

"*If the reader's name comes off, the next one signs it alone.*"

Nobody in that room said anything for about four seconds.

"That," said Sabin Orle, "is a sentence I have heard before in this building and I cannot remember where, and I am not going to ask, and it is minuted, and the Chair may add what he likes to it and I would advise him to add nothing."

---

The assessor ruled at about half past twelve that the reader's statement of the nineteenth and the reader's answer of the Saturday were consistent with each other and that consistency was not corroboration, and that the plate was not a plate the Office owns, and that the review had now established the following on the record: **that a claim of a stated route displaces a turn; that a turn on a reach is not a figure in any instrument of this office; and that there is no instrument in this office that can establish what a reach-level turn was worth to a district.**

And she said, before she left, the thing that stopped that morning, and she said it to the table and not to Marek.

"I am going to record one more finding and I am going to record it in the negative, and it is the first of the two halves of the terms of reference and it is the half that the room has been circling for three days.

"Mr Vale has told this room, in general, what a claim displaces. Mr Prynne has told this room that the Office's record of the order of movements on a given evening is a copy of a voice. **And nobody in this review has yet asked a reader in this building where a reading came from.**

"Not once. In thirty-one years, on the evidence of the only witness I have had, the question has never been put."

"Assessor," said Lior Venn, "I have put it."

"You have put it to yourself, in a corridor, to a copy clerk, ninety seconds, on the third of this month, and you answered it yourself, and I was not in the corridor." Sabin Orle looked at the minute. "That is a man finding out something about his own office. It is not the Office asking a reader."

"It is the same thing."

"It is the same thing and it happened in ninety seconds in a corridor by one man," said Sabin Orle, "and the reason the two are not the same thing is that a rule gets written the second time and a question in a corridor does not. I am not minuting that as a criticism of you. I am minuting that the question is now on the table, in a room, in front of nine people, and it was put there by a witness, and he put it there by accident, and he has said so."

She closed the folder.

"Item seven is tomorrow and it is the districts, and the districts are not citations and there is nobody here from either of them, and I have spent two days trying to get that fixed and I have failed, and the reason I have failed is that the terms of reference name the districts *affected*, and a district that is affected is by definition a district that can be shown to have been affected, and the entire purpose of this review is to find out whether one can be shown to have been affected."

Nobody said anything to that either.

"So we will go to the districts tomorrow, with the only two districts that asked to be asked, which is Ferrygate and the ninth ward, and neither of them is in this room, and I have that on the record, and Clerk, minuted, and I would like it minuted in the order of the sentence rather than tidied into a heading."

---

Marek Vale got out of the chamber at about ten past one and stood in the corridor for a while.

He had come in that morning braced for a room. He had got a room.

He had been asked which instrument showed him the bend and he had answered a plate with no chain behind it in his coat, in a room with a clerk in it, and the clerk had written it down, and three men had declined to ask the next question, and the assessor had put the number three in the minutes so that nobody could later pretend it had been one, and a reader he had never met had explained, out loud, and for free, and without being asked, that the Office's whole record of the ninth of the month was a copy of a voice in a room — and had explained it as a curiosity about the record, and not as a thing about a man standing eleven feet away from him, and had done it in a flat voice in a room with a Director in it.

Nobody in that room was on Marek's side, and nobody in that room was against him either, and that was the discovery of the Saturday, and it was worse than the alternative: **there was no faction in that chamber.** There was a Director who was fair, an assessor who was better than fair, a Deputy who was protecting a form with one line on it, a records man who had had an epiphany in a corridor and was frightened of what he had found, a route officer who had published her yard's errors and been priced for it, a district chair who had fought a review into a column, a clerk writing it all down, and a reader who had told the truth about a copy for free in thirty-one years and had never once been asked.

Every one of them had been right. That was the finding, and it was the whole of the finding, and it went into the minutes of the thirty-seventh day at item six in the flat words the assessor had given it, and it reads:

*That the reader's attestation is a fact about the reader, and that this office has not in the history of the instrument asked a reader from which instrument a reading was taken.*

And Marek Vale stood in a corridor in the basin house on the Saturday of the thirty-seventh day of a month in which nothing whatever was happening in the sky, and understood, with the same clarity he had got about the eleventh minute past midnight on the twenty-ninth, and with no relief in it whatever, that he was going to be asked in a room, in about four days, whether he wished his name to remain on the attestation of the order of the movements on the ninth of the month.

Because somebody in that chamber had just built a small, correct, load-bearing, entirely professional piece of machinery, and it did not require anybody to want him caught, and it worked the way a column of differences works, and it had been built in public in eleven minutes by the best records mind in the basin, and nobody would ever be able to show her a hand in it.

That is the difference between a trap and a method, and the difference is that a trap has a person in it.
