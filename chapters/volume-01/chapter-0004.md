# Chapter 4 — The Bell and the Pencil

The bench was number four, and the good thing about number four was that it had the window, and the bad thing about number four was that the window looked north over the yard and the yard was where the noise came from, so that Tovan Iles had spent four years learning to write a ward claim in a voice that did not have to compete with a steam crane.

She had four years on the bench and a bad rack in front of her. The weather had been making noise all week — the bulletins had gone from *unsettled* to *unsettled, western draw* to *unsettled, western draw, watch the low ground* in the space of three days, which in her experience meant the Office was nervous and had not yet decided what nervous was about — and nervous weather generated paperwork, and nervous paperwork generated unattached observations, and unattached observations were her job.

There were fourteen of them on the rack before the watch bell.

That was not a complaint. An unattached observation was a reading with no place, no time, and no effect: a pressure oddity at the ninth ward, a curious trace on the Hearthward feed, a glass-tender's note that the Kestrel line's aneroid disagreed with the ridge by a third of a millimetre and probably needed nothing at all. They went in the rack, they got the day stamp, and at the end of the cycle they were either promoted into a claim or they were not. Most of them were not. That was the correct fate of most readings, and Tovan had been taught that in her first month by a records clerk named Pettigrew who had waved a sheet at her and said, *An observation is not a promise, Tovan. If it isn't made into something somebody can act on, it isn't anything yet, and it never was.*

She believed him. She still believed him. It was the single most useful thing anybody had ever taught her, and it was the reason she had not yet been afraid, and she had worked out over the summer that the day she stopped believing it would be the day she needed to.

What she had not worked out was what it would feel like to be the one who stamps.

The bell was going to go at the hour. Then the rack would clear down to the watch room and the day would belong to whoever was in the chair, and the day clerk's job for a bench is to be finished before the bell and correct after it, in that order of importance.

So when the maintenance hand from the glass shop put a plate-wrapped tube on the end of number four at nine-fifteen, and said his name was Vale, and asked whether he could file a reading, Tovan's first thought was not about the weather.

Her first thought was: he is going to make me put a sheet in that rack with a real number on it, and if it is wrong, it is my hand under my name, and the glass-tender's examination is on the twelfth of next month.

"Sit at the end," she said, "not the working side. What's the reading?"

Marek Vale set the tube on the bench and drew out of it a strip about nine inches long, which was not paper. It was thin glass, and she recognised the type, because the bench had a rack of these for the observation wall and they were the working instrument of a reader's trade and completely useless to a copy clerk.

"There's no claim in it," Marek said.

"Then it's not a filing."

"There's an order."

"Right." She took the strip and upended it to the window light, which was what everybody did and which was a waste of a good pane, and she looked at it. She could read a trace about as well as she could read a bill of lading in the local dialect, which was to say she could tell that there were lines on it and that somebody had wanted lines. "Mr Vale. I can see six movements."

"They're in the wrong order."

"Wrong how?"

"Break comes where Weight should be. Witness comes in the last third, where Release belongs. There's a doubled Route in the middle and Release doesn't finish." He said it evenly. "It's a collision. Three air masses inside one cycle, so the six movements are going to reach the basin out of order. The ridge has had the front at sixty-eight to seventy-four hours for three days and can't agree by six, and the six hours is exactly what you get when you've timed movements against each other and the movements turn up shuffled."

Tovan put the glass down.

"Can I ask you something technical," she said, "and you can say no?"

"Yes."

"Is there weather in that, or is that an order?"

"Both. There's weather in it. Pressure's stacking in the west draw. But I can't give you a place and a time and an effect out of it, and that's the part I can't do, and I know that's the part that counts."

"Right." She turned her chair. "Look. I can put it in the rack. I stamp it unattached, it goes down with the day's sheets, and at the end of the cycle somebody in records looks at the unattached rack and decides whether any of it means anything. Usually it doesn't. And if somebody later wants to say *well, you'd been told*, they can't, because nobody acted on it, and you can't make a thing compellable after the fact out of a piece of paper nobody sent anybody on."

"Fine."

"It's not fine, it's the rule, and I want you to hear it in words because you sound like a man who has read the rule." She turned back. "It is *an observation is not a promise*. If you give me a place and a time and an effect and a witness, then it stops being a reading and it becomes a claim, and a claim is a real thing, and I have to strike the bell, and the ward head lights, and the tower sends a crew, and a family on the fourth row shuts a shop. And that is the moment it stops being mine and starts being theirs. I don't decide any of it. I write down what somebody with instruments says, and I strike the bell, and it goes out. That's the job."

Marek Vale did not say anything for a moment. There was a stillness about him that she was beginning to understand was not calm but arithmetic.

"Then what's the emergency book?" he said.

The answer came out of her before she had decided to give it, which was a thing she was careful about and did not do.

"There's an emergency ledger," she said. "Separate from the bench. If a claim touches a public route, a water system, a hospital, a school, or a market, it goes in the emergency ledger inside one cycle — one cycle being the six hours from one watch bell to the next. Named author, place, time, effect, witnesses, and the expiry. That's so that anybody affected has a thing to hold on to afterwards and something to appeal against, and so that a private person who makes a public claim can't do it quietly." She looked at the rack of fourteen sheets. "Nobody has ever used it on this bench. Not in the four years I've been on it. I know because I've got the file and there's four years of empty in it and one line from before I came, in the old ink, about a ferry."

"Right."

"But you're not touching that book, because there is nothing in your strip with a place in it." She reached for the stamp, and then stopped, and looked at the strip of glass again, and then at him. "And I want to say something to you, and then I want to stamp your sheet, and I want you to hear both of those in that order."

"Go on."

"There was a claim on this bench two winters ago." Tovan set the stamp down again. "A good one. A proper one. Place and time and effect and a bell, and a sponsor, and an expiry, and my pencil across the sponsor line. And the district read the bell and refused it."

She had not planned to go further. She went further.

"They had a plan of their own. They published it, in advance, with the numbers in it, and they were entitled to, and they stood on it, and it was a better plan than mine, and it worked, and nobody was hurt. And I struck the bell for them anyway, because the claim was already written and the author was standing in the room." Tovan sat back. "So that is what a claim is. It is not a thing that happens to a district. It is a thing a district does something about. One of the things they can do is say no. And if they say no, then that is the end of it, and whatever the claim would have displaced stays available, and no record anywhere says so, because nothing happened, and nothing happened is the easiest state in the world to write down and the hardest one to argue with."

"And the other way round," Marek said. "If a number goes out without your pencil on it and somebody acts on it."

"Then it is a claim," said Tovan, "and it is a claim I had no part of and no name in, and the only person who will ever be able to tell you what it cost is the person who acted on it. And that is a bad place for the only witness to be."

The room was very quiet for a moment. Somewhere below, the yard crane took a turn and complained.

Marek's voice, when it came, was the flattest thing in the room. "So a reading that was never acted on is a reading that was never tested. That's not the same as a wrong one."

"No," said Tovan Iles. "It isn't."

"That is the answer to a question I have not asked you yet."

"Then I'm glad I didn't hear it." But she heard it. She heard the shape of it very clearly, because it was the shape of a man choosing a sentence that would hold and had chosen a better one than the sentence he had wanted. She was twenty-seven years old and one examination from a post that paid for glass and a wage and a room over a locksmith's, and she let the sentence stand, because she had not yet learned to push, and because there were fourteen sheets on the rack and the bell was twenty minutes off.

She stamped it. UNATTACHED OBSERVATION, day seven, first hour, bench four, and the small red number of the sheet in the rack, and she wrote the time and the ward on the tab, which was the ninth ward, and she wrote his name against the source, which was unusual, because a source that gave a name was a source that could be asked a question, and nobody ever wants their sheet to be asked a question.

"Vale, M., glass shop, sequence plate, ninth ward, cut from the ward's replacement pane, unlogged, six movements traced out of order." She read it back. "That says the glass is a piece of somebody else's notice and there is no chain behind it, doesn't it."

"It says the glass is a piece of somebody else's notice and there is no chain behind it," Marek agreed. "It doesn't say I got a good trace out of it."

"Mm," said Tovan, and stamped it, and put it in the rack, and that was that. The whole of it took four minutes, and one of those four minutes was the whole of how authority works in the city of Veyra, and she had felt it go through her hand like a cold coin.

Then, off the record, with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the ward heads along the wall and her hand nowhere near the bell:

"Mr Vale. A real question, and you can go away."

"Ask it."

"If a reading is never turned into anything that anybody acts on. If it just sits in a rack and it gets filed at the end of the cycle and nobody is sent anywhere because of it." She was aware that her voice had gone somewhere she did not usually go. "Does it count as having predicted anything? At all? Or is it only a prediction if it ends up doing something?"

Marek Vale did not hurry. She had given a great many people an answer to that question and this was the first time she had watched one of them consider it.

"Two parts," he said. "The prediction happened or it didn't. That's not a matter of what people did with it. If the sky does the thing, it did it, and my being slow on a bench doesn't put it back." He turned the strip of glass over on the table, once, and did not look at it while he did it. "But a reading that nobody acted on is a reading that was never tested. And that's not the same as a wrong one, and it's not the same as a right one either. It's just untested. And you can't put it in front of a family on the fourth row and say *we told you*, because they can quite reasonably say *you never told anybody, and you had a bell, and the bell was a foot away from your hand.*"

"That," said Tovan Iles, "is a very good answer to a different question."

"Sorry."

"No," she said, and did not push, and the bell went at ten exactly with a flat brass sound that was louder in that room than in any other room in the building, and the strike arm dropped, and the ninth ward's head did nothing at all, which it had not done for a fortnight, because the pane in that tower was a temporary with a bubble in it and the ninth ward could not publish a legible state even to itself.

"Watch bell," she said, and stood up, and started putting the rack into its case, and did not look at him, and did not have to.

At the far end of the long room, past the pigeonholes and the wet coats, four people came in off the step and stood dripping on the mat, and one of them was a tall woman of forty-six in a wet assembly coat with the Hearthward ward pin on it and rain running off the hem onto the floor, because she had come up the steps from the market without an umbrella and had not cared.

She was looking at the bench the way people look at a counter they have been told to come to.

"Is this where it happens?" she said. "The bell. The boards. Somebody's supposed to be able to tell us what the Office is going to publish for Hearthward before tomorrow, and I have been down at the market since six this morning being told that it is coming, and nobody will write it down."

Behind her, a younger man put a ledger on the bench and opened it and squared the edge of it with the flat of his hand, which was the gesture of a man who had done it every day for twenty years and was going to do it tomorrow.

Tovan Iles looked at the four of them, and then at the maintenance hand from the glass shop standing at the end of her bench with a strip of sequence glass on the table and a tube under his arm, and she had a very clear and completely unprofessional thought, which was: oh, no.

Because she could see, without any weather instrument at all, that the only shape that would get a district anything before a storm arrived was a claim. And to write a claim she needed a place, a time, an effect, and a witness, and she had one of those four in her hand and a bell she had never struck for a weather claim in her life, and it was five past ten, and the front was forty-three hours out.
