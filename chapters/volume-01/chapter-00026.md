# Chapter 26 — A Roster With a Hole In It

The request came back up off the board on the morning of the twenty-sixth, and it came back with a date on it, and Marek Vale found out about it in the outer office because the outer office got everything.

Two sheets. The first was the districts' letter, and the second was a printed Council answer with the Council's seal on it, and both of them had been in the building since the previous afternoon and both of them had been read by about nine people.

He read them standing up, because there was a bench, but he read them standing.

*From the Kestrel Yards route office and the ninth ward district office, to the Weather Council and the Weather Office. — The joint request of the ninth of the month is re-filed. Its terms are unaltered: the western draw may be used either to hold the Kestrel corridor open or to ease the exposed floodplain of the lower basin, and these are the same air. A body of weather is now upon the basin. We ask that the request be answered before the weather is upon us and not after.*

*Answer of the Council, returned the twenty-sixth. — That the method certified on the nineteenth is sound and its report is before the seats. That districts are advised to prepare in the ordinary course and to return prepared. That the office is unable to certify a claim covering two goods which require the same air, and that this incompatibility is not a matter of degree. That the office will supply figures to both parties on receipt of a stated requirement.*

Marek read the second paragraph four times.

That was the whole of it. It is the correctest piece of drafting he has read in a month, and there is not a word in it anybody could argue with, and he found that he could not construct a single objection to it, and that this was the problem, and that he had three days' notice.

*Prepare in the ordinary course and return prepared.*

Eleven words. The first four are a district's own business and cost the district whatever the district's own business costs, and the last three are an instruction to a city, and the two halves of the sentence are not compatible and cannot be made compatible, and the reason they cannot is not a subtlety.

If the low wards prepare, they prepare to keep the water off their own ground, and a district prepared to keep the water off its own ground is a district whose people are in their own basements and whose carts are pointed at their own streets. That district cannot then be *returned*. To be returned, a district has to be somewhere it can be got from, at an hour somebody names, with its carts facing the other way.

The Office was not asking the low wards to defend themselves. It was asking them to be clear. And that is not a harder sentence to write, and it is not a dishonest one, and every word of it is true, and a Deputy can put it on a sheet with a seal on it in nine minutes and go and stand at the back of a room and be right about the weather.

Marek had read a good sentence in a corridor on the nineteenth of the month that said the same thing more slowly, and the woman who had said it had lost nothing for saying it.

---

He went and found Corrin Malle at eleven, because there was one thing he wanted and he was entitled to ask for it, and he wanted it before the weather got here.

"The long watch," he said. "The Bellweather watch for the body and the peak. It's your roster. I'm not on it."

"No," said Malle, without looking up.

"You know I'm not on it. You've known since the eleventh."

"I have known since the eleventh and I did not put your name on it and I am not going to explain myself to you in a corridor on a Tuesday." Malle put a pencil down, which was the thing he did before he said anything he had arranged. "The first watch was staffed on the ninth of the month and stood down on the eleventh, and you were the reader on it, and it was the best-staffed watch this office has run in four years, and it worked, and one of the four crews went out at six twenty in the evening into something nobody had ordered them into and came back an hour and ten minutes later with two men shaking, and that is the only reason the district still trusts this building."

"I know all of that. I was on it."

"And the second watch is going up today for the body and the peak, and it is a six-day watch, and it needs a reader on it for six days, and I have got a man on it." He turned round. "His name is Aldis Prynne. He read the order in the Ternway reversal in the spring and he got it wrong by about forty minutes and the forty minutes cost a ferry and nobody has forgotten it inside a year. He is a good reader and he is a careful one and he will get this one right, and I have given him the whole of the ninth of the month to work from because I have had the whole of the ninth of the month to work from, and it is on the ridge in three places and it is in the technical cell's file and it is in the annex, because I put it in all three myself."

Marek stood in that corridor for about four seconds.

"You put it in the annex," he said.

"I put it in the annex," said Malle. "Nine seats were going to read that report and I was not going to have them read it with a hole in it because I did not want to argue with them about the hole. You said it out loud in a room and I put it in a file. Those are different acts by the same man and neither of them is a favour and I would like you to stop looking for one."

"I wasn't looking for one."

"You were looking for something," said Malle, "and it was not going to be anything, and now you have it and it is a reader's name on a roster and you are going to say thank you and go away."

---

"I can time the order," Marek said.

"I know you can. That is not the question and you know it is not the question, and if you want me to say the question then the question is this." Malle came round the desk. "You are the best reader in this basin and I have said that about you in a room twice in a month and I will say it again on Friday if anybody gives me an opening. And there is a document-shaped hole in you that is visible from the front door, and every room in this building can see it, including the room you were in. So when I need a man in a room in four days' time — and I will need a man in a room in four days' time, and I will not know which room until the morning of it — I am not going to send for the man who cannot produce a sheet of paper. That is not spite. That is the only sentence I have got and I have had it ready since the nineteenth and I have been waiting to see whether you would make me use it."

Marek did not have an answer, and it was not a rhetorical silence, and Malle let it stand for about three seconds and then went back to his desk.

"Also," he said, "I am not being cruel to you, and I want that on the record in my own office with nobody in it, because I am aware of how this looks. You are off the field list because you are unprovable in public, and every single thing you have said since the nineteenth has been correct, and one of them cost me a whole morning of Council time and one of them was the best work anybody in that building has done this year. Both of those are true at the same time and the second one is the reason the first one is."

---

He did not go away.

He asked the question at ten past twelve, in the field office, with the door open, because the only favour he had left was a question.

"Malle. What does the order of the movements do to a western corridor?"

The pencil went into the ear.

"That is not a small question," said Malle. "That is a two-day question and it is a question about a basin, and I have got a hundred and eighty people in a technical cell and a claim to certify in a fortnight, and you are asking me on a Tuesday at ten past twelve."

"You have had the whole ninth of the month," Marek said. "You said so yourself. You put it in three places. So put it in a fourth."

Malle sat down.

He was quiet for perhaps a minute and a half, and Marek watched a fifty-four-year-old man with thirty years of field in him decide to be wrong in a small way because it was a Tuesday and because the man asking was the only person in the city who could use the answer.

"It does two things," said Malle. "The first is the one everybody gets wrong. The movements out of order do not make a corridor better or worse; they make it *repeat*. A corridor is not a road, it is an hour that you can put a hull in, and an hour is made of a pressure gradient standing between two airs, and if the movements arrive in the wrong order the gradient stands up twice in the wrong place and comes down once, and a lane that should have been two hours long is an hour and a half, or a lane that should have been an hour and a half is two hours. The sequence does not change the corridor. The sequence changes where the corridor is in the day."

"And the second?"

"The second is the one that is in the joint request," said Corrin Malle. "And it is the one that nobody will write down, because if it is written down then it looks like an argument, and it is not an argument, it is arithmetic." He took the pencil back out of his ear and looked at the point of it. "The draw that opens a corridor over that lake is the same draw that lifts water off a low reach. It is one pull of air. There is a version of tomorrow in which that pull comes up the western side, hard, all afternoon, and it holds a corridor for six hours and puts two feet of water on a low ground for six hours. And there is a version in which the pull comes in soft and short, and you get a lane for two hours and the low ground drains. And these are the same air and the second one is not the worse of the two for anybody on the ridge, and it is much the worse of the two for a district with a population in it, and both of those statements are true and both of them are about the same afternoon."

"Which means the choice is not a choice."

"No," said Malle. "It means the choice is a decision about who is standing where at the end of it, and it is not made by the air."

He put the pencil on the desk.

"You already had all of that," he said.

"Yes."

"Then why did you ask me?"

"Because I wanted it said out loud in an office," said Marek Vale, "by a man who is not me, on a Tuesday, while it is still cheap."

---

Malle did not take a reading off him. That is the part Marek understood afterwards, in the way you understand things late, standing in a shed or on a bank or on a bridge in the rain.

He had been ready for it. He had come in with the order on the tip of his tongue, the whole of the ninth of the month, Gather alone and Weight unfinished and Break on top of it and Route twice and Release complete and Witness on the Break — he had it ready, and it was true, and it was in three files on the ridge, and it belonged to a plate that had been in a locked room in a poor ward's tower, and if Malle took it, then for the rest of that week there would be a reading in this building that had come from a man with a tube in his coat, and every future claim that man made in this basin would be answered with the phrase *that is the reader who could not produce a sheet on the nineteenth*.

He did not offer it.

"You have something," said Malle, "and you are not going to say it, and I am not going to ask you for it, and we are both going to spend the rest of the week pretending that is a coincidence."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Get out of my office," said Corrin Malle, "and when the Council sits, bring nothing. Bring a hat. If you bring a reading into a certifying room from a plate with no chain behind it, you will not be asked a question about it, Vale. You will be asked where it came from, and you will answer, and that will be the end of you."

Marek went and got his hat.

---

The long Bellweather watch went up on the field office board at four o'clock on the twenty-sixth, in a new hand, with a new pin in a new hole, and it covered the body and the peak and it ran six days, and it had four crews, two pump houses, a district warden, a second reader, and a name on the reader's line.

The ninth ward's head on the wall did nothing at all, as it had not for a fortnight and would not for a long time to come, and a district of four hundred and forty people went on reading four women's handwriting.

And on the thirty-first of a month, at the south-bank shed, a banded timber stack and a replacement dewatering pump stood loaded under a tarpaulin, with nine crew assigned and no instruction of any kind outstanding, waiting on a window that was not a weather window and would not be anybody's business when it came.

Marek read the roster from the front, twice, standing at the back, the way a man stands at a roster when his name is not on it and he has come to look for somebody else's.
