# Chapter 29 — The Ward Ledger Bench

A docket came down off the ridge at half past three in the afternoon on the twenty-ninth day of the Bellweather autumn, in an envelope, and it had a violet square on the corner of the sheet inside it, and the violet square had a bell struck across it and the Director's name under it.

*T. Rook, Director. Authorized. Bellweather. — A single claim shall cover the yard side and the low side.*

That was the whole of the authority. It was three lines long and it did not specify a place, a time, an effect, an alternative, a sponsor or an expiry, because none of those were its business; it was an authorization to enter a claim, and it came from the same man who had read a two-line request out of a printed sheet on the Wednesday and asked for a number and been given one.

Tovan Iles read it twice, which she did with everything, and then she put it in the tray under the bell and went and got the board.

"Sit down, Mr Vale."

"Does it need to be certified?"

"No," said Tovan Iles. "That is the entire point of it and I would like you to sit down and notice it. It is not certified. Certification is the Council's business and it takes a sponsor, an expiry, an alternative, a concurring seat, a reader's attestation and a positive routing effect at the yards. You do not have the last of those and you have told me so yourself, twice, and I have written it down, and it is on your file under a heading that says *offered without certification, witness's own statement.*" She squared the board. "This is a Director's authorization. It goes on the ward ledger. It gets a bell. There is no concurring seat because no seat is required, and there is no assessor, and there is nobody anywhere in the building who has to be satisfied that the routing effect is positive."

She put her pen down on the bench, which was the thing she did before she said the rest of it.

"Nothing compels anybody. That is what a claim under an authorization is. Nothing in this sheet makes the Kestrel Line come. It makes you answerable. The only things it can do are put your name on a thing that goes into the world and make the bell ring."

"Yes," said Marek.

"You said *yes* very quickly for a man who has spent a fortnight being careful."

"I've spent a fortnight being careful and I've been careful and it got the trial into a folder with a records stamp under it," said Marek. "I have a claim with a good alternative on it and a sponsor's name and an expiry the same line as the effect, and it is not going to be certified, and I am going to enter it this afternoon, and I would like you to understand that I know exactly what that means and that I have thought about it for two days."

"Have you."

"Yes."

Tovan Iles looked at him for a moment longer than she usually allowed herself, and then she drew the emergency ledger out of the cabinet where it had been since the seventh of the month.

---

She wrote it in one hand without lifting the pen, as she had written the Hearthward claim and the certification sheet and the four lines of an attested minute, and it took her two minutes and forty seconds, and the only time the pen came up was at the ends of lines.

*Place: the Kestrel corridor — the western lane over the reach, from the point to the yards' mark.*
*Trigger: the Kestrel Line's arrival at the yards' mark, accepted.*
*Effect, as entered: the Kestrel corridor remains passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth.*
*Listed alternative, as required: if the Line's arrival at the yards' mark is not accepted, no corridor is claimed; the draw backs at the western end, the return lane closes on the published band, the four carriers' window is not met, and the low wards' floodplain takes the draw for the same hours.*
*Expiry: 04:00 on the thirtieth day.*
*Sponsor: M. Vale, reader.*
*Entered under the Director's authorization of the twenty-seventh. Not certified.*

She read it back, and then she put her hand flat on the sheet and squared the book against the edge of the bench with the flat of her hand, which meant that the thing was in the world and could not be got back.

"Mr Vale. You read the Hearthward one out twice."

"I did."

"Why are you not reading this one twice?"

"Because the second reading is how you find out whether you are sure," said Marek. "And I am not looking for that this afternoon. And I read the last one twice because I was not sure, and the only useful thing the second reading did was make me stay in the room for another ninety seconds, and I do not need ninety seconds. I need the bell."

Tovan Iles reached over and pulled the brass arm of the entry bell.

One strike, for the yards' ward.

Along the wall above the bench, the indicator for the yards' ward head came up and stayed up, clear, the way it had come up for Hearthward on the seventh of the month and had stayed up all day.

And the ninth ward's head did nothing at all.

---

It had not done anything for a fortnight. Marek had watched it not do it twice on the seventh — once with the watch bell going at ten in the morning and an unentered emergency ledger lying open on the bench in front of him, and again at eleven when he put his name under a claim and put his hand flat on the table afterwards — and on the fifteenth with a permanent brass surround bedded by his own hands around a half-size bubbled temporary with a flaw in the middle of it the size of a thumbnail.

Underneath, the tower's feed was running on the best plate he had ever cut. A spare was on the shelf in its box, dated, not fitted. And the frame above the second landing had two sets of screw holes in the iron, an old pair counter-sunk and rust-brown from four hundred years of weather, and a newish pair from a batten, and the good glass for the old pair was in a glassblower's shop at Kestrel Yards, quoted at the end of last year, ordered, and paid for, on a district's account that could not cover a man and a crate sent forty miles, and would never cover it, and would never be sent.

He had put a brass surround around the hole. That was the week of the thirteenth. He had bedded a permanent frame around a temporary, which is either good work or an obscenity depending on who is asked, and the reason he had done it was that a tower with a dead feed seat and a dead notice is a tower that is finished, and that ward has nothing else.

And there was a claim in the world now, with a bell on it, whose *alternative* said in plain words that the ninth ward's floodplain would take the draw for the same hours, and the ninth ward had no way of being told that, and had not had a way of being told anything at all since the middle of the month.

Nobody in that room would have understood the particular thing that Marek was looking at. Tovan Iles was writing the entry in the book and giving the sheet to the arch wire and would have said, if asked, that the ninth ward's head was dark, in the tone of a woman reporting a fault.

He watched the dark head for about four seconds and then he said, out loud, to nobody, in the flattest voice he had:

"The ninth ward cannot read this."

"Marek." Tovan had the wire in her hand. "Do not do that. Do not say that in this room on the day you have entered a claim. It is not an argument against the claim and if it is read as one I will have to write something in the book and I do not want to write something in the book."

"I am not making an argument. I am stating a fact about a light."

"Then state it to yourself," said Tovan Iles, "and put the sheet on the wire."

---

He put the sheet on the wire and the arch wire took it and the docket with the violet square went with it, because a claim entered under a Director's authorization travels with the authorization attached, and that is the whole of the difference between the two kinds and it is in the tray, and Tovan Iles put both sheets in the tray and squared the tray.

Then she said, without any particular weight at all, as a piece of housekeeping between two items on an afternoon's list:

"Your left ear."

"What about it?"

"It has been half a tone up since the seventh and I have been listening to you lean towards the right for three weeks," said Tovan Iles, "and you have not mentioned it and I am not going to mention it to a doctor, because if a doctor writes *hearing, left, reader* on a maintenance hand's file then that maintenance hand's file stops being a maintenance hand's file. I am mentioning it to you. Do something about it or do not, but do not let it be a surprise in a room."

Marek put his hand up to his left ear the way a man does about four times a day, and found that it was no worse than it had been on the seventh, and no better, and understood, with the particular clarity of a man who has a rule about it now, that he was relieved.

---

The plate was in the tube in his coat and the coat was on a hook by the bench in the maintenance shop, and he got the tube out at about five and set the plate on the frame and lit the lamp, because he had an hour and a half of good light left in the day and because he wanted to be certain.

There was a panel standing across the middle of the glass, and it had not been there at seven o'clock that morning, and it had been standing there since about half past four.

```
CLAIM: the Kestrel corridor remains passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth; the Kestrel Line's arrival at the yards' mark is accepted.
ONE POSSIBLE TURN WILL CLOSE.
```

He read it twice.

It did not say which turn. It had not said which turn at eleven o'clock on the seventh of the month in front of a copy clerk and four people who had walked up a hill in the rain, and it had not said which turn on the nineteenth in a shuttered room in front of twenty-six people, and it was not going to say which turn now, and the reason it did not say which turn was the same reason a ticket machine does not tell you where the money went.

It gave him the exact words that were on the sheet on the arch wire. It told him, in the flattest possible voice, that one of the things still available in the world had stopped being available, and it did not tell him what it was, and it was not going to, and there was no arrangement of reading, or lamp, or tilt, or eleven years of practice that would make it tell him, and the only way this city had ever found to know what a claim had spent was for a reader to be the one who looked.

It had been standing on a dark plate in a cold shop for the half hour since the moment she finished the word *certified*, and he had not seen it because he had not been looking at it. He had been looking at a corridor. He had had it in his coat on a hook for the whole of that half hour, four feet from a bench on which a clerk was writing without lifting her pen, and no instrument in this basin had ever been built to say a thing like that out loud.

He put his hand flat on the bench and made sure the room was solid, the way a man does, and then he read the western draw off the last two days of the run in the ordinary way — down once through the whole of it, up movement by movement out loud, once with the lamp cold, once tilted. And the shape he had been carrying in his head for two days was on the plate, and it was the shape he had expected it to be, and the expecting was the part he did not enjoy afterwards.

The draw came up the western side in a long band. A long band is a lane and a lane is a corridor and a corridor is an hour, and the hour is made of a pressure gradient standing between two airs, and a long band has a *bend* in it. Under the long band there was a hook, a short one, which came off the long band about two thirds of the way along and ran for a quarter of an hour, and then the long band picked up again underneath it and went on. He had read that shape on the ninth of the month on a stone floor in front of nineteen people and had given it out loud as *Route twice — a short hook under a long one*. He had been describing a feature of the collision. He had not known then that it was a description of a feature of a draw.

A hook is not a lane. A lane wants the long one.

But a hook in a draw is where a basin dumps. It is the air easing for a quarter of an hour, and in that quarter of an hour the basin's outflow runs free, and the reach at the bottom of it goes down to the lowest point of the whole month, and a flatbed and a replacement pump and nine men can stand on the flat at the bottom of the reach for about forty minutes, and then the long band comes back over the top of it and puts the water back. The reach comes down when the basin's draw is on it; Petrie had told Tarin Senn that on the eleventh of the month standing in a shed door, and it was on a board in a yard in chalk, and it was correct, and it was also the reason the mark on the post at the edge of the flat showed when it showed.

And the hook was on the plate, and the hook was the ebb, and the ebb was the window, and the window was about forty minutes wide and it was on the morning of the thirtieth, and it was the only morning in about nine days.

He had the other half of it too, and Malle had put it into him in a corridor on the Tuesday in the flattest possible voice, and there was nothing in it that was anybody's opinion: *a corridor is an hour that you can put a hull in, and an hour is made of a pressure gradient standing between two airs.* A claim that holds an hour does not improve the hour. It makes it dependable, and a dependable hour is not a variable one, and the sequence does not distinguish between a thing that is probable and a thing that is fixed, and a fixed turn has no bend in it, because a bend is a thing you might or might not get and a claim has removed the might.

So the claim takes the long band — it takes it because the long band is what the claim is for — and the hook goes with it, because the hook is the turn that most directly contradicts a long band held to certainty. And there is no wind in it, and that was the sentence he had gone over eleven times in two days and it was the sentence that made him put his hand flat on the bench a second time.

Nothing about this is a weather event. There is no front, no belt, no collision, no lead and no cycle, and nothing whatever in the sky on the morning of the thirtieth will be different from the morning of the twenty-ninth, because the reach is the reach and the reach comes down on the basin's draw and the basin's draw is a matter of how much water the basin is trying to move, and no amount of rain changes that, and no instrument in the Weather Office of this city is built to look at it.

A reach-level window is not a weather window.

That is not a spell and it is not a curse and nobody has to do anything at all, anywhere, ever, to bring it about. The banded stack stays banded. The pump stays on its pallet under its cover with the Union guarantee on the drum. Nine men are assigned and no instruction is outstanding. Nobody has cancelled it and nobody has stood it down, and there is no document in Veyra, in this basin, in any office, that says the convoy will not go to the Lower Sill headwall on the morning of the thirtieth, and there is not going to be one, because to write one a person would first have to decide something.

---

He put the plate down on the bench and put both hands on the frame on either side of it, and sat there in a cold shop for about four minutes, and did not take a reading, and did not need to.

Then he put the plate back in the tube and the tube in his coat, and went down the hill, because the yard wanted doing before the lamps and the *Petrel* wanted her morning checks and there was a man on the boards at Kestrel Yards who had said yes.

---

The yard at nine o'clock on the Friday evening had four ships on the apron, a lift that worked, and about forty people on the concrete because a yard does its evening work in a crowd.

Nessa Tarrow had three sheets on the board. The Office's figure, sealed, with its hour. The Line's figure, with the hour it was taken. And the third column, which was about half an inch wide, and which had been half an inch wide for eight days and had been nothing at all on the twentieth.

Above them, on a new line, in Nessa's careful hand, with no instruction and no note:

*Claim entered, 29th, ward ledger bench. Conditional. The Kestrel corridor remains passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth. Sponsor M. Vale, reader. Director's authorization. Not certified.*

Tarin Senn was standing at the boards with her hands behind her back.

"Read it out," she said, when she saw him. "Read that out properly, in order, because in about six hours four hundred people are going to be told by somebody else and I want this yard to have heard it first."

He read it out. He read the place and the trigger and the effect and the alternative and the expiry, and he read the last line, *not certified*, twice, and the yard listened the way yards listen, which is completely, with one man still doing up a lift rope and not stopping.

"That is on the board before the ships go," said Tarin. "Third line. And your reading goes up before the claim, and it is up, and it is on the same board as the Office's, and the difference is a thumb wide, and Ilva has the launch book." She looked at him. "You are the sponsor."

"Yes."

"So if the corridor shuts at four o'clock instead of half past four, then the sheet in the basin house has your name on it and not mine, and every carrier in this yard is going to read the difference column and find that you were an hour and a half optimistic, in public, for a season, on your own plate."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Then go up the hill and sleep," said Tarin Senn, "and be wrong in the morning, because the wind window is at four and the *Petrel* will be over the point at half past, and if you are not on that apron when she comes round I will leave you."

He got on the tram at ten with the tube in his coat and the two readings and a filing mark in the left cuff and a claim in the world that was entirely true, and entered in a good hand, by a clerk who wrote without lifting the pen, with a Director's approval stamp on the sheet and a bell struck for the yards and nothing at all struck for the ninth.

The wind window was at four the next morning, and it had been at four the previous afternoon, and it would be at four again on every wet afternoon and every grey morning for as long as a body of weather sat on that lake, and not one of those hours was on the sheet.

He slept about two hours, which was more than he had been getting, and he did not dream, and he woke at half past two and lay in the dark for an hour and a half and thought about a hook of air under a long band, a quarter of an hour long, on the last morning of a month, forty minutes wide, a thousand yards from where he was lying, with nine men and a stack of timber and a replacement dewatering pump already loaded and waiting for a morning that had nothing wrong with it at all.
