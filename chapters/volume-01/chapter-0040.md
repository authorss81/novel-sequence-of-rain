# Chapter 40 — Nobody Asked Me For A Copy

The Ferrygate district council met on the Sunday of the thirty-eighth day at ten in the morning because the district council had always met on the Sunday at ten in the morning since about the time of the war, and because a Sunday was the only day on which six of the nine of them could be there without losing a day, and because the district's one ambition, in the words of its clerk of nineteen years, was to get through a week in which nothing interesting happened.

There were nine people in it and a chair who was a chandler, and a table with a jug on it, and a minute book, and a Council assessor sitting at the end of the table with a clerk of her own beside her, which had been arranged by telephone on the Thursday and which Nell Cray had met at the door herself and given a chair and a cup of tea and had then apologised for, twice, for the state of the room.

Nobody looked pleased. That is worth saying about Ferrygate and it is the only thing about that room that is remarkable: nobody in it had wanted this and everybody in it was extremely competent.

"Right," said the assessor. "Mrs Cray. I am going to ask you a small number of questions and I am going to tell you before I start that none of them is an accusation, that four of them have been answered in writing and lodged in my folder, that I am not here to make you produce anything, and that if you have a document I have not seen I would rather have it in this room than in a file in the city."

"Then ask," said Nell Cray.

The assessor had her folder square on the table with a pen across it, and she had done that in a Council chamber on a Saturday and she did it at a district table on a Sunday, and Nell Cray noticed it and understood it, and thought later that the woman had done it in order to be read as neutral, and understood by the time she was halfway down her second cup of tea that the woman had done it because it was a professional habit and not a tactic, and that the difference was worth something.

"Three questions about the twenty-first. The first is a question of fact and I am going to write your answer down exactly as you say it.

"Did the district hold a low-ground shelter rota for the twenty-first?"

"Yes."

"Where was it lodged?"

Nell Cray put her cup down.

That was the whole of it. That was the question, and it was the question nobody had asked, and the reason nobody had asked it was that it had not occurred to anybody that there was anything to ask about, and it had not occurred to anybody because the district's whole method of operation for nineteen years had been a set of arrangements that nobody upstream had ever known existed, and the reason upstream had not known was that nothing about them was a matter of public record, and the reason that was not a failure is that there is no form in this basin for a district's ordinary arrangements.

"Nowhere," said Nell Cray. "It was not lodged anywhere. It is in the district book."

"Where is the district book?"

"On the shelf in my office under the window," said Nell Cray, "between the return for the spring boundary stone and the return for the lamp glass, where it has been since the winter before last, because that is where it was filed the first time and nobody has ever moved it."

"And who was on it?"

"Ottilie Fisk, four Quay Steps, ground floor. Abel Fisk, four Quay Steps, ground floor." She said it evenly and she did not look at the minute book and the minute book was open on the table in front of the chandler, who had been a member of this district for forty years and had read the sheet at some point in the last six years because everyone did, because it is a district book and it is on a shelf. "Two names, six years, moved on every wet winter and twice in the wet. It has never been sent anywhere and it has never been a notice and I have never once entered a claim on it."

"Was it cancelled on or about the twenty-first?"

"No."

"Why not?"

And here Nell Cray did the thing she had been doing for seventeen days, which is to answer the question the assessor had actually asked rather than the one on the paper, and she had got extremely good at it and she had not told anybody she was doing it.

"Because it could not have been," she said. "A decision can be responded to and a piece of paper in a book cannot. Nobody was ever told about it, so nobody decided about it, so there was nothing to withdraw. It stopped being a plan. There is no event. There is no date on it and no reason on it and no box on it for a reason, and I have never wanted a box for a reason, because the reason was a boat put in for three days in the winter six years ago and a woman who had turned out to be perfectly well, and I have never wanted anybody to know that, and it is the only thing on that sheet that anybody would want to know."

The room was very quiet.

"Clerk," said the assessor, "will you enter that answer exactly."

Mrs Ireson was not there. It was a clerk of the Council's own named Denby, twenty-six, who had never in his life taken a minute in a room and who had brought a folding table for the assessor to write on and had no idea what he was in for. He wrote it down and got about two thirds of it and looked up.

"Slow down, please."

"I am not going to slow down," said Nell Cray. "I have been writing down the whole of it since the twenty-first and I have had seventeen days and I have got it in a better hand than yours and I would be glad to read it to you, and you may take it down, and I would prefer it if you did."

---

So Nell Cray read it out, and the clerk took it down, and the whole of the answer was about a hundred and forty words and it was the only account of the twenty-first in the district's possession and it had never been written down anywhere before, and it was composed, in order, of the following facts, and every one of them was true:

*No notice in Veyra has ever mentioned the low-ground shelter rota, and it was not sent to the water office, the Council, or the market factor, and it is not in the four-line letter of the nineteenth day.*

*Two of the district's three preparations for the twenty-first were decided about. The rain-garden clearing rota was withdrawn in the letter of the nineteenth, on the agenda, minuted, and returned to ordinary work on the twenty-first morning. The ferry's extended night hours were cancelled in the same letter, on the same agenda, in the same minute, and posted down again on the twenty-first evening.*

*The third was not decided about and could not have been. It was a two-line sheet in the district book, held since the winter before last, moved on by the clerk in boots.*

*The clerk is of the opinion, and will state it if it is wanted, that if the sheet had been on the agenda of the meeting at which the four-line letter was agreed, somebody would have asked what it was for.*

*It is a piece of paper about two old people on the Quay Steps with no stairs, in a district where the ground floors flood twice a winter by design, and somebody would have said that that is not this, and the clerk would have said that it is not this and it is cheap and it is in the book and I move it myself, and somebody would have said very well, and it would have been minuted, and it would have been a decision, and a decision can be responded to.*

And then, in the flat voice, at the end:

*The district's position is that a piece of paper in a book is not a preparation until somebody has read it, and that a thing nobody has been told about cannot be responded to, and that this is not a complaint against the Council, and that the district has never in nineteen years made one, and that the district's clerk would like it recorded that she made four copies of the sheet after the twenty-first and sent one up the hill to the warden's council with a covering note, and that this was done because she had learned that a thing kept once is a thing that can be lost, and that it has since occurred to her that four copies of a thing nobody was told about are four copies of a thing nobody was told about.*

The chandler, who had been a member of this district for forty years, said, "Nell."

"Yes."

"You never told us."

"No," said Nell Cray.

---

The assessor let about thirty seconds go by, which was generous, and then said:

"Two more questions and then I am going to ask you for the sheet, and I am telling you that I am going to ask you for the sheet before you offer it, so that the offer and the request are on the record separately."

"Ask me."

"May I see it?"

Nell Cray got up, went out, and came back with the district book, and put it on the table in front of the assessor, open, at the right place, because she knew the place.

The assessor looked at it for a while. Everybody at that table looked at it for a while. It is a piece of paper with two lines on it in a clerk's hand, six years old, and it is the only object in the history of this basin that joined a claim to a room in which two people had stood in water, and it is worth precisely nothing, and it is the most frightening document Nell Cray has ever handled.

"That is the original," said the assessor.

"That is the original," said Nell Cray. "It has never been copied. It is the only copy. That is the reason I cannot give you a copy of it, and the reason I sent you a copy of a copy is that a copy of a copy is a piece of paper in a district file with a district stamp on it, and a piece of paper like that can be produced by anybody, and the sheet in front of you can be produced by nobody, because it is not a copy of anything."

"Yes," said the assessor. "I understand that completely, and I have understood it since the fifteenth, and I want to say that what you have just done is the most correct piece of records work anybody has done in front of me in eleven years, and I am going to rule that the copy in my folder is accepted, and filed, and I am going to rule it in about ninety seconds, and I would like you to understand that the ruling is the correct one and that it is going to cost you the only thing you wanted today."

"Go on," said Nell Cray.

"The copy in my folder is admitted," said Sabin Orle. "It is a true copy of a district record made by a district clerk in her own hand, dated and signed, and it establishes that the district held a low-ground shelter rota for the twenty-first, of two named persons at a numbered address, and that the district held it in the ordinary course for six years and moved it on it in the ordinary course.

"It establishes that a preparation existed. Clerk, enter it in those words.

"It does not establish, and cannot establish, and I want the minutes to say why, and the why is not a refusal, Mrs Cray, and it is the most important sentence I have said in this review."

---

She put both hands flat on the table the way she did it in the basin house, and the chandler looked at her hands and then at the ceiling.

"A claim displaces the turn that most directly contradicts it," said Sabin Orle. "And a turn is measured — by this Council, by the method, by the instrument — against a **published alternative** or a **lodged preparation**. That is how the method knows what it displaced. Not because anybody can see the turn. Because a claim has to list its alternative, and the alternative goes on the record, and a district's response to a published alternative is a fact that can be exhibited.

"Your shelter rota was neither. It was not published and it was not lodged, and therefore it was not before the method, and therefore it was not among the things the method could see when it took a turn, and therefore a district holding one is not a fact about a displacement. It is a fact about a district.

"And I want to be very careful here, because I am about to do the thing that will appear in the report, and it is the only thing in this review that I am afraid of. I am ruling that this document is not evidence of a displaced preparation, and **I am right**, and the reason I am right is the same reason your sheet was in a drawer for six years.

"It was not lodged, so it could not be responded to, so it could not be decided about, so it could not be the turn that contradicted a claim, because a turn that nobody was exposed to is not a turn anybody was exposed away from.

"Which means that on the twenty-first, in the Ferrygate low side, this Council's method did not take your rota. And I am going to say the second half of that, and it is the half that will be quoted. It did not take it, and it did not leave it, and it never went near it, and **it was not in the record at all**, and the reason it was not in the record at all is that nobody was ever going to be able to tell the difference between a preparation that a claim closed and a preparation that a claim never touched.

"That is not a hole in the method, Mrs Cray. That is the method. It is doing the only thing it knows how to do, which is to see what has been put in front of it."

---

"It is a hell of a thing to be told on a Sunday," said Sabin Orle, "and I have been trying to find a way to write it that does not make me sound pleased, and I have failed, and I would like the minutes to record that I failed."

"Will you be reporting it?" said Nell Cray. She said it quietly and she said it with her hands flat on the table, which she had never had to do in a district meeting in nineteen years.

"Both halves," said the assessor. "The finding is that the effect of a certified or authorised claim on the preparations of the districts affected **cannot be demonstrated from the record**, and the second half of the terms of reference is that if it cannot, we report that it cannot. Clerk. Enter it now, in those words, with the date, and add that the finding is made on the first day of district evidence, and that it is made on the evidence of a document admitted by the assessor and produced by the district without compulsion, and that the district's clerk volunteered the second half of it and the assessor would not have found it.

"Mrs Cray, that is the finding of this review. It was on your list of six questions and I got it out of a district clerk's own mouth on the first day, and it is a finding about records, and I want to be extremely clear, because I know exactly what it will be read as: **it is a finding about records and not about the twenty-first.** It is not a finding that nothing happened to Ferrygate on the twenty-first. Nothing in what I have said bears on what happened to Ferrygate on the twenty-first. That is the difficulty and that is the whole of it."

"Then I would like to say one thing," said Nell Cray, "and I have thought about whether I am entitled to."

"You are the clerk of the district and you are entitled to."

"I am not going to ask for a finding," said Nell Cray. "I have spent seventeen days waiting to ask somebody to say that something happened to two old people on the Quay Steps and I am not going to ask for it in a room, because it would not be a finding, it would be a minute, and because you are right that it is not in the record and it never was and it is not going to be in it now that you have said so out loud.

"I am going to ask you for a form."

---

The assessor put her pen down.

"There is no form."

"There is always a form, madam, and you know it better than I do, because you have spent eleven years filling in boxes and so have I, and I have been in this job nineteen years and I have put a hundred and forty things into a hundred and forty boxes and about a hundred and thirty of them came back with a stamp on them saying that the box was for something else."

"Mrs Cray—"

"No. Let me say it properly, because you have been decent to me all morning and I am going to ask for something and I am not going to do it as a favour." She turned round on the table and looked at her own council rather than at the assessor, which is a thing she has never once done in nineteen years. "I am asking this assembly to ask the Council for a place in its records for a district's own preparations, whether or not anybody forecasts them and whether or not anybody in a different building wants to know.

"Not a return. Not a forecast, not a claim, not a notice, not a list of flood precautions. **A place.** Somewhere in this building's records where a piece of paper that a district keeps about itself can be put, with a date on it, so that it exists in more than one building, and so that if one day a claim comes down with an hour on it, somebody in a room can go and look at a piece of paper that says *this is what this district had ready*, and be able to say whether the district used it or did not.

"Mr Vale said on the Saturday, in front of nine people, that this office has never asked a reader where a reading came from. I have read the minutes of that and I have not stopped thinking about it, and I have got a week of it and I have got the rest of my life, and I want to say one thing to the room and then I want to sit down.

"**Nobody has ever asked this district what it keeps ready.** Not in nineteen years. Not once. And it is not a complaint, and I have said that twice, and I am not going to say it a third time, and I would be obliged if the minute did not have it written down three times because the third one starts to sound like a woman who wants something."

The chandler said, "Seconded."

"Seconded," said the assessor, to the room, and it was carried without a division, and it is the only thing Nell Cray has ever got through a district council in nineteen years and she is going to be asked about it for the rest of her life and she will say *it was nine minutes and nobody argued.*

"Can you give me the form?"

"There is no form."

"Then I will take the paper," said Nell Cray, "and I will write to the technical cell and I will ask them what the form is, and they will tell me, and I will fill it in, and I will file it, and in four years somebody will be able to say that I asked. And I want it minuted that you told me there is no form, because in four years I am going to want to be able to say that I was told there is no form by the assessor of the review, and that is worth more to me than the form."

The assessor looked at her for a moment.

"Minuted," she said.

---

Afterwards, on the Quay Steps, in about nine inches of a Saturday's earlier mud and a great deal of smell from a drain Nell Cray had chased on the Monday and not yet caught, they stood about four feet apart and looked at a door.

"Will you come in?" said Nell Cray. "There is nothing to see. There is a room and a flag floor and a barometer with no glass in it, which has never had any glass in it, and a boat hook against the wall of my office at home which came out of about eight inches of water and which nobody will explain to me, and there is a smell from that drain which I am going to have chased by the end of the week."

"No," said Marek Vale.

"No," said Nell Cray. "Then I will tell you what the room looks like and you can decide about it later, which is the only thing anybody in this basin can be got to do.

"There is a front room with a stone floor and three rooms and a yard at the back with a metre step down into it, and the boat hook's dust is still on the yard wall where it leaned, and the two of them are alive and at home and one of them is seventy-eight at Michaelmas and the other is eighty-two and says seventy-seven, and I have been down that lane in boots every winter for six years, and Mr Vale, they argue about the composition of tea."

He did not say anything. There was not very much to say to it and he had about nine inches of a look left in him and the look was not for this.

"They are not on the page," said Nell Cray. "That is what I have understood, and I have understood it on my own, and I am not asking you to explain it to me, and I am not asking you to help me get them on the page, because I have been in this job nineteen years and I know exactly what a page is for and I know exactly what it costs, and if I put two old people on a page they stop being two old people and start being a **cost**, and there is no column in this basin for a cost, and if there were one it would be a column with no names in it, and I have had four copies of a sheet made and I am not making a fifth and I am not making a *claim* out of two names and a boat hook.

"What I want is a box. That is all. A box where a piece of paper can go and be a copy instead of an original, and there is no name in that and nobody has to be brave about it and nobody has to lose anything.

"And I would like to say the last thing, and then I have to go and catch a drain, and it is going to be the only thing I say in this room that anybody repeats.

"I did everything right."

"Yes," said Marek Vale.

"I did **everything** right," said Nell Cray. "Six years, in boots, on a Thursday, in the wet. That is what I did, and the reason the room is still standing in front of me is that I did it perfectly, and the thing that happened to it happened because it was perfect, and I have had seventeen days and I have got to about two o'clock in the morning most nights with that, and I am not asking anybody to fix it, and I would be very much obliged if nobody in this basin ever came up with a way to fix it, because I know what the way to fix it looks like and it looks like a box, and I want a box."

Then she went up the steps and got her key ring off the hook and unlocked the district office and let herself in and shut the door behind her, and Marek Vale stood at the bottom of the Quay Steps in the mud with a boat hook he had been told about three times and did not understand, and looked at a district that had kept a two-line sheet in a book for six years and had been, for exactly that reason, the only district in this basin that had never once been able to be shown what it had lost.

---

**Clerk's finding, entered in the minutes of the thirty-eighth day, item one, and the finding stands:**

*That the effect of a certified or authorised claim on the preparations of the districts affected cannot be demonstrated from the record; and that this was established on the first day of district evidence, from a document produced by the district and not compelled, and that the reason is that the district's shelter rota was not published and not lodged, and was therefore not before the method; and that a preparation not lodged cannot be shown to have been displaced and cannot be shown not to have been displaced, and that this is the ordinary operation of the method and not a defect in it.*

*And that the Ferrygate district has asked, through its clerk, for a place in the records of this Council for a district's own preparations, whether or not anybody forecasts them; and the assessor has advised that there is no such form; and the request stands on the record.*

*And that the assessor has further advised that nothing in this finding bears upon what occurred in the Ferrygate low side on the twenty-first, and that no finding as to that is available to this review or to any other body in this basin on the material before it.*

Nell Cray read all three of those paragraphs out of the minute book in her own office at about half past five that evening, to an empty room, because that is what a clerk does with a thing she has got, and then she shut the book and went and chased the drain.
