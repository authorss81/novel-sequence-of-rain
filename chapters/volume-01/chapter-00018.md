# Chapter 18 — Twelve Years Back

He went up the ladder at eight on the Saturday morning with the spare plate's box in one hand and no reason at all in the other, which is a luxury he had not had since he was nineteen, and he had been on the low ground five days without sleeping and had noticed on the Thursday that he could no longer keep two things in his head at once unless one of them was glass.

The document was in the second drawer of his bench on the ridge. He had started it on the Wednesday. It had four hundred and thirty words in it and every one of them was procedure, and the three sentences that were not procedure were in a different ink because he had gone back to them twice and had left them alone again, and he had got as far as the top of the third one and had understood that he was about to write the sentence *and my own reading of the order is not confirmed by any instrument in the Office*, which is true, and which is also an admission that a reader on a stone floor spoke six movements out loud to four hundred people on the strength of a piece of glass he had cut out of their own unpaid notice.

There was a better version of that sentence. He had not found it yet.

So he was on Tannery Walk at eight on the sixteenth day of the Bellweather autumn, in a tower with a brass surround around a temporary and a spare plate in a box on a shelf, and Gant Ollis let him in and went out to fix a length of the district's water main, and left him the tower.

---

The feed seat was under the copper tank and the tank sweated, and the plate in it was the one he had carried up a ladder in the dark at ten o'clock on the Friday night of the previous week, and it was warm.

That was the first thing: a sequence plate in a live seat should be cooler than the hand that goes near it, because the feed is a cold circuit. This one was warm. It had been running for eight days in a brick shaft in a cold season, and it was warm to within a degree of his own palm, and that meant the feed had been doing what a healthy feed does, which is holding a steady low gradient with the water moving, and a steady low gradient in a feed is the difference between a plate that shows a sequence and a plate that shows a smear.

He set the lamp up and read it.

He did it properly, which took two hours, and the proper way is the way he had done it a few hundred times in his life and would have taught a glass-tender's apprentice in a fortnight. Down through the whole run, once, to find what the instrument was. Then up through it again, movement by movement, naming each one out loud under his breath because a man who reads silently has no way of knowing later whether he read a thing or remembered reading it. Then the whole run again with the lamp cold, and then once more with the frame tilted, because a tilt changes the incidence of the film and some things only show you at an angle.

Friday night's drainage. All of Saturday, and the whole shape of the collision, which he had read off a plate in his coat on a stone floor, and which was here in a form that would survive him: Gather alone; Break standing in for an unfinished Weight; Route doubled, short hook under long one; Release at about five to seven, eleven minutes, complete; Witness out of order on top of the Break, at about ten past seven, which nobody in the world had published and which had been sitting in a locked room in a poor ward's tower for a week.

The basin emptying itself at the low end for two days and stopping on the Sunday.

And the Release was clean.

Not merely present. Clean, in the specific sense he had spent eleven years learning: no smear, no hard edge, no crescent, no thinning. The moisture came up on the film at an angle and it kept coming at the same angle and it did not shallow and it did not come back late, and at the end of it the traces closed over into the ordinary business of an ordinary night, the way they close over when a plate has nothing to hide.

The feed did not drop out.

He took the lamp down and sat on the second landing in a cold brick shaft for a while and let that be a fact.

The March dropout in this same feed, which he had logged correctly on a tag in his own hand in the second drawer from the top, dropped hard, in one step, and left a grey crescent. It is on that feed's history because he filed the cause. It is in the register because he is a man who files the cause.

And in eight days of running, this feed, in the biggest collision any of them had had in nine years, had produced no fault of any kind at all.

Which meant — and he had to have it, he had to have the sentence, because he was going to be asked for it by somebody eventually and he would rather have it from himself — which meant that there was nothing wrong with the ninth ward tower.

Which meant that the two and a half hours of nothing in the moisture trace of the second turn was not the plate, and not the wet cell, and not the lamp, and not the lamp twice, and not a fault in a feed in a documented dropout history.

The only remaining explanation was the one he had been unable to put in a sentence since the Friday morning of the previous week, and it required no instrument at all, and it required no reader, and it had been available to any person in that market hall on the Thursday who had cared to ask a man with a shovel where he had been.

---

He went back over the plate again at about eleven, and this time he did what he had been trying not to do, which was to read it further back than the feed had been keeping.

That is a thing a plate does. It is not a thing anybody teaches. A sequence plate holds about two days of legible trace and then the registration under the fine trace goes coarse and cold, because the glass has been written into at a depth that does not get wiped back when the working surface is cleaned, and if you light it obliquely and let your eyes go soft you can see a great deal further back than anybody has any business seeing.

He had done it twice in his life, both times by accident, both times on cracked glass where the strain had left the record standing where the sound plate had thrown it away.

This was not cracked glass. This was the best plate he had ever fitted and it was clean.

He tilted the lamp, and he stopped breathing for a while without noticing, and he went back four days, and five, and then six, and the six-day-old registration came up out of the cold out of the plate in a slow bloom of film that was not moisture and was not heat and was the same stuff as everything else on it.

On the sixth night of the plate's run there is a Witness.

It was a complete movement. Not a fragment, not the edge of one, not a smear where a claim had been entered at a busy hour and had got onto the film out of order. It was a whole Witness, with its gather and its weight and its break and its route and its release all present and instrumented and in the ordinary order, and the sequence glass had rendered it in exactly the way it renders a trace, which is to say as though a plate had been sitting in a seat at the moment it was made.

And in the plate's own margin, against the Witness line, in the tower's own stamped column, there was a date.

Marek put his hand on the brick to make sure the tower was solid, and then he read the date again, and then he read it a third time, because the first two times he had read it as a thing he expected to see.

Twelve years back. To the day, in the stamp. A watch number beside it, in the same die.

The date was old enough to have browned the paper on a chart margin and there was no paper anywhere near it. It was a registration in glass, in a feed seat, in a shaft on Tannery Walk, and it was twelve years old, and it was complete, and it had arrived in the plate from below rather than out of the air.

Above it, in the legible run, the rest of that night's registration was the ordinary business of a ward with a dry spell in it: a little water, a little pressure, nothing. The claim had not been made in that night. It had been made twelve years before that, in the same seat, in the same shaft, and whatever was left of it had come up through the glass underneath five days of ordinary weather and made itself visible because a man had gone back six days instead of stopping at two.

He could not account for it.

That was the part he had to write down, if he was going to write down anything, and he was not going to write down anything, and so he said it to himself instead, standing in a cold shaft with a lamp in his hand:

*There is no instrument in the maintenance manual in the office at the ridge that will tell me how a working plate holds a twelve-year-old registration, and I have read the manual, and it does not say, and there is no index in that building I am permitted to read that would tell me, and the only two people alive who have ever asked that question are both in offices I cannot get into without a reason, and I do not have a reason, and I am not going to manufacture one out of a piece of glass I have no right to remove.*

He put his hand on the feed seat's frame, where the plate was sitting, warm from eight days of running, correctly installed, in its correct place, in a public building with a door and a keeper and a weather book and a glass book that had nothing in it since March.

He did not take the plate.

He did not copy the trace. There was a rule across it and a folding rule in his pocket and forty minutes' work in it, and the copy would have been exact, and the copy would have been in his coat by one o'clock.

He did not write it in the register. There was no register on that level, because there is no register in a ward tower — the register is on the ridge, in a locked drawer, in a room he walked past every working day of his life, and a line in it would have had to carry a plate number, and the plate number was on the shelf in its box, and a spare plate carries the date he cut it and nothing else. But there was the tower's glass book in the keeper's hut, which had weather in it and a weather column with a ruled space a half-inch deep for a note, and a date, and his own initials, would have taken eleven seconds.

Three things and he did none of them, and each of them had cost something different, and he knew exactly what each one had cost, which was the thing that was hardest to put down anywhere at all.

The plate went back against the feed and the feed took it and the six movements came up the filament one to the other, Gather, Weight, Break, Route, Release, Witness, in the order that six movements arrive in when nothing is wrong, which is the order that all the books in that building say they arrive in, and which is the order they had *not* arrived in for a week.

---

At six he came down the ladder and put the spare plate's box back on its shelf and shut the door on the tank and started down.

The kettle was going on the second landing. Gant Ollis had come back in from the water main and had made tea, and had made it without being asked, and had put a cup on the shelf by the hatch so that a man going down a ladder would not have to ask.

"Thank you, Gant."

"Six o'clock is a bad hour to come off a ladder," said the keeper. "You'll be back up here in a fortnight and I'll not be surprised."

He walked down Tannery Walk at about ten past six with the tube in his coat and the light going and the tram not due for twenty minutes, and he got as far as the corner of Sump Lane and stopped, and stood there, and thought, quite clearly and without any argument at all: *go back up.*

There was a plate in that tower with a twelve-year-old Witness in it and a date in the margin and a keeper in a hut who kept everything and had told him, without being asked, that he had nine years of a better mechanic's arithmetic in the book upstairs.

There was a rule in his pocket. It was nine inches long. He could be back up that ladder, across that room, and onto that plate in four minutes, and he could have the whole of it copied by seven, and it would be in a coat pocket in a cardboard tube by half past seven, and there is no arrangement of custody anywhere in the world that is simpler than a fact in a man's coat.

He thought about how long a plate keeps a registration, and about whether the six-day bloom would still be there after a week of handling, and about whether the lamp on a wet night would show it to a man who was not looking for it.

Then he thought about a chart in a frame on a north wall on which a strip had been taken off the top with a good blade and a steady hand, and about the fact that the person who had done it had not destroyed the sheet, had only removed the part that made it checkable, and had left the signature sitting in the middle of the gap where anybody would look at it and conclude that there was something to find.

He did not know who that was. He had a wife who knew a lot of people. There were probably a hundred people in this city who could have told him, in a room, in an afternoon, and he was not going to spend the plate to go and ask one of them.

So he stood at the corner of Sump Lane for about four minutes, in the last of the light, with a twelve-year-old date in his head and three blocks of pencil in the left cuff of a work coat, and then he walked down to the tram.

It was, he reflected on the steps of the car, the third time in nine days that he had decided not to do the thing that would have let him, and the first time that he had watched himself decide it on purpose.
