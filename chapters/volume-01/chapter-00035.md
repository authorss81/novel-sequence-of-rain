# Chapter 35 — A Copy Is What a Copy Clerk Keeps

Tovan Iles had been told on the Friday afternoon that the thirtieth was going to take a full day's entry and that she was to do it herself, and on the Tuesday morning of the third day of the month she came in at seven, took out the day book for the thirtieth, squared it against the edge of the bench with the flat of her hand, and began.

She was good at this. That is the first thing about her and it is the reason the rest of it happened: she wrote in one hand without lifting the pen, and her figures sat where a figure sits when somebody has been taught, and she could put a day's weather and a day's damage and a day's claims on one sheet in about forty minutes and make it read like a document rather than a list.

The thirtieth had: the body, the sustained western draw, the peak from two to five, four point one inches on the ridge, the headwall at about half past three, the south-bank road at about five, six ground floors in the ninth ward at about ten to four with no casualties, the first pump house drowned, and one claim.

The claim was the hard part of the sheet, and she wrote it exactly as it stood, because she had written it and she had read it back at the time and it was correct.

*Claim entered, 29th, conditional. Kestrel corridor passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth. Sponsor M. Vale, reader. Entered under the Director's authorization of the 27th. Not certified. Condition accepted and triggered, thirtieth, 23:11, Kestrel Line at the yards' mark. Claimed effect held to expiry. Listed alternative not triggered.*

*Yards: no damage. One mooring-hand injury at 11:11 p.m., right wrist, not broken, cause recorded on the yard's own board and not disputed by the yard. Cause is the claimed turn's second effect and is not within the claim's stated reach.*

She read that last line back twice and left it exactly as it was, and it is in the record, and it is the only line in the Office's whole day sheet for the thirtieth that refers to a cost, and it refers to a wrist, and the wrist is the yard's own account and the yard published it themselves.

She wrote the damage items and she wrote the classification, and at half past eight the item she was reaching for was the headwall, and she stopped with the pen about an inch above the paper.

---

Malle came down at about nine and put a folder on the bench and did not sit down.

"Two minutes, and then I want your opinion on a sentence, and then I want it entered by eleven."

He opened the folder. The day sheet for the thirtieth was on top of it and the *return* that had gone down to the districts on the Sunday was under that, and a third sheet was under that, and it was a short one, headed in a level hand in violet.

"Read that."

---

It was about eleven lines and it was drafted the way everything in the Office was drafted, which is to say that a person who did not like it would not have been able to point at a word of it.

*RECLASSIFICATION. Item (a), Lower Sill headwall, freight apron, thirtieth day. The structure was a Bellweather-era concrete headwall, shored in the season ending nine years ago and again six years ago, with a gravel loss at the west cheek above the water line reported to this office in the spring and carried on a deferred maintenance schedule for two funding years without execution. The failure of the thirtieth is a maintenance failure. No weather event on the thirtieth bears on the condition of that structure. The water office's deferred schedule is amended to record the item as overdue on the thirtieth day and not as a casualty.*

"Records and Public Notice countersign it in their own day book this morning, at ten past nine, and the violet sheet goes out with the district returns," said Malle. "I have brought it down so that you do not have to be surprised by it and so that I can tell you the reasoning before I ask you to enter it in a particular way."

"It is a maintenance failure," said Tovan Iles.

"It is."

"The gravel at the west cheek has been on their list since the spring."

"It has."

"Then it *is* a maintenance failure, and every word of that is correct, and it was correct in the Sunday's return, and the Sunday's return said *classified maintenance, deferred* and the reason it said it in those words is that somebody in that building put it there." She looked at the third sheet. "This is the same sentence with the reasons removed."

"Yes," said Corrin Malle, "and I want you to understand that I have not come up here to be argued with, and I am not going to be, and I have been wrong in this building before and been right about it afterwards, and I am going to tell you the whole of it in about ninety seconds and then I am going to ask you to enter it and you are going to have a problem."

---

"You know what is on this watch," he said.

"The claim's on it."

"The claim is on it and it held. It held to the hour and the yard put it on their own board and the *Petrel* was in the lane and the yard is intact and the only damage in this basin in three weeks is a mooring hand's wrist, and I want you to write that down somewhere in your head as the best night this office has had in nine years, because I am about to say something that will sound like I do not agree with you about it."

"Deputy."

"Every part of the twenty-ninth is a spend that happened on my watch, and a spend that happens on my watch is a spend with my name under it, and there is a category in this building called *branch displacement* and it is the only category in it that I cannot explain, and I have been looking at the record of it for four days and there is nothing in it." He put both hands on the bench. "I could not explain it if I were asked in a room. I have tried it out four ways and every one of them comes to the same place, which is that I would have to tell a Council that a corridor held and that something went, and that I have got the corridor and I have not got the something, and that the something is not a thing anybody can produce.

"And a Deputy who cannot explain a thing is entitled to call it the thing whose name he does not have to explain. That is not a sentence I invented. It is in the Charter. Section nine. It has been in the Charter since before I came in and it is the reason this building has survived nine of these. If you cannot account for an effect, you do not leave the effect sitting on the ledger unattributed. You classify it to the nearest true description, and the nearest true description of a wall that has been gravel at the cheek since the spring and has been on a deferred schedule for two funding years is a wall that was overdue."

"That is a true statement about the culvert," said Tovan Iles.

"It is a true statement about the culvert," said Malle, "and it is not a true statement about the thirtieth, and those two things have been the same sentence since the day this building was founded, and I have never before had to say them both in a room, and I am saying them both to you because you are the only person in this building who writes things down for a living and I would like you to know that I know the difference between them."

---

She sat quite still for a moment.

"And if I write it," she said, "then a thing that has happened in this basin on the thirtieth will be in the record of the thirtieth as a thing that was already overdue, and a man in a year will not be able to tell that anything was lost on the thirtieth at all."

"Yes."

"Even though something was."

"Yes," said Corrin Malle, "and I am not going to insult you by pretending there is a box on that form for the second sentence, because there is not, and if there were, I would have used it, and I have looked. The form has items for damage. The form has an item for casualties. The form has an item for claims, and the claim is in it and it is in it correctly and it is *true*, and it will be in the record for ever with the Director's authorization on it and the yard's mark on it, and nobody is going to take any of that away from anybody, and the method works, and the Council will be told again in the spring that the method works, and no district in this basin is any worse off as a matter of public record, and *that last clause is the one that has taken everything out of me twice, Tovan, and please do not make me ask a person to hear it a second time.*"

She did not answer that, and he did not wait, and she noticed, and was glad of, and has thought about since.

---

The one conversation with Records and Public Notice happened at about half past nine on the same morning, in the corridor outside the counter, and it lasted ninety seconds.

Tovan had a question and it was a clerk's question and she asked it because there was a line on a yard board in Kestrel Yards in a night watchwoman's careful hand with the word *BIND* on it and a captain's chalk about a wrist underneath it, and because the yard had published both, and because a thing that has been published in public is, in the view of nine out of ten people in this basin including her, a record.

"Is a yard board a record, Mr Venn?"

Lior Venn was about forty-five and had a face like a closed ledger and answered without any delay at all, because he had been asked some version of this for eleven years.

"It is a record of what a yard chose to write. It is not a reading, it is not lodged, it is not instrumented, and it is not compellable." He did not soften it. "An observation that nobody was obliged to act upon is not a promise, and a promise is the only thing in this building a person can be answerable to. Four years ago a captain published an hour off a copper box off a wreck-surveyor, and it was right, and when the water came up a man's ground floor against it we could not ask her one question, because she owed us nothing and had promised nothing and had been paid by nobody. That is not a gap in the law. I have read the gap in the law and it is not an accident, and I would write it the same way again, and I have written it the same way six times this month."

"Have you ever lifted one?"

"No," said Lior Venn. "And I have thought about it, and there is a version of my job in which I lift things, and in that version I am not doing records, I am doing something else, and there are people in this city who would be much better served by that other thing and it is not this." He put his pen down. "I have never lifted one. Ask me again in a year."

And that was the whole of it, and Tovan Iles went back to the bench with it and sat down, and it stayed with her for a long time, and this is the shape of it in her own account, written out years later in a hand that had got worse: *a rule is not a lie. It is never going to be a lie. I want that written down. I did not agree with it and it is not a lie, and the protection of a record is a classification, and a classification does not have to be a lie in order to be one.*

---

She entered the reclassification at eleven.

The day book for the thirtieth has a column for classification, and a change of classification is a Records and Public Notice act under the Charter and not a field act, and it is entered in the Records day book by the Deputy for Records and Public Notice. Venn had countersigned the violet sheet in that book at ten minutes past nine that morning and the sheet was in Records' file two floors away, and the violet sheet was in Records' file two floors away in a building he has not been in all day.

A field day is written up on this bench. That is the trade. The field day book takes the weather and the damage and the claims, and the classification column in it is filled in by Records, and it is filled in by Records because a change of classification is not a field act, and nobody on this bench has ever had a reason to think about why.

Tovan Iles filled in the classification column and signed the entering clerk's line.

Then she made the copy.

---

A copy clerk makes a copy. That is the whole of the trade and it is why they are taught it first and it is done in one hand without lifting the pen and it is done on the district's own paper with the date and the initials against it and it is filed in the clerk's own box, because the original goes up and the copy stays, and a district that has not got a copy has not got a record.

Nobody had ever taught her to make a copy of a day sheet from *before* a reclassification, because that is not the job.

She made one anyway. She made it on a clean sheet out of the pad on the second bench, in her own hand, with the date and the classification lines and the day's items and the claim, exactly as they stood at half past eight that morning, and she put her initials against it and the time, and she put it in her box.

It is a true copy of a record that existed. It is not a stolen document. It is not evidence of anything at all — it has no chain behind it, it names no district and no person, it is a copy clerk's private copy of a day's weather, and it is worth precisely nothing in any room in this basin, and if she produced it in the Council's technical cell on the thirtieth of the month a Deputy would hold it up and say *that is an unattached copy of a record of weather, miss, and the record it copies is a record of a day* and put it in a tray, and it would be stamped in about forty seconds.

She wrote it out in eleven minutes and she did not shake once, and then she went back to the bench and finished the day's entry, and the day entry is correct in every particular and it is the document that went out, and it is the document that the Council's report on the method will be built on in the spring.

---

She was at the copy desk by twenty past two that afternoon.

There is a copy desk in the west end of the records room on the ground floor of the Weatherglass and it is a good job. Four desks, two clerks, a rule about everything, and about nine hundred sheets a day. It is below the ward ledger bench and it is below the glass benches and it is not a punishment anybody gloats about, because nobody gloats about anything, because there is a form.

The form is a *change of post, on grounds of record procedure*, and the ground is one line long, and it is true.

*A reclassification of a day entry under the Charter of Public Notice is a Records and Public Notice act, entered in the Records day book by the Deputy for Records and Public Notice. The reclassification of the thirtieth day's item (a) was countersigned in the Records day book at 09:10 on the third day. It was additionally entered in the field day book at the ward ledger bench at 11:00 the same day and signed at the entering clerk's line, which is not the entering clerk's line. Sequence noted by the Deputy for Field Forecasts.*

"You are quoting his time at," said Tovan Iles. "Nine minutes past ten. I entered it at eleven, and he had countersigned it two hours before, and both of those are correct, and neither of them is the fault, because I did not put a Records act in the Records book. I was not in the Records room and he was not on this bench and I had the field day in front of me and a folder with a violet square on it, and I wrote down what I had been given, in the column on my own bench, the way I have written down everything since I was twenty-two."

"I know that," said Corrin Malle. "I have thought about that sentence since eleven o'clock this morning and it is a good sentence and it is the sentence of a clerk who has been good at a job for five years, and the ground on the form is still the ground on the form."

"No," said Tovan Iles. "I would like the sentence about the reader, though."

"The sentence about the reader is that the reader is a reader and that I have said so in a room in front of a Council and I will say it again, and that it has got him nothing, and that it is going to cost him a great deal, and that none of it is fair, and that I told him the price of it in an ante-room on the nineteenth and he did it anyway and I have not stopped respecting him for a second and I have been the man who took his name off a watch.

"You have put your name on an act that is not your office, on a reclassification of a weather event, in the month that a reader put his name on a thing he cannot account for. I have to have one of the two off this bench, and there is no version in which it is the reader, and I have thought about it, and it is not spite and it is not cowardice, and if you want the whole of it in writing I will put the whole of it in writing."

"Right," said Tovan Iles.

"The glass-tender's examination is in the spring," said Malle. "It will be held. It will be held by somebody else and you will not sit it, and that is the arrangement and I am not going to dress it up. You are on the copy desk from today, and you will be on the copy desk after that, and if you want to leave the Office you have a fortnight's notice and there is no condition on it and I would rather you stayed, and both of those are true at once and you may think about it on the walk home."

---

He went up the stairs at about ten past three and did not stop at the ward ledger bench, and the bench was Tovan Iles's for two more hours, and after that it was nobody's, because the bench was a bench and there was a bell on it and the bell is struck by the clerk on the bench and a clerk on a copy desk does not strike bells, and the ninth ward's head on the wall of the ward ledger room went on doing nothing at all for the same reason it had been doing nothing since the middle of the month.

Nobody thanked her. Not the Deputy, who had been right about four out of five things that morning, and not the countersignature on the form, and not the woman from the third bench who came down at four o'clock to give her a parcel she had left behind in October and who did not know what had happened, and not one person in that building, and there is not one line in any record anywhere in Veyra that says a twenty-seven-year-old copy clerk did the correct thing on the third day of the month and lost a glass-tender's post for it.

She copied nine hundred and six sheets that afternoon and took them to a woman at the second desk, and at about half past five the light went off the copy room and the west end of the records room went dark the way that end of the building goes dark, and she sat there for perhaps four minutes with her hands in her lap and did not shake, and thought about the sentence a woman in a technical cell had said on the nineteenth of the month, which was that a cost you can document is a cost somebody can be compensated for, and it had been said in a room with eighty people in it, and it had been true, and Tovan Iles had been the person who wrote the claim it was said in.

And the thing she had in her box was a copy.

It was in her box, in her own hand, dated and initialled, and it was a true copy of a day's record, and it was the only object in Veyra that said a corridor had been held and a wall had not been shored and the two facts were on the same morning, and it was worth nothing, and it could not be produced, and no court in this basin would compel it, and there was no category in the Charter of Public Notice under which a copy clerk's private copy of a day sheet was a document anybody could be shown.

It was a piece of paper in a box, and there had been a claim on that bench a fortnight ago with a bell on it, and she had written that one too, in one hand, without lifting the pen, and it had been entirely true, and nobody had thanked her for that one either.

It was also, as of four o'clock on the third day of the month, the only true record in the city in the hands of a twenty-seven-year-old woman sitting two rows back from the ward ledger bench with a hand that did not shake, and nobody knew, and there was no instrument in this basin that would ever tell anybody, and she was going to have to carry it, and she was aware, with total clarity, sitting in a dark room, that she was going to have to carry it for a very long time.
