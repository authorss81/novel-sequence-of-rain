# Chapter 41 — The Line About Herself

The assessor's note reached the ninth ward district office on the Monday morning of the thirty-ninth day at about ten, brought by Pet on a bicycle, and it was four lines long and it asked whether the district would make a warden available to the review, and Neris Kade read it standing up in front of her own board, with Sable Orin's card still pinned on it from the eighth of the month, and she said, out loud, to an empty room:

"They have asked for me in writing."

Which was not the first thing in the month that had made her say something out loud to an empty room, and she noticed herself doing it and did not stop, because she had been doing it for about three weeks.

---

The difficulty was not the review. The difficulty was the drawer.

It was a drawer in the back of her own desk in her own office with the district's records in it, and the page was in the drawer, and it had been in the drawer since the thirty-first, stamped and dated in her own hand with the district's stamp, and it had seven lines on it and the seventh was the one that carried everything.

The assessor had asked for the district's record of its preparations. That was all. The word on the note was *record*, and a record is a thing you hand over, and there are nine things in that drawer and one of them is a ruled page with seven lines on it and the seventh one says that the Office has no instrument that looks at a reach and therefore nothing that was lost on the reach can be said to have been lost by anybody.

And the sixth one has a bracket round a word in it whose meaning she had not decided, and she has still not decided.

There were two ways to do this and both of them were defensible and she went round both of them on foot for about three hours, which is a thing she does, and which is the only form of thinking she has ever had.

The first way: take it herself. A warden may produce a record in person. She could put the page in her coat and go down the hill and stand in front of a Council assessor and read seven lines out loud, and every word of them is true, and the stamping and the date and the district's own stamp are on it, and she is the district warden, and nobody could say a word.

And there is not one institution in this basin that would let a warden do that. A document produced by the officer who wrote it, in a room, off her own coat, is a statement. A document handed over through a request is a record. She knew that better than anybody alive because she had spent four months writing down what notices did not say, and the whole of the second sentence of the fifth line is that a district that cannot read its own notice cannot be argued with, and that is not the same as a district that is right.

The second way: send it. And that is a denial of a lawful request in substance, and it is the reason she could not decide for three hours, and it is the reason that when she finally sat down at about twenty past twelve she wrote out the decision in her own notebook before she made it, in about forty words, because she is a woman who has a page headed *what the notice did not say* and who has learned the hard way that a thing she did not write down did not happen.

*Send it. It is stamped and it is dated and it is true. A page I keep is a page nobody can be shown. A page I have handed over is a page somebody has read out and disagreed with in a room with a clerk in it. I would rather be disagreed with in a room than agreed with in a drawer, and that is the whole of the reason and it is a good enough reason to act on at half past twelve in the afternoon on the thirty-ninth day of a month in which nothing whatever is happening in the sky.*

She sent it at about half past two, with a covering note, and she stamped a copy of the copy in her own hand and dated it and put the copy in the drawer, which is the third document in this city to be duplicated on purpose by the person who made it and the first time anybody has done it on purpose rather than as a trade.

That is the whole of what she got for it. It is worth recording, because the thing that happened next is the thing that the chapter is for.

---

They read the seventh line out in a room with a clerk in it, and they read it kindly, and it was not evidence of anything, and the assessor said so politely, and Neris Kade was not offended, because she had known.

"The seventh line is this, and I am going to read it as written." Sabin Orle had a copy in front of her. "*The thirtieth's return does not say the reach levels came down, because there is no instrument in the Office that looks at a reach, and therefore nothing that was lost on the reach can be said to have been lost by anybody. The notice is correct. The district cannot be shown to have been right about the levels and cannot be shown to have been wrong. A window the city cannot see is not a window the city loses.*"

"Thank you, warden."

"It's not evidence of anything," said Neris Kade, before the assessor had got there.

"It is not," said Sabin Orle. "I am going to rule in about a minute that it is not, and I am going to do it in about ninety seconds, and I am going to do it politely, and I would like you to know before I start that I do not think you wrote it in order to be evidence. I have read the first six lines and I think you wrote it because you could not sleep, and I have read a great many documents in eleven years that were written by people who could not sleep and about a third of them turned out to be instruments and the rest of them turned out to be exactly what they said they were, which is a thing."

"It is the only object in this basin that joins a claim to a shed," said Neris Kade.

"It is," said the assessor. "And I want to say something about that sentence, because it is the best sentence anybody has said to me in this review and it came from a district warden on a Monday morning, and it is going in the minutes in those words, and then I am going to rule.

"It joins them. It does not carry them. A document that says two things are connected is not a document that connects them; it is a document that says a woman thought they were connected, and there is no instrument in this basin, or in any basin, that can take a woman's thought and turn it into a fact, and if there were, then every district in this city would be able to prove anything it liked about any other district by writing it down in a good hand with the district stamp on it.

"So the ruling is this. The page is admitted. It is filed, in the technical cell, as a district record, and it is not compellable, because it has no place, no time, no effect and no witness, and it is not a notice, and it is not a claim, and under Rule 6 of the Charter of Public Notice it never will be, and I have not been able to find a way round that in three days and I have looked hard, and the reason I have not been able to find a way round it is that you have not written a claim, and you could not have, and I am the person in this basin who is best placed to tell you that you could not have.

"Which brings me, and I am sorry, to the fact that you have written the only document in this city which connects a claim to a shed, and that nobody can do a single thing with it."

"Yes," said Neris Kade.

"Do you know that?"

"I knew it when I wrote it."

"Then say it out loud for the minute, because in about a year somebody in a corridor is going to ask you whether you knew, and I would like there to be a room in which you did."

"I knew it when I wrote it," said Neris Kade, "because on the thirty-first I stood on the top of a low flat with a lamp in my hand and I understood that the claim was a notice and the shed was a silence, and a notice and a silence are two ends of the same piece of string, and there was one object in Veyra that had both ends on it, and it was mine, and it was not compellable, and it was worth nothing, and I stamped it and put it in a drawer and I did it anyway."

"It was the correct decision."

"It was the correct decision and it is worth nothing," said Neris Kade, "and I would like the minute to say both halves, because the second half is the part that costs."

---

They asked her the ninth-hour question at about half past two in the afternoon and they asked it as a question of fact, and it was the same shape as the question that had been put to a clerk in Ferrygate two days before by a different woman.

"Warden. The ninth ward's own low-ground shelter rota. Nine names on the card, as I understand it."

"Yes."

"Where is it?"

"It is not written," said Neris Kade.

The assessor put her pen down. "I am sorry?"

"It is not written. It has not been written for about two years. There is a card in the district office with nine names on it and it is a card, and the nine names are nine people who can be got, and there is no sheet that says when they are got or by whom or for what, and I have been asked that question by people in this building and I have never been able to answer it, and I have never been able to answer it for a reason that I have known for about a year and a half and have not said out loud to anybody until now."

"Go on, warden."

"Last winter I acted on a number — a good number, off a wall, out of a drying room, in a public baths — and I moved a shelter rota on it. And afterwards the list was different. That is the whole of the mechanism and it took about a fortnight to work out and it is in my notebook, because I write everything down.

"The original sheet was copied out. That is what a rota is. Somebody, in this district, some years ago, copied out an original list of nine names on a card onto a sheet, and the ward ran the sheet, and the ward copied the sheet forward, and at some point in about four years the sheet and the card diverged and the sheet became the plan and the card became the thing the plan was copied from.

"And I asked. Of course I asked. I asked the woman who wrote the original out, in a room, with a kettle on, and I asked her whether the sheet in the book was hers, and she said yes, and I said, *do you remember writing it out,* and she said — " Neris Kade stopped, and it was not for effect, and it was about two seconds. "She said *no.*

"And I want the room to understand that this woman is not playing at anything. She is a grandmother in this district and she has four grandchildren and she is the best memory for faces in the ninth ward and she was asked a straight question and she gave a straight answer, and the answer was that she did not remember writing it out, and she has never once, in the two years since, pretended otherwise or blamed anybody or offered a theory.

"Which means the original is not findable. Not because anybody has taken it. Because a person wrote a list out once, some years ago, for a good reason, into a district book, and a card has been in a drawer ever since, and no one copied the card and nobody ever will, and that is all."

"Clerk," said the assessor, gently. "Enter that exactly, and enter the time, and enter the warden's own words for the answer and not a summary of them."

---

That was the moment of the chapter, and it was not dramatic, and there were eight people in the room.

Neris Kade heard herself say *she said no*, in a room, with a clerk writing it down in a room that was going to send it to nine seats, and about two seconds after she had said it she heard a different sentence, and the different sentence was not hers, and it belonged to a clerk in Ferrygate who had said it to a man on some steps two days before and had had it minuted:

**The thing that is true and correctly kept and cost nothing to obtain is worth nothing at all, and the mechanism is that it was perfect.**

Neris Kade, who had written seven lines about what the notice did not say and had acted on a stranger's number and closed a school and moved a schoolmistress's morning, and who had spent four months pitying clerks — pitying them, specifically, as the people who are on the wrong side of a thing, which is a thing she had said out loud in a chamber on the ninth of the month and had been rather pleased with —

stood in a room on the Monday afternoon of the thirty-ninth day and understood that she had become one.

She had understood that about a ferry two years ago and had written it in a notebook and had never once applied it to her own card.

She went home at about six and she did not write the line.

She sat at her own table in her own front room with a pen and a sheet of her own paper and she wrote the heading out of habit, in her own hand, at the top of a fresh ruled page:

*NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came.*

And then she put the pen down, and looked at it for about ten minutes, and she could not write it.

The truth is that she could not write it, and that she had known she could not since about the middle of the previous month, and that the reason she could not is that a list with names on it is a **promise**, and she had spent two years not making one because she could not remember having made the first one, and there is no version of a hand on nine names in a drawer that is not a promise to nine people who are on the low ground of a poor ward and cannot read their own notice.

Nobody is waiting for it, and she does not know that, and that is also true.

---

The warden's council met at eight that night, in the assembly rooms, with fourteen people in it, and she asked them for one thing and got it in four minutes.

It was a small thing. It is worth describing exactly, because the size of it is the point.

"The clearing rota, the shelter rota, and the pump order. On one sheet. With the date on it. Kept here, in the district office, in the drawer, and sent up to the warden's council once a quarter whether anybody has asked for it or not, and whether or not anybody upstairs is forecasting anything at all, and whether or not there is a claim."

They carried it in four minutes. There was no division. Nobody argued. One man asked whether it cost anything and the warden said no and he said *right* and that was the entire discussion.

And then Neris Kade said the thing out loud that she had been carrying down the hill since ten o'clock that morning, in a room with fourteen people in it, in a voice that was not a warden's voice, and the room laughed.

"It is cheap," she said. "That is what is bothering me. It is the cheapest thing this council has ever passed, it is cheaper than the tea, and it has taken six years in this district not to have it, and I have just worked out that the reason it is cheap is the reason nobody did it in six years.

"If it is cheap, then nobody is losing anything by not doing it. And if nobody is losing anything by not doing it, then nobody will ever do it. That is not a council's fault. That is how the whole of this works. It works because the thing nobody is expected to do is cheap, and the price of a thing nobody is expected to do is nothing, and the price of nothing is nothing, and that is exactly why the district keeps a card in a drawer instead of a sheet in a book, and why I cannot write the eighth line of my own list, and why a woman in a low flat on the Quay Steps stood in nine inches of water on the twenty-first and why nobody in this building will ever be told her name."

The room laughed.

They laughed because she was being funny, and because she was right, and because a warden making a joke at eight o'clock in an assembly room is a thing that happens, and because the cheapest resolution of the ward's structural blindness had just been carried without a division and everybody in that room could see, all at once, that it had been a formality, and that the formality had cost the ward nothing at all, and that the ward was going to be exactly as blind in three months as it was tonight.

Neris Kade took the minute back and read it, and she did not soften it, and she put one line of her own at the bottom in her own hand, which is the way she does, and which nobody upstairs has ever asked her to stop doing and which nobody downstairs has ever asked her to start:

*It is cheap and it is carried and it does not matter and I am not going to stop proposing it.*

---

She walked back up the lane at about twenty past nine and stopped, because the ward's tower is on the corner of Tannery Walk and Sump Lane, and it is a brick shaft with a copper tank on it that has been repaired eleven times, and it is a hundred and thirty yards from her own front door, and she had walked past it twice a day for five years.

The frame above the second landing had a permanent brass surround in it, bedded properly, with four screws, and a good even edge for a pane to die on, and in the middle of the surround, in a frame that has been set true for four hundred years of weather, there was a half-size bubbled temporary with a flaw in it the size of a thumbnail.

It has been unreadable for a fortnight, which is a figure the district has stopped saying aloud and has started writing on its own forms, and the permanent pane is in a glassblower's shop at Kestrel Yards, quoted at the end of last year, ordered, paid for, on a district account that has never in two years covered a man and a crate sent forty miles, and never will.

There was nothing wrong with that tower. That was the part that had taken her five years. The feed under the tank was running on the best plate the Office had cut in a decade, and the plate was warm, and the man who kept the tower kept everything and had never once, in the five years she had been the district warden, asked her a question about anything.

There is a tower in the ninth ward that has a feed and cannot publish, and four hundred and forty people on the low ground, and a card with nine names on it in a drawer in the district office, and a warden who has just carried a quarterly return in four minutes and a page of seven lines that nobody can do anything with, and a public notice glass that has not been readable for a fortnight, and it was Neris Kade's professional opinion, standing on a dark street at twenty past nine on the thirty-ninth day of a month in which nothing whatever was happening in the sky, that of those five items the frame was the one that had cost the district the most and would go on costing it, and that there was no form for it, and that the reason there was no form for it was that a district is a thing you are, and not a thing you are given.
