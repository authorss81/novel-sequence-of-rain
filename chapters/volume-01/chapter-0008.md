# Chapter 8 — What the Notice Did Not Say

The notice was in Neris Kade's hand, because it was always in Neris Kade's hand now, and she had got so good at copying the Office's published wording that she could do it from memory, which was a thing she had told the district council exactly once and never again.

It said: *Bellweather, unsettled. Western draw. Low ground of the ninth ward expected to take standing water between the second and fourth watch of the second day. Take the usual precautions.*

She had read it out loud in the district office to eleven people at four o'clock on day eight, and one of them, Sergeant Ampow, a man who had been a warden for twenty-two years and had once closed a door on a flood call and never told anybody what was behind it, had said the thing she had been thinking since noon.

"It's true."

"I know it's true," said Neris.

"It's the truest notice I've ever read in this district." Ampow put his cap on the table. "And it doesn't tell me when to start the pumps. It doesn't tell me whether the school closes. It doesn't tell me what's happening to the ninety people on the ground floors who are not going to move because I've not got a cart for them and neither has anybody else. It says *between the second and fourth watch* like that's a place a man can send a crew to."

"That's a fair account of it."

"It's a forecast for the weather, Neris. It's not a forecast for us."

Neris Kade was thirty-seven and had been the district warden for Low Spindle and the eastern storm drains for five years, and in that time she had made herself into the sort of person who is not liked and is asked for anyway, which is a thing you can achieve on purpose but very few people do. She had a chalk mark on the door frame of the district office at four feet two, put there in the year before she took the job, and she had added a second mark since, in the same place, in a different colour, a foot lower than the first, which told her something she would rather not know and which the district council had voted to keep.

What she wanted, in the next four hours, was three things, and they were not negotiable with anybody: a warning that the lowest four blocks could act on, a name she could shout at if it was wrong, and the ward's school emptied before the second belt came in.

"Right," she said. "Then we go and find out about the glass."

---

The Ninth Ward tower is not a tall building. It is a brick shaft on the corner of Tannery Walk and Sump Lane with a copper tank on top that has been repaired eleven times, and its public notice pane is meant to be the one thing about it that anybody on the low ground can see from the end of their own street.

It has been unreadable for a fortnight.

The permanent pane cracked in the autumn, on the frame side, and the replacement was quoted at the end of the year and paid for and never collected, and it has been sitting in a glassblower's shop at Kestrel Yards ever since, and what went up on a Wednesday morning six weeks ago was a temporary — a bubbled, cheap, half-size thing, glazed in the ward's colour with a flaw in the middle of it the size of a thumbnail, which under any light at any distance reads as *clouded, disputed, do not rely on this*.

The ward had been living off handwritten copies since. Four women with good handwriting and no authority and a great deal of resentment, and a chalk board outside the assembly rooms, and a runner boy on a bicycle called Pet who could not ride a bicycle.

Neris had sent Pet up the ridge at eleven that morning with a note that said, in the plainest language she could manage, that the ninth ward could not read its own notice and had four hundred and forty people on the low ground and one pump house with a working pump in it.

He came back at half past three with a folded sheet and a face.

"The pane's out for replacement," said Pet. "They've got it written down. Replacement's delayed." He put the sheet on the table. "And they said — only if you ask, and I did ask, because you said ask — they said it's out because somebody cut a working plate out of it."

Neris read the sheet. It was a routing slip: *ninth ward tower, public pane, out of service pending replacement. Queries to records.* The word *deferred* had been used in a hand that had never been in a hurry.

"Cut it out of it," she said.

"Off the top corner," said Pet. "The man came and he told the crew himself. Two of them were there, and one of them wrote it on a docket. He says it's in the docket that he owes the tower the glasswork and that it's on his own name."

Neris looked at the slip for a while.

"Did he say why?"

"He said the tower crew didn't," said Pet, with the literal fairness of a boy who has been sent to fetch things his whole life. "I said the man ought to have thought of that, and the man at the desk said it was the wrong question, and I said what was the right question, and he said the right question was who was going to carry it."

"Right," said Neris Kade.

She thought about it for perhaps a minute and a half, standing at her own desk in her own ward office with the district's whole low ground outside the window.

She did not decide anything about the man. That was the honest part, and she was careful with herself about it, because she had spent five years learning that the fastest way to lose a district is to find a person to be angry at. A man who cuts a working plate out of a poor ward's notice in order to make himself a private instrument is not a villain. He is something she had not got a word for, and she had a whole district of people who needed the ward's water and heat and light and no glass at all, and she was not going to hand that district over to a mood.

So she wrote the name down. Not in the district book — in the back of her own notebook, on the page where she kept the things she had not yet decided about. *Vale, M. — glass shop. Pane out since the sixth. Told the crew himself. Reason: own instrument.* And under it, in the same small writing, she put the question she actually had, which was: *did he know.*

And she left that question with no answer under it, which was where she left most of them.

---

The back room above the Ninth Baths is a drying room, and it is warm, and it smells of hot linen and the particular damp of a place where the ventilation has been shut to keep the heat in for the price of the coal.

Sable Orin had a table, two chairs, a wall of hooks with weather glass on it, and eleven or twelve people in the room on a Friday afternoon, which was more than Neris had ever seen in there, and half of them were old women who had come for the warmth and one of whom was asleep.

"You've been reading my numbers," said Sable.

"Since November."

"November," said Sable, in the voice of a woman reading a figure in a report. "Right. Then you know what they are, and you know I am not paid by anybody, and you have never once written to me for one. Not one letter, in four months. You have never had to, because I put them out anyway, and that is the arrangement, and I would like it noticed that the arrangement works in one direction only."

"I know they're numbers with a time on them and no place, and no name, and no seal, and nothing on them that says who is responsible." Neris put her hat on the table. "I know they're usually right. I know you are not paid by anybody. And I know that the Office has been publishing *between the second and fourth watch* for a week while you have been putting an actual figure on a board where people can read it for nothing."

"I have."

"Why?"

"Because the shape of this is being withheld." Sable said it without heat. She was twenty-eight and had the particular stillness of a person who is very good at holding a position and knows it. "That's not an opinion, that's a fact about the Office's behaviour. On Tuesday they published a window with a four-hour margin in it, which is a margin that is really an instruction, and they published it to the districts and not to the yards, and the yards have their own instruments and the districts don't. And on Wednesday morning four of the ward heads in this basin asked me, in the hall, in front of each other, what the second belt was going to do to their ground floors, and not one of them could tell me what the Office knew and was not saying. So I put a number out. With an hour on it. That's all. That's not a conspiracy and it's not a rival office, it's a chalk mark on a wall."

"You put it out without asking anybody on the low ground whether they could use it."

"I put it out in a public room, in a building with a public bath in it, with a plate on the wall, where anyone can look at it and nobody is obliged to do anything." Sable's chin came up a quarter of an inch. "I am not the ward. I don't get to decide what a district does with a number. That is rather the whole point of having a number and not an instruction."

"You put it out on a Tuesday night. You knew the low rows were the ones that fill first, because you have said so in this room in November, in front of me, and it is in my notebook, and I wrote it down because I write everything down."

Sable Orin did not answer for a moment.

"And if I had waited for somebody to ask," she said, "then whoever needed it would have been whichever district had a seat in the hall, and the ones without seats would have had it in the morning. And you would have had it in the morning. And on the morning of the second belt is too late for a pump crew to be standing in a doorway when the water comes, Neris, and you know that better than I do, because you have done it twice."

"I have done it twice," Neris agreed.

"Which is why I put the number out before I knew it was you, and not after," said Sable, "and if you want to be angry with me about that then be angry with me about that, but do not stand there and pretend I sent it to you, because I did not, and I would not have been allowed to."

That was a good answer and it was also, Neris thought, entirely beside the point, and she had four hours.

"There's a school on Sump Lane," she said. "Two hundred and forty children, and forty of them walk up from the low blocks, and the undercroft floods. Ampow says empty it at dusk. The notice says nothing about schools."

"Then the notice is a forecast for the weather."

"The notice is a forecast for the weather," said Neris, "and I have a pump house."

Sable's hand went to the wall of hooks, and stopped, and came down again.

"Do you want the number," she said. "Because I will give it to you. I will give it to you now, tonight, whatever it is, and you can use it or not use it, and if you use it then I will say publicly where you got it, with the hour on it, so that it is on the record that it was out before you had it."

"That's the first sensible thing anybody has offered me this week," said Neris Kade. "Yes. Give me the number."

The number was *second belt, low blocks first, 01:20 to 04:40, and the undercroft on Sump Lane goes before the low blocks do.* It came on a card with nothing on it but the number and the hour, in a hand that was very good.

"Last winter," said Neris, turning the card over. "You gave me a number in a similar vein, and I moved a shelter rota on it."

"Yes."

"It was right. The water came exactly where you said." She put the card in her coat. "It changed things afterwards, though. We had run that rota for six years off a sheet of paper the ward copied out once and never looked at again. After that night the list was different, and when I asked, the woman who had written the original out said she could not remember doing it, and she was not playing at anything, she had plainly forgotten, and the original in my own book at home does not match the rota that ran. Nobody can find the sheet it was copied from. That is not anybody's fault and it is not a small thing to me, Sergeant, that I cannot find out."

"Yes," said Sable Orin, and stopped, and for the first time in twenty minutes did not have a sentence ready.

Neris put the card inside her coat, went out into the street, and walked to the school.

She closed it herself, at six o'clock, in person, in the rain, with a warden and a lamp and forty-one children waiting out front in wet coats and a great many mothers standing in it.

"Might as well not," said the schoolmistress, a woman of sixty called Pob who had kept that school for thirty years and had never once been wrong about a parent's mood. "Whatever it is you've come for, Neris Kade, it is going to cost somebody's morning."

"Yes," said Neris. "It will."

She did it on her own authority, as a warden, under a standing order that let her empty a building for safety without a forecast and without a claim and without a single signature. It cost the ward a morning of wages out of four hundred families' mouths, and there was no column anywhere for it, and no notice would ever mention it, and no one at the Office would ever know it had happened except by accident.

Which is the whole bargain, thought Neris Kade, standing in the wet in front of forty-one children. Nothing spent. That was the word for it. Nobody's possibility had closed. The only thing that had gone was a morning, and the morning had belonged to them and not to the weather, and the Office would never put it in a book because nothing had happened in the sky.

The glazier who came at first light to look at the market's storm pane told her, on her way down, that the Hearthward assembly chair had been up at the Weatherglass twice this week and had read the same claim on the ward board both times through a glass, and had gone away saying nothing either time, and had come back on Saturday morning with a clerk and a man in a wet coat. Neris wrote that down as well.

She got back to the district office at a quarter past eight with Sable Orin's card still in her coat, and she pinned the card to the board, and she wrote the pump order off it, which was the real work of the night: the low blocks first, the Sump Lane grate crew up onto the terrace and into the school undercroft before midnight, both crews on the terrace end of the drain run standing by at the second pump house, and the ward's own line to the assembly rooms open by ten. It took two hours and forty minutes and she would not remember writing it down. She did not ring the Office and tell them where she had got the shape of it. There was nobody in the number to ring, and nothing in it that named a district, and by the middle of the night four hundred and forty people in this district had moved when they moved because of a figure set in a drying room above a public bath.

That one had a witness in it somewhere. It was simply not her and not the Office, and she was not going to find out which, and she was going to do it anyway, tonight, on that number, because the alternative was forty children standing in an undercroft at two in the morning on the strength of a published word called *between*.

Then she took a fresh ruled page out of the drawer at the back of the desk, where she kept the district's own records because the Office's records had a two-year wait in them, and she wrote a heading at the top, and then she wrote a line, and then she wrote three more.

*NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came.*

*1. Fifth day, the unsettled bulletin. Does not say what unsettled means. There is no number in it at all.*
*2. Seventh day, the western draw. Does not say when to start the pumps. It gives a watch, and a watch is not a time.*
*3. Eighth day, the second belt. Does not say what to do with the ninety-odd people on the ground floors, for whom there is no cart between here and the assembly rooms. Does not say how long the water will be here. It says when it arrives, and nobody has ever yet been told how long.*
*4. It does not say why the ward's own glass cannot be read, and it does not say whose plate is in my coat.*

She read the four lines and did not write a fifth, and the fifth was there in the room with her, sitting in the chair where the rota had hung out its last winter, and she knew its shape, and she had a book at home with the original in it, and she was not going to put it on a public list at twenty to nine on the evening before a storm, because a public list is a thing you put your name to, and she had not yet worked out what her name would be doing there.

Four lines, then. She put the page in the drawer with the district's records and locked the drawer, and went to stand the pumps.
