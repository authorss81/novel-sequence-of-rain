# Chapter 12 — The Fifth Line

At eight in the morning on the tenth day, which was a Sunday and the only morning any of them was going to get, the district office on the corner of Sump Lane had a kettle, three people who had not been to bed, and eleven people who had gone to bed at nine and been woken at four by water.

That was the whole of the victory, and nobody in the room called it a victory, because a district that has come through a Bellweather without a death does not congratulate itself before breakfast; it counts.

"The fourth's holding," Ampow said, coming in with his coat open and his cap in his hand. "Nine inches where it was nine inches, and it's stopped, and the third pump has a cracked gland and the second pump house crew are bolting a new one out of a shed in Ward Six that has been sitting in a shed for two years doing nothing for anybody."

"Anybody in the low blocks got hurt?"

"Two sprains, one stove, and a man of about eighty who would not leave his ground floor and had to be got out of a chair, and he's in the assembly rooms and he's furious and he's alive." Ampow put the cap down. "That's the list. That's the whole list, and I have checked it twice, and I want it written down twice."

"Ott wrote it down twice," said Neris Kade.

Ott Belm was at the end of the long table with a wet notebook open in front of him and a second, drier notebook beside it, and he had been in the district's service for nine weeks and had spent the last night in a stone room writing down the words of a stranger, and he had the particular expression of a young man who has discovered that he is good at something and has not worked out yet whether it costs anything.

He put the first one down in front of her without being asked.

She had asked for it at about five in the morning, standing on the floor, and he had written nineteen names in a column and the hour against each and the crew each of them had gone out with, and at the bottom he had written the order, in Marek Vale's words, with Ampow's second version, which was the right one.

"Well?" said Ampow.

Neris read it. She read it twice, which was what she always did, because reading a thing once is a mood and reading a thing twice is a record.

"Put the times against the names as they stand," she said. "Ott, copy this one out. Same hand, no tidying, and if you can't read one of the entries, copy it as you can read it and put a bracket where you stopped. I do not want a clean document. I want a true one."

"That'll be a mess."

"It'll be evidence," said Neris Kade.

She put it in the drawer, and shut the drawer, and locked it, and then she stood at her own desk for a moment with her hands flat on the wood, because there were four block captains in the room and every one of them had the same question and none of them was going to ask it in front of the others.

They were going to ask it in about two minutes and she was going to have to answer it and there was not going to be an answer.

"Schools," said the captain of the first block. "They're open."

"They've been open since seven."

"They've been open since seven and they were shut all of Friday morning and Friday was a work day and Friday's wages came out of the same mouths as this morning's bread, and I'd like somebody to tell me who is paying for Friday."

"The ward is paying for Friday," said Neris.

"The ward is a word for a building," said the captain of the third block.

"Yes," said Neris Kade. "It is, and I am the one in it, and I shut that school on Friday morning at ten on my own authority with no notice and no claim, and I emptied it again on Friday evening at six o'clock and kept it shut all night, and it has cost this district a morning of wages, and a morning of wages is the cheapest thing the ward owns. Put it in the book. I will put it in the book. It is in the book already because Ott was awake when I wrote it down at four this morning, and it says *school closed on the warden's own authority, no notice, wages not recovered*, and there it is, and nobody has to raise it again."

The captain of the third block looked at the notebook for a while.

"And the week," he said. "The week we're having. Bellweather on the ninth, a school day on the seventh for the rain, and the market shut at Hearthward since the day before last, and there's another one coming, everybody's talking about a front coming back. How many of those are there going to be before the year turns?"

"Two," said Neris.

"You don't know that."

"No," she said. "That is my opinion and it is not a notice, and I will not put a number in this room that I cannot stand behind at a council meeting in daylight, because the last person who did that in this district is going to end up like the Fourth Ward's did."

They sat with that, because there is nothing a room can do with a person who refuses to be dramatic at them.

And then, from the back, in a voice that had not been used in this office for as long as Ampow had been a warden:

"The Office's bulletin is on the board."

They all looked.

Ott Belm stood with his hat in his hands. He had brought it in off the street an hour ago, cut off the assembly-room board, and it was drying and it had been read forty times, which you could tell because the fold had gone to fur and one corner had come away in somebody's hand.

"Then read it out," said Neris.

He read it out. He read all of it, from *BELLWEATHER COLLISION, NINTH DAY* to *the following day: settled, cool, light airs*, and he read it the way a clerk reads, and when he got to the end he put the sheet down on the table face up, and nobody said anything for a moment.

"Right," said the captain of the fourth block. "So the Office told us."

"Yes," said Neris.

"The Office told us on Friday that the front was coming and told us nothing else, and on Saturday morning it has put up a notice that says what actually happened." He folded his arms. "So where were we wrong? Because I want to know, warden, I have four hundred people behind me and I would like to be able to say the word wrong about something."

"You weren't wrong," said Neris Kade.

"Then the Office was right."

"Yes," she said.

She let that sit, because it was the true thing and the room needed it more than it needed anything else.

"Write that down as well," she said to Ott. "Nine hundred on Saturday morning. The Office's published hour was right. The strongest turn of the week came through at the hour they gave us. Say it in those words, because in a year somebody is going to try to say the warning failed, and it did not fail, and the reason it did not fail is that we did not use it. We used something else. I want the two things in the same book, one under the other, so that whoever reads this in a year cannot pick the one that suits them."

"Write it as the Office's hour was right?" said Ampow. "Warden, I have been stood in front of four hundred people all week wanting somebody to be at fault."

"I know you have," said Neris. "Write it."

Ott Belm wrote it. And when he had written it he looked at it, and then he said, quite quietly:

"Then the Office is the only thing in this that anybody can be shown to be wrong about."

The room went very quiet.

"Yes," said Neris Kade. "That's the fifth line. That's the fifth line and it took me four days and two of them are about this room and not about the Office."

---

She wrote it out that afternoon, on the ruled page, under the four lines that were already there.

The page was headed *NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came*, and it had four lines on it, and the first three had dates and the fourth had not, because the fourth was not about a notice. The fourth was about the ward's own glass and whose plate was in a maintenance man's coat, and it had been written on the Friday evening with a question under it that had never been given an answer.

She had spent four days not writing the fifth. Everybody in the building knew she was not writing it, in the way that people know which drawer a person is avoiding.

And here it was, and it was not that.

*5. The ninth ward's public glass has been unreadable for a fortnight. This means that nothing the district did on the eighth and ninth nights can be compared with anything the Office published. We cannot be shown to have been wrong, because there is no published figure of ours to be wrong against. We cannot be shown to have been right. A district that cannot read its own notice cannot be argued with, and this is not the same thing as a district that is right.*

She read it four times.

The one she had been avoiding was still in the chair where the rota hung out its last winter, and it was still the one she could not write, because it was about her and not about the Office, and because the original sheet could not be found, and because a public list is a thing you put your name to and she had not yet worked out what her name would be doing on a page about a sheet nobody could produce.

So she wrote a line about the ward's glass instead, which was the same wound seen from the outside, and she locked the drawer, and she noticed that she had got away with something, and she noticed with total clarity that this was what she had done.

Then she got the chair's rake of it out: not the page. Not the list. Something else, and she had thought about it for a day and a half, and it took her until about four in the afternoon to write it properly.

She wrote to the Kestrel Line.

Not to the Weather Office, who had already published, and not to the Council, who would not answer, and not to the district council, who would ask her what she wanted and then not be able to give it to her.

She wrote to a yard on the west side of the lake, to a captain she had never spoken to, and what she asked for was not a forecast.

*Warden Kade, ninth ward, to the Kestrel Line.*

*I am not asking you for a window and I am not asking you to take anybody's contract. I am asking for a turn.*

*On Saturday evening a man stood on our floor and told us the order the six movements arrive in, and he was right, and he had nothing behind him that anybody could check, and every pump order in this district is built on an hour, and an hour is the one thing that was not the problem. The problem was that we did not know which movement was going to hold the air and which one was going to let it go, and we could not know, and there is no instrument on your boat or on the ridge that will tell us. What your captain's box reads, by the look of it, is not order at all; it is what a passage does. That is a different thing and it is the thing I want.*

*Publish the turns. The western ones, over the low ground, with the hours on them, on whatever board you keep, and let a warden be as welcome to read it as a carrier. I will put it on this district's slate and I will not send it to anybody who has to pay for it.*

*If it is a stupid request, say so and I will not write again.*

She read it four times, which was what she always did, and put it in an envelope, and gave it to Pet, who could not ride a bicycle but could run six miles and had run further than that on Thursday.

The boy's name was Pet and he was sixteen and he was the fastest thing in the ninth ward, and on that afternoon he was given a piece of paper and a great deal of responsibility and went off up the hill towards the trams with the expression of a man who has been trusted with something at last.

It is the ordinaryest document produced in Veyra that week and it is the only one anybody acted on.
