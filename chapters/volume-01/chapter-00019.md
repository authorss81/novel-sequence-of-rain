# Chapter 19 — Hours on the Board

The joint request went up on the field office board at four o'clock on the ninth day and it was still on the board on the seventeenth, which surprised nobody who had spent a week watching what happens to a document when the weather that justified it goes away.

It did not go away.

*The western draw may be used either to hold the Kestrel corridor open for the return lane or to ease the exposed floodplain of the lower basin. These are the same air. They cannot both be had.*

A Bellweather had come and gone and the trade was still true, in the same way that a fact about money is true after a market closes. The yards' return lane ran on that draw. The lower basin's floodplain drained on it. Both districts had signed the request and neither had withdrawn it, and every day it hung there a Deputy with a basin to get under a roof got one day closer to having to answer it.

Marek read it standing up on the Sunday morning at half past nine, with the chart room to himself and the whole of the north wall sweating behind the frames, and he had four hundred and thirty words of procedure in a drawer downstairs that he had promised to a woman in a corridor on the ninth day and had been not-writing for eight days, and a term sheet in his coat that a captain had asked him for and that he had not started either, and he found that the one thing in the world he wanted to do on the seventeenth day of a wet autumn was go and stand in front of a board at Kestrel Yards and put numbers on it.

It was a completely unprofessional reason and he went anyway.

---

Nessa Tarrow had kept the yard boards for six years and had spent a week being told what to put on them, and the thing she had done on the Saturday — she told him this before he had got to the boards, standing in the doorway of the route office with a sheet in each hand — was to put the Office's window back up with a new one under it, and to write *both* at the top, and to leave both on all day with the hours against them.

"That's not an instruction," she said.

"No," Marek said.

"There's been no note."

"Good."

"I've never done it without a note."

"It's the correct procedure," said Marek Vale, "and you did it without a note, and the reason you did it without a note is that you have been putting two hours on a board since the Tuesday after a front and you are the only person in this yard who has ever thought of doing it."

Nessa Tarrow looked at him for a moment with an expression that would have taken a great deal out of him on a worse day.

"Captain's in the office," she said. "She wants to see you and she's not going to pretend she's been waiting."

---

The two sheets on the yard board that morning said this:

*Office window, corridor, return lane: passable from the first watch to the third. Sealed.*

*Kestrel Line, corridor, return lane: passable from the fourth watch of the previous day to the second. Published from the Line's own glass and the Line's own box, with the hour it was taken.*

They were not a small difference. They were an hour and a half.

"That is the same hour and a half as the Bellweather corridor window," Marek said.

"It is," said Tarin Senn.

"And it is not a coincidence, and it is not luck, and I want to know how you knew on the Tuesday."

"I didn't know anything on the Tuesday," said Tarin. "I have a copper box with a needle in it that I bought off a wreck-surveyor and that I hold in my hand because the bracket lies, and it says a pressure is stacking in the west draw. That is all it says. It doesn't say how much and it doesn't say when and it does not know the word *sequence* and if you asked it the order the six movements arrive in it would swing about a quarter of a degree and look pleased with itself."

"Then how—"

"Because I fly," said Tarin Senn, "and because a ship in the lane when the turn comes goes through, and a ship that waits for the window is in the yard at the turn, and I have been doing this for eleven years and I have been wrong about it perhaps four times and one of those four times cost me a month of overhead and I remember which month."

Marek put the two sheets on the table in front of her, side by side, the way a man lays out two plates he wants compared.

"Say the rest," he said. "The part where you are about to tell me that I can read the order and you can't."

Tarin looked at him for a moment.

"I cannot read the order of a sequence," she said, "and I have never pretended to and I would not know what to do with it if I could. Your whole trade is *in what order the movements arrive* and mine is *how many hours of air there are in a lane before it shuts*, and those are different questions, and the only reason anybody thinks they are the same question is that the Office has spent thirty years publishing the first one and calling it the second."

She tapped the Line's sheet.

"That is the whole of what I can do. Not the order. Not why. The hour. And I can only get the hour by being in the thing, which is expensive, and which is why it is worth publishing and why it cannot be done by a desk."

"Then let us make it a method," said Marek.

He said it too quickly, and heard himself, and sat back.

"Say that again slowly," said Tarin, "because I want to know whether you have had this thought for a week or for about four seconds."

---

It took them until four o'clock in the afternoon and one sortie and a great deal of argument, and what came out of it was four lines on the back of a slate, which were the Monday's four terms restated and turned round to face the Office instead of the Exchange, and they are these:

**1. The Line flies the lane on its own reading. Not the Office's.**
**2. The Office publishes the Line's reading, with the hour on it, and the Office's own figure beside it, and does not substitute one for the other.**
**3. If the two differ, the difference is published as a difference, in a column, with the dates.**
**4. A claim built on the Line's glass names the Line. It says so in the sponsor's own line and not in a footnote.**

"There is no footnote," said Tarin. "That is the entire reason for the fourth line."

"There is no footnote," Marek agreed. "I have watched the Exchange's window get entered into a room with a legal standing because four carriers paid against it and no name anywhere in it of the man who read the lane. Four lines that say *the Line* in them are the whole of what a small yard has against four businesses."

The sortie had been the reason the four lines existed.

They had gone out at eleven in the *Petrel* — not to prove anything, because neither of them had wanted to prove anything and they had both said so — but to take the hour one more time with two sets of eyes on it, and because Marek had asked to come, and Tarin had said yes in a way that was not warm and was not cold and was a professional judgement about a man who could read a plate in a hand-holding instrument at two thousand feet.

They climbed to the lane in the flat light at the top of the afternoon and sat in it, and Marek read the corridor off his plate in his hand, and Tarin read her box, and they were not looking at the same thing at all, and both of them came back with the same hour, which nobody on the ridge had believed was possible.

The turn came early while they were in it, at about twenty to two, and it came the way a western turn comes, which is that nothing happens for four minutes and then the lake changes colour. Tarin put the helm over and held it, and for about ninety seconds the *Petrel* was doing everything she had told Marek on the ground that a nine-year-old copper box with a needle in it could not do, and Marek put his hand flat on the rail and watched the whole of it and did not say one word, and afterward she said, "You didn't say anything," and he said, "There was nothing to say. You had it," and she said, "Say that again in the hall," and he said, "I won't," and she said, "I know. Write it down."

At about three hundred feet the metalwork on the port side was cold enough to sting and the box was in her hand and the needle was doing a quarter of a degree of nothing, and Marek understood, in a way he had not been able to explain on the ridge, why she would not put that box in a bracket.

---

The Captain Sorn was in the route office with the charter book open when they got back, and Ilva Sorn had done the arithmetic that she always did, and she did it out loud, and it was the correct arithmetic.

"Eleven crews in this yard take work off our name," she said. "Every one of them is a family. If the Office publishes a figure that differs from ours and the figure is right, we will have proved a point for a season and earned nothing for a year. If we are wrong, we do not get insured and the yard's cover goes."

"Then say the number," said Marek. "Both numbers. With the hour on each, and the dates, and a column for the difference. That is all the third line is."

"And who pays for the column?" said Ilva Sorn. "Ink."

"A shilling of ink is not a reason," said Tarin. "Write it and put it on the Exchange board as well and let the carriers read our column instead of a rumour, and if the Office won't print ours then print theirs and put ours under it, and if they won't do that then the column is the only thing in the basin that anybody can be shown."

Ilva Sorn looked at the two sheets for a while, and then she took the slate off the wall and wrote, in the small flat hand of a route officer signing a launch:

*Column of differences, published. Both hours, both dates. Agree, subject to the yard bearing its own errors in public.*

"Subject to that," she said. "Not subject to the first three lines. You two can settle the first three lines."

---

They settled them in the yard, at the boards, in front of a working yard, which is where Tarin Senn said a thing like that had to be settled because a thing settled indoors is a thing somebody can put in different words afterwards.

Marek wrote the hours up. Tarin read them back. And then, because the whole of the argument of a week and a half depended on who was holding what, she said it out loud, in the yard, to about eleven people and a man from a licensed carrier's office who had come to look at a ship:

"If the Office's method is one answer for everything, then the Line is a resource. A resource has a rate and a schedule and it does not get a column." She put her hand flat on the board. "The Line is not a resource. The Line is a witness, and if somebody's claim is made true by my hull being in the lane at an hour, then the claim names me, and the hours go on the board, and anybody in this yard can come and check whether I was there."

"That is going to cost you the work," said the man from the carrier's office.

"It has already cost me the work," said Tarin Senn. "It cost me a month of the *Petrel*'s overhead on the Tuesday morning of the previous week and it is going to cost me the rest of it over the next four, and I have been doing that arithmetic since the Tuesday and I did it then, and the only thing that has changed is that I have written it down."

And Marek Vale stood on the wet concrete of Kestrel Yards at four o'clock on a Sunday afternoon and understood that he had, for the first time in eleven years, been asked a question by a competent adult that he could answer exactly, and that the answer had been wanted, and that somebody had put it into a slate in chalk and would defend it in a room on Tuesday.

He felt the specific warmth of it go through him the way it had gone through him in a hall on a Thursday a week and a half ago, and this time he did examine it, because he had a rule about it now.

It was the warmth of *this is now somebody else's to do.*

And the somebody else's to do was a captain with a chalk line and four conditions, and a route officer with a public column, and a night watchwoman with two hours on a board, and about eleven crews in the yard, and none of them him.

He stood there in the drizzle and let the warmth be what it was, and told himself that this time it was simply the pleasure of competent company, and that this time there was no cost in it at all, and that he would find out what this time cost in about a fortnight, and that he had no idea what that was yet, and that not knowing was, on the evidence so far, the most dangerous condition a reader could be in.

Then he went and found the sheet on the notice case on the ridge, and read the new stamp on it, and stood in a corridor with a small level hand in violet ink and understood that four lines of chalk in a yard were going to have to be worth more than a good rule applied well.

*Reclassified 15:40, 17th day: unattached observation. The publication of an instrument, a place, or an author does not convert an observation into a claim. An observation remains an observation until it is entered with a place, a time, an effect, and a witness, and no amount of good faith supplies the four. Rule 6, Charter of Public Notice.*

She had printed the basis on Saturday's sheet — the western turn, the corridor, the four o'clock, published with the instruments named and the author named and a sentence about not being able to say why it was right. It had been on the wall of a drying room for five days and it had done what a truthful document does, which is to look more like a document and to be exactly as usable as it was on the Tuesday.

It had changed the appearance of the sheet by an enormous amount and had not moved it one inch, and Marek Vale stood in the corridor and thought: *I know exactly how to do that. I have been doing it since the sixth day with a piece of glass in a tube.*

---

The Council's request for a concurring seat for a trial claim had gone out on the Friday and the packet had come up the ridge on the Sunday morning with the ordinary post, and Marek read it in the outer office with the light off the frames.

It was addressed to three assemblies.

One of them was the Hearthward rain-garden assembly, because the terraces' upper tanks sit on the northern feeder, and the Council's own water schedule is a document that the terraces' steward had corrected in writing twice, which is a thing no district does by accident.

The request was reasonable and specific and procedural. It asked a single assembly to concur in support of a narrow certified trial claim at the Nacre Gate, with a named sponsor, a published expiry, and a listed alternative, and it explained in one paragraph what a trial claim was for, which was that the method had a good record and had never once been tested in public on a district that had prepared for it, and that the Council wished to establish whether a certified claim improved or degraded the preparations of the district it was tried on.

That last sentence had been written by somebody who was not a fool, and Marek read it three times, and thought: *somebody in there has read about the Hearthward claim, and has decided to find out whether it is general, and has decided to find out on a district rather than on the one that has already been burned.*

The request had been on Anwen Sarr's table since the Friday. She had had two days with it, which was one more than she needed, and Marek knew exactly what she was going to do with it, and understood, with a clarity that sat badly with him, that he was not going to be able to stop her and was not entitled to try.

She was owed a document. He was not going to have it.

It was the seventeenth day of the Bellweather autumn and the weather had settled cool and light and there was no reason on earth for anybody to be in a great hurry, and the Bellweather watch had been stood down on the eleventh, and Marek's name had come off it and gone onto nothing at all.
