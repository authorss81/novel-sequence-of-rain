# Chapter 17 — Nothing Interesting

Nell Cray had been the clerk of the Ferrygate district assembly for nineteen years and her entire professional ambition, which she had stated out loud to a council at the time of her appointment and had been gently mocked for ever since, was to get through a week in which nothing interesting happened.

The week of the fifteenth day of the Bellweather autumn was the best week she had had in four years. The western airs went soft and warm on the Thursday and the reach drained down past the low marker by the Friday afternoon, the road up from the ferry landing dried out to the top layer and stopped, and the only weather item on her board was a *possible* release at the Nacre Gate some time in the next ten days, of an uncertain size, at an uncertain hour, which is the single most useful kind of item a district can have on a board, because there is nothing at all to do about it yet.

The market was the thing. The Ferrygate market was three hundred yards of cobbles and canvas between the ferry landing and the gate road, and it had opened on the Saturday and had done so every Saturday for two hundred years, and it was the reason anybody came to Ferrygate at all, and the reason the Ferrygate district existed as a body that met, was that when the low flat is soft the carts cannot get in and out and a market that has to close is not a market, it is a room with vegetables in it.

So she had four things on the board and none of them was interesting, and she did them in order, and she went home at half past five both days.

---

The first was the lamp glass, which came up on the Wednesday barge and had to be counted against the order before the cart went back, because the order said forty-two and the barge always brought thirty-eight, and this time it had brought thirty-eight and a crate of hinges nobody had asked for.

The second was the boundary stone at Cauldron Rigg, which had been moved in the spring by a man putting in a fence and which had cost the district four hours and a great deal of goodwill, and which was resolved, this week, by the simple expedient of the fence being moved, because the man was a decent fellow and had never once had any idea that a stone was in his field.

The third was the release band, and the band was: *Nacre Gate, likely, within ten days, size not stated, hour not stated.* The water office publishes a band and not an hour, and the reason it publishes a band is that it does not have an hour, and the reason it does not have an hour is that the release depends on the upper reservoir and the upper reservoir depends on melt and rain and a great many people in the mountains making decisions about a lake.

The fourth was the rain-garden rota, and it was the only one of the four that Nell Cray had ever had any doubt about, and she had been getting it right for six years.

There were six names on it and the gang that turned up was generally five of them and a boy. They worked the low side of the district: the two rain gardens themselves, which were public beds and had been public beds for a century; the channels between the quay steps and the landing; and eleven hundred yards of the low lane that everyone in Ferrygate called the causeway and nobody called anything else. They cleared on a rota. It was not clever and it was not impressive and it was a piece of paper with six names and a column of days on it and about two hours of work on a Tuesday and a Thursday in the wet season.

The rota did not forecast anything. It made no claim on anybody. Nobody's bell rang for it. It had never once appeared in a weather notice, because a weather notice is about the sky and this was about a lane, and the Office's whole system of records had no drawer in it for a piece of paper about a lane.

But it was, without any ceremony and without anybody upstairs ever asking for it, the thing that kept four footpath closes from becoming a lane you could not get a cart down.

On the Friday she put it in the ordinary return.

---

The Council does not ask for a district's rain-garden rota. She was sending it because the Council had sent her a form in the spring, in the middle of a completely unrelated business about culvert inspection, on which one line asked for *"local flood precautions, list."* It was a badly designed form. It had a box for the precautions and no box for anything else, and a district clerk who wants a form to go away answers the box, and that was the whole of the reason the Ferrygate rain-garden rota had ever been seen by anybody in the Council's own building.

She filled it in. *Low side channels and causeway, cleared Tues and Thurs in the wet season, six-person rota, names attached, no forecast made, no claim entered, cost to the district about nine shillings a week in labour.* She wrote *no claim entered* because it was true and because she had discovered over nineteen years that a form with an empty box in it invites somebody to fill the box for you.

She addressed it to the Council's technical cell, which was where the form said to send it, and she put it in the Friday afternoon post with the boundary stone correspondence and a lamp-glass credit note, and then she did the thing she always did, which was to read the cover note one more time and decide it said enough.

*Sir, — Further to your form of the fourth day, the district's flood precautions are a clearing rota for the low side channels and the causeway. There is no forecast in this and no claim. It is not on any public notice and I do not put it on one. I send it because your box asked. — N. Cray, clerk.*

"There," she said out loud, in an empty office, to a form on a table. "Now nobody can say nobody told them."

---

On the Thursday afternoon she did the fourth thing, which was the shelter rota, and she did it herself, in her own boots, with a sheet of paper under her arm, because in nineteen years the sheet had been copied twice and it had never once been sent anywhere and there was no version of Ferrygate's low ground on which a piece of paper about two named people was going to be improved by a courier.

The rota had been on the same sheet since the winter before the one before last, and it had two names on it and it had been started for a reason that was no longer the reason.

*Ottilie Fisk, 4 Quay Steps, ground floor.*
*Abel Fisk, 4 Quay Steps, ground floor.*

That is the whole of it. Two lines and a sheet. It had been started six winters ago when the district had put a boat in for three days to take a woman called Fisk off the low side before the ice went in, and she had turned out to be perfectly well, and the sheet had stayed on the books anyway, and every winter since — in December, and in January if it had been hard, and twice since in the wet — Nell Cray had walked down to the Quay Steps and moved them up to her own mother's rooms above the chandler's, and put them back when it was over.

She did it in a warm afternoon in boots, with the two of them arguing about the kettle.

"You'll have the front room," said Ottilie Fisk, from the doorway, without any preamble at all. "And don't say it's a spare room, it's my mother's front room, and I'll have the bed made up, and you can have the chair that doesn't rock."

"It's not that kind of move, Ott."

"It's that kind of move."

"It is not," said Nell Cray. "It's a wet-season precaution and there is no water coming, and your gate's dry, and I have come down the lane for a different reason and I want you to look at a list of six names with me because one of them is your grandson's whole reason for anything."

She stayed an hour. She had intended to stay twenty minutes.

Ottilie Fisk was seventy-seven and turned seventy-eight at Michaelmas and had three rooms on the ground floor of number four with no stairs above them and a yard at the back with a step down into it that was a metre, and had lived in those rooms since before the ferry had a motor on it. She had a good ear, an appallingly good memory, and a method of testing a statement that consisted of asking it back to you in a slightly different shape.

"Abel," said Nell. "He'll be out."

"Abel will be out," agreed Ottilie Fisk. "Abel will be out by nine and he will be at the third sluice and he'll say the word *water* four times before he says his own name, and then I'll go and get him, and then you can have your wet-season precaution."

"He should come with us."

"He is eighty-two and he is perfectly capable of walking up a lane in the rain, thank you, and if you start putting eighty-two-year-old men in boats because of a piece of paper you wrote, this district will have a word with you."

"He is eighty-four."

"He is eighty-two and he will tell you he is seventy-seven and you may pick which of you two is lying to make me feel better about my winter."

The stove was going and the room smelled of tea and of a coat that had been damp since Tuesday. There was a kettle on the hob, two cups on the tray, and a third cup in the sink that had been in the sink since before Nell Cray was born, and there was a barometer on the wall by the door that had no glass in it and never had, and there was a lamp on the wall, and there was a boat hook standing in the corner by the yard door, which had been there for a very long time and which nobody in the household would explain to her.

"You've a hook in the corner," said Nell Cray.

"That's Abel's," said Ottilie Fisk. "That's Abel's business."

"It is a boat hook."

"It is Abel's business and you've come about a list of six names."

Nell Cray laughed until she had to put her tea down.

Then she said, because she had come to say it and had come down a lane to say it and it was the only reason:

"Ott. I've got to be honest with you about something I've been asked to sign."

"Are you."

"No. But somebody's asked me to put a name to a trial of the new method, at the gate, for a few days, and I said no, and I want you to know I said no before anybody starts coming round here asking."

"What method?"

"They're going to make a claim," said Nell Cray. "A narrow one. At the gate. With a time on it and a size on it, so the market can open on schedule instead of waiting for a band."

"Ay," said Ottilie Fisk. "And what's it cost?"

"I'm not sure anybody knows yet."

"Uh," said Ottilie Fisk, and got up, with a hand on the chair, though she did not need it, and went to the window and looked out at a yard and a step and eleven hundred yards of causeway with the tide out of it and the low marker clear by four feet. "Well. That's a good thing for the market," she said. "Nobody's market is a thing I'd want shut."

And then she turned round and said: "You're still doing the sheet?"

"I'm still doing the sheet."

"Fine," said Ottilie Fisk. "Then you'll do it in the wet as well this year, and don't come and tell me about it afterwards, and put the kettle on again, that one was weak."

---

Nell Cray walked back up the lane at half past five on the Thursday with the wet-season release band in her pocket and nothing at all on her board for Friday, and the two of them at number four still in the ground-floor rooms with a barometer with no glass in it and a boat hook in the corner, and the causeway dry, and the reach down, and the market that had opened on the Saturday and would open on the Saturday after that.

She had been doing the sheet for six years. She had never once written on it why.

---

On the Friday morning a clerk from the water office came down on the market bus with a printed sheet in a sealed folder, and the sheet said: *Nacre Gate release, probable, within fourteen days, size not stated, hour not stated.* It was a very slightly worse band than the one she already had on her board, and the Office had sealed it, and the seal was on it for a very good reason, which was that the water office could not put a number on the release and would rather that nobody acted on a band as though it were a number.

Nell Cray read it, said thank you, put it in the district book, and went out to the market.

The causeway was dry. The channels were clear. The rain gardens were cut back and the beds were doing nothing, which was what rain gardens do in a dry fortnight, and the two crews on the rota had turned out at ten on the Tuesday and at ten on the Thursday and had cleared the low side and gone home, and the ferryman had his extra hours posted on the board by the landing in case the lane went, which it had not, and would post them again in the wet, and nobody in Ferrygate had given that a second thought in six years because it was not a thought; it was just Tuesday.

She got through the Friday.

She closed the book at half past five, and she put the ordinary return into the tray by the door — the boundary stone and the lamp glass and the rain-garden rota with its covering note and a box on a form that said *local flood precautions, list* — and it went out with the last post, and she did not think about it again for a very long time.
