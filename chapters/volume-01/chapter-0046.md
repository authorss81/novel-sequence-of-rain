# Chapter 46 — The Word In The Queue

Nell Cray had come in on the Monday morning of the forty-sixth day at about half past eight with a form in a satchel and a covering note in her coat that she had written three times, and she got through the door of the basin house at about ten past nine, and by ten past ten she was in a queue.

There were about sixty people in it. That is the number and it was counted, later, by a man in the outer office doing a head count because nobody could think what else to do with a morning.

They were all there for the same thing. Four licensed carriers and two insurers had paper priced on the fifth of the month against a corridor figure the Office's own bulletin had since made an hour and a half too long, and a corridor that held for four days on a claim entered under a Director's authorization in a yard-side district, and a column of differences that had been in a Council's file for a fortnight, and two of the four carriers who had read that column and discounted the yard's cover in writing over it.

They wanted to know whether they were insured.

That is what sixty people wanted to know on the Monday morning of the forty-sixth day at a public table in a public building, and the answer to every one of them was **no**, and it was no because of a rule in the Charter of Public Notice and not because anybody in the basin had done anything to anybody, and it was correct, and there was no form anywhere for it, and there was no table anywhere in this city that anybody could go and stand in front of and read a column out of and be told what their own situation was.

The report was on the table. It was laid there at ten in the morning in the ordinary way, on a table, under a string, with a card, and it was twenty-two pages and it was, by the assessment of a woman who had spent four days in a review and had no reason to be kind to anybody in it, **a good report**, and she said so out loud in the hall, twice, to two different people, on the record as far as anybody was concerned, because she had lost nothing by any of it and it was true.

The method is recommended for continued trial.

The claim of the twenty-ninth is recorded as held and accurate: the corridor was passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth, the condition was accepted at 23:11, the effect held to the expiry, and the listed alternative was not triggered. **The claim is recorded as a claim, entered under the authorization of the Director of the twenty-seventh, not certified, with the authorization and the approval stamp on the sheet.** That is the whole of the finding about it and it is exactly the finding one would want if one were a carrier and it is exactly the finding one would not want if one were a warden, and both of those are consequences of a method that was recommended for continued trial on the same page.

The review records that **the effect of a certified or authorised claim on the preparations of the districts affected cannot be demonstrated from the record**; that the reason is not that it was not written down but that the record has never had anywhere to put it; and that the review recommends **one thing**, which is the column, and recommends no other remedy of any kind, and does not recommend that the Charter be amended, and does not make any finding against any individual.

The two reclassifications are set out in full, and correctly, on page fourteen, under a heading that says *matters of classification arising*, and nobody in that hall read page fourteen, and nobody in that hall would have objected to either of them, because both of them are true, and there is no crowd in Veyra that will stand up in a public hall and object to a thing that is true.

They are these. That the Lower Sill headwall on the thirtieth is a **maintenance failure**; that the structure was a Bellweather-era concrete headwall, shored in the season ending nine years ago and again six years ago, with a foot of its west cheek gone to gravel above the water line, reported to the water office in the spring and carried on a deferred maintenance schedule for two funding years without execution; and that no weather event on the thirtieth bears on the condition of that structure. Which is true.

And that the ninth ward's list of what the notices did not say, being seven lines on a ruled page in a warden's hand, stamped and dated by her, is **an internal district record, and not a public notice**, and is filed in the technical cell's file, and under Rule 6 of the Charter of Public Notice is not compellable and not producible. Which is also true, and which the report says with something approaching approval, because the report observes that the district's warden acted correctly throughout and that the Office's own instruments are the only reason the district could not be shown to have been right.

---

The yard's column was on the wall, because a column is a thing you put on a wall, and the yard had sent it up itself, framed, on the Line's own account, and it was the only object in that hall that anybody actually wanted.

There were about nine people round it at any moment and by eleven there were twenty, and two men from a carrier's office stood in front of it for about a quarter of an hour without saying anything at all.

One of them was about thirty and had a wax in his pocket that he did not take out, and he had read the column before anybody else had, and he said, out loud, in the ordinary voice of a man making an observation to a colleague in a public building:

"That is the only honest weather record I have ever held."

And his colleague, who was older, and who did not take his eyes off the column, said:

"So is mine. That is why it is a problem."

---

And then, at about half past eleven, out of the queue, in the plain sentence form that everybody in a basin uses when they are saying something they have been saying for years without knowing it is a fact:

"My side of the river has got a **dry line** on it."

Nobody in that hall had used the phrase. Not once. Not in a report, not in a minute, not in a column, not in a review, not by a Director or an assessor or a Deputy or a records man or a route officer or a district chair or a warden or a shed foreman with a brush.

A woman in a queue, who had been standing in the wet for an hour, and who had come in out of the rain-garden end of the basin with her coat streaming, said it to the man in front of her in a queue, the way you say a thing that has been sitting in your mouth for a month and has only just found a shape.

And it went into the record as a member of the public's word for a place where a preparation used to be available and is not.

---

Marek Vale was not in the hall. He was in the maintenance shop on the ridge, because he had been censured on Sunday and dismissed from field duty on Sunday and was not, as of the Monday morning, on anything at all, and was doing the ordinary backlog, because there is always work in a maintenance shop.

He heard about it at about two in the afternoon from a shop's boy who had been sent down to fetch a frame and had come back with it and a story, in the way that shop's boys have been carrying information out of public buildings in this basin for two hundred years.

"There's a word going round, Mr Vale."

"What word."

"Dry line," said the boy. "It's not the Office's. A woman said it in the hall and it's got about now. They said at the table it was a woman's word, not a term, and they wrote it down as a woman's word, and there's two men arguing about it in the queue who can't agree whether it means the south bank or the whole of the low ground."

"What did the two men say?"

"The young one said it meant the reach, because the reach is where the line is. The old one said it meant the ground, because the ground is where the people are." The boy shifted the frame. "And then they both said the same thing, which is that neither of them knows who ruled it, and the old one said that was the part that would be interesting."

---

**The Office adopted it inside a week.**

That is the whole of that sentence and it should not be softened. On the same afternoon, in the outer office of the Weatherglass, a clerk was asked, in the ordinary course, to open a new heading in a returned ledger, and wrote at the top of it **DISPLACED TURNS — DRY LINES**, because the form already said what the form said and a clerk completes a form in the form's own words and this building has spent six weeks proving that that is how everything happens.

Nobody costed it. There is no line in any budget in this basin for a heading, and a heading is not a thing anybody funds, and the ledger was already printed and the type was already set and the column was already going in the returns whether it was called something or not.

And that is the whole of the Office's adoption of a member of the public's word for the thirtieth of a month in which nothing whatever was happening in the sky: a clerk wrote two words on a ledger in a fair hand without lifting the pen, on the same afternoon, in the ordinary course of a clerk opening a page.

**Nobody in this building chose the phrase.** That is the actual finding about the coinage, and it is not a comfortable one, and it is the same shape as every other finding in this month: the institution's answer was a form, and the form had a box, and a woman in a queue put the right two words in it, and the box is now full.

Which is not the same thing as the woman being listened to, and has never at any point in the history of this basin been the same thing as the woman being listened to, and there is no version of a coinage in which the person who coins it is the person who gets the credit.

---

The queue got to the desk at about half past two, and Nell Cray was in it, and she was not there for the report.

She was there with a form. It was a sheet of the Council's own paper with a heading she had been given in the outer office in the March and had put in a tray in April and had never returned, and it had a box on it, and the box was the only reason the thing had ever been seen by anybody in the technical cell, and the box said:

*Local records of district practice. List.*

She filled it in on the Monday of the forty-sixth day in about eleven minutes, and it is the fourth time anybody in Ferrygate has filled in that box, and it is the first time anybody has filled it in under a heading that is not *flood precautions*, and the first time it has gone in with a covering note longer than the last three, and the last three covering notes were three of the best pieces of writing Nell Cray has ever done and this one is the best of the four and it is four times as long as any of the others and it begins like this:

*Sir, — Further to your form of the fourth day, and to the review of the method reported at the public table on this day, the Ferrygate district records the following as its own local records of district practice, and asks that they be admitted to whatever place the Charter provides for them, if there is one, and if there is not, that this form be returned to the district with a note saying that there is not.*

*The practice of this district in the ordinary course, for at least nineteen years and in my own hand for the last six of them, is as follows. A clearing rota for the low-side channels, the two public rain gardens, and eleven hundred yards of the low lane, with six names and a column of days, worked on a Tuesday and a Thursday in the wet season, at about two hours a day, at a labour cost to the district of about nine shillings a week in the wet months. A low-ground shelter rota, a sheet of two lines, held in the district book, six years, moved on by the clerk in boots. And an ordinary return to your office on the fifteenth of the month, in answer to a box on a form about culvert inspection, sent because your box asked.*

*None of these is a forecast. None of these makes a claim. None of these has ever been a public notice and I do not put them on one. I have said all of that in three previous covering notes and I am saying it again in this one because this form appears to be a different form, and because the reason I am saying it again is not for your benefit.*

*The reason is this. On the twenty-first day of the month, the Council's certified claim did two of those three things, in a four-line letter, on the agenda, minuted. The third it did not touch. I have established that since the twenty-first and I have established it as a matter of record, and the record of it was made by a Council assessor in this building on the thirty-eighth day, and it says in eleven words that the effect of a claim on the preparations of a district cannot be demonstrated from the record, and it gives the reason, and the reason is my own district's fault, and the fault is that we never told you we had anything.*

*I am not writing to complain and I want that in your file. I am writing because there is no box in this building for what we are, and there is a form with a box, and I am putting our practice in the box, and the fourth time I have filled that box in I would like somebody to write back and say either that it is a place or that it is not.*

*Yours faithfully, N. Cray, clerk, Ferrygate district assembly.*

She handed it in at about ten past three and the man at the counter read the first line and then looked at the length of it and read the rest of it, standing up, which took him about four minutes, and he was not a clerk of the technical cell and he had no authority over any of it.

"Madame Cray, this is very unusual."

"I know. It is the fourth one and it is the fourth that has been sent, and I have said in the note where the other three are, and I would be obliged if you would initial the bottom of it, because the first three have not been initialled and I am tired of being told that nothing arrived."

He initialled the bottom of it. She checked that he had dated it as well, and he had, and she thanked him, and she went out into a corridor.

And the thing she had been carrying for five weeks — the specific exhaustion of a competent woman who has spent nineteen years being right and has discovered that being right was the mechanism — sat on her in the bottom of the stairs of the basin house at about ten past three in the afternoon, and it sat there because the form went in and the box was still not a box, and the box was still a long way down, and she had been told, in writing, in a corridor, by a man with a stamp, that there is no such form.

And it did not help. And she had not expected it to. She had written the note in the knowledge that it would not help, and she had written it because it was the fourth one and because the first sentence of the first one had been *there, now nobody can say nobody told them*, and she was not going to stop saying that until somebody told her something.

Then she went to look at the column on the wall, because sixty people had been looking at it all morning and she had not, and she stood in front of it for about a minute and a half, and read the six items, and read the sixth one twice:

*Turns displaced: a reach-level window on the morning of the thirtieth, forty minutes, not established in any instrument of this office.*

Nell Cray had a great many letters. She had about four hundred and a bit words left in her by about two o'clock in the morning most nights. She had, since the twenty-first, been entirely unable to convert any of it into a sentence, and this was the first time in five weeks that a piece of paper had given her a sentence.

"Forty minutes," she said, out loud, in a public hall, to nobody. "**Forty minutes.** And not one instrument in this building has ever looked at it."

And a woman of about sixty standing next to her, who had been in the queue for an hour and had not got a form and had not got a column and had come in from the low end of the basin, said:

"Forty minutes is what a dry line is, love. It isn't a place. It's a length. It's the length of the bit you'd have had."

---

Nell Cray turned round and looked at her.

"My name's Nell Cray," she said. "I'm the clerk of Ferrygate district. I have a form that they won't take and I have a boat hook against the wall of my office and I am going to be forty-one in a year nobody is going to remember, and I would like to know your name, because I have just been given a sentence by a piece of paper and I would like to know who gave me the other end of it."

"Ida," said the woman. "Ida Crome. Ninth ward. I live in the fourth block and I have got a foot of water in my ground floor till the first and I am not insured for a bucket."

And then the two of them stood in a public hall in a basin city on the Monday afternoon of the forty-sixth day of a month in which nothing whatever was happening in the sky, in front of a framed column of a yard's published differences and a card on a table with a twenty-two-page report on it under a string, and argued about the thirtieth with a ledger between them, and they were both right, and it was a better argument than anybody in the building had had all day, and it went on for about forty minutes, and about nine people joined it, and by half past four there were about thirty, and there is no minute of it anywhere.

Which is the first time in four months that anybody in this city argued in public about a cost, and it did not happen in a chamber with a clerk in it, and it did not happen because of a hearing, and it did not happen because a Deputy had been asked a question in public, and it happened because two women were standing in front of a column.
