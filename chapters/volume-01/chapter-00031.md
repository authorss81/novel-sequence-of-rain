# Chapter 31 — Four Minutes

The south-bank road went at five o'clock in the morning.

It came down in the ordinary way that a bank comes down in a peak — about forty feet of hillside above the third switchback, wet through, off a root and onto the road, and then the road itself going for a length of about thirty yards into the cut above the reach.

Marek Vale heard about it at twenty past five on the south bank, from Petrie, who had walked up two miles to the hard ground at the top of the switchback at half past four to see whether a water cart could get through, and had come back down, and was standing at the head of the flat with a lamp in his hand at twenty past six in the morning and looking, in the words of a man who has been keeping a shed since before Marek's father was born, like somebody who has been hit in a place that is not on him.

"That'll want the water office's engineer and a week," said Petrie. "And a week's the end of it, and it's the end of *the* water office, and I'll not see it. It's a road. It's storm. It happens. You don't put a road in a column."

"Right," said Marek.

"Don't you," said Petrie. "They'll try. They'll put it under the culvert because the culvert's on the sheet and the culvert's old, and then a fella in a wet coat can't get a ledger to say a hill did it." He spat. "Write it where it goes. Road. Storm. Fifth of the month. If a hill's got to hold up a freight apron it never should have been let near the water in the first place."

He said it the way a man says a thing he has said before, to a road, and then he went and got the level staff.

---

Marek had signed out a tide-gauge docket on the Friday afternoon from the water office's district crew, and it was a real docket and the errand on it was real, because the ninth ward's outfall gauge had not been read by anybody since the ninth of the month and the district read it by eye and the Office did not read it at all, and there is a strip of glass in a tube that tells you what a stone apron has been doing.

It got him through the turn onto the south bank at half past five in the morning. It got him nothing else. There is no other use for it and he knew that when he signed it out and he had signed it out anyway, on Friday, at half past four, before he had decided he was going to do this, and he was careful about the order of those two things and the order was correct.

He had come to be near it. That was the whole of it. He had entered a claim at half past four the previous afternoon which had taken a quarter of an hour of eased air out of the last evening of a month, and the quarter of an hour of eased air was the ebb, and the ebb was the bottom of the reach, and the bottom of the reach was under a foot and a half of water and would come out at about half past seven if the basin drew the way it had drawn for about nine days out of every nine, and nine men were standing at a shed on this bank with a banded timber stack and a replacement dewatering pump already on a flatbed, loaded, assigned, and told nothing at all.

He came to be near it and there was no one to tell and no one to be near it with and no reason on earth for a maintenance hand from the Weatherglass ridge to be on a low flat at six in the morning with a lamp, and he had known that at half past four the previous afternoon, and he had come.

---

The reach came down.

Marek had been on the flat for an hour with the lamp and the staff and the docket, and at about twenty past seven Petrie put his hand on the post at the edge of the flat where the weathered mark was cut round it at the height of a man's knee, and where, on the eleventh of the month, the mud had stood four inches over the mark and had for weeks.

The mud was off it. All of it. The mark was standing out of a flat grey mud that was taking a boot without argument, and Petrie looked at it for about four seconds and then said a word that is not a swear and is much older than most swears, and the shed door went up and two men came out with a pole.

"Right," said Petrie. "Right. That's it. Get the flatbed."

There were nine of them. They had been nine all month and they were nine at seven in the morning on the thirtieth, and the flatbed came off the blocks with four hundred feet of shoring timber on it, banded, with the bands on, and the replacement dewatering pump on its pallet behind it, and the pump's drum with a Union guarantee on it and a cover over it that had been on since the eleventh of the month and would be on for ever.

They got to the mark at ten past eight. The mud took the leading wheels. Petrie walked it forward on the staff, ten feet, twenty, forty, out toward the middle of the flat with the water still on the far side of it and about a foot deep where he could see the light go into it, and the tide-marks on the flat in front of him were at three feet four inches below the mark and falling.

Marek stood on the hard ground with the lamp and looked at the reach.

It was the correct water. That was the thing he could not get past and it was the thing that took him the whole morning. The reach was down four feet from where it had stood on the Friday, and the ebb was on it, and the basin was drawing, and the draw had come up the western side and gone through and was going out through the reach exactly the way a man would want it to, and the last morning of a month was standing there in front of him in the correct shape, and nine men and a timber stack and a pump had come forty feet onto a low flat in the basin.

And at about ten past eight the long band came back.

---

He felt it before anybody said anything, and he would have felt it anywhere, because the thing that changed was not the water and it was not the wind and it was not the air: it was the *going*. The flat had been going — the ebb, the pull, the water leaving the bottom of the basin — and at about ten past eight it stopped going, and then it came back, and it came back the way a man walks backwards up a slope, steadily, without hurrying, for about four hours.

Petrie's staff went in two feet inside a minute and three inside three.

"Get her back," he said, and did not shout, and his voice was the voice of a man who has done this four hundred times. "Tim, get the flatbed's nose round. Do not back her, do not turn her, take her round on the dray horses and put her off the flat the way we came on. Off the flat. Now. Nobody hurry and nobody run."

Eleven minutes to get a loaded flatbed ninety feet back onto hard ground with a foot of water coming up under the hind wheels, and they did it, and the pump's pallet went over at the very end and four men got a hand under it and put it back on the bed and the bands held.

At about ten to nine they were on the hard ground at the top of the flat with the timber banded and the pump on its pallet and the beds full of the light brown water of a reach that had come up four feet in fifty minutes.

Petrie walked back down the flat with the staff to about eighty feet off it, and stood there a while, and came back up.

"That's the ebb," he said.

"Yes," said Marek.

"That's the ebb coming *back*."

"Yes."

"Right," said Petrie. He put the staff down and put his hands on his hips and looked out at about four hundred yards of basin water and a basin beyond it with a body of weather sitting on it that had not moved in a fortnight. "That's not the ebb. The ebb goes down till it can't. I've been on this flat thirty years and I've watched it go down a foot and a half and stand and go down again and it never comes back up while it's still raining and there's draw on the top. Not once. Not in thirty years."

"No," said Marek.

"Then what's it doing."

And here is the sentence, and Marek Vale has thought about it since, on a long road, and once in the small hours, and it is the one thing from the thirtieth of that month that he would take back if he were permitted to take one thing back.

"It was just the water," he said.

---

Petrie nodded at him for about two seconds.

"Right," he said. "Just the water."

And Marek stood in the mud on the top of a low flat forty miles from a ward with four hundred and forty people in it, with a lamp in his hand, and he knew the whole of it, and every single word he had said was a lie, and the lie was a *true sentence in the only vocabulary the conversation had*. There is no word in this basin for a quarter of an hour of air going out of an evening. There is a word for it in four instruments on a ridge and none of them is in this basin and one of the four is in his coat and belongs to nobody. There is a word for it in a filing system in a room he may not enter without a reason he cannot manufacture. There is no word for it in a shed, and in a shed there is one language, and the language of a shed is *weather* and *water* and *maintenance*, and the water in the language of a shed is just the water.

He had, in the space of one sentence, closed a subject. That was the whole of what he had done and he had done it to protect a plate with no chain behind it, and it had cost nothing at all, and nobody would ever be able to show a single thing, and there is not one person in this basin who is harmed by it, and a man and a barrow and a shed foreman are standing on a flat in the rain and none of them will ever know.

He had never felt better about anything in his life. He noticed that, too, and did not write it down.

Petrie went and got the level book.

It is a shed book. It is a hard-backed book with a column for the date, a column for the mark's height, a column for the weather, and a column headed *work done*, and it has been kept in that shed since before Marek's father and it is the only record in the city of what the Lower Sill culvert's repair window has been doing for about forty years.

He wrote in it. He wrote the date, and he wrote the height of the mark at twenty past seven and at ten past eight, and he wrote the weather, and then he came to the last column and stopped with the pen about an inch above the paper.

"There's no box for this," he said. "There's a box for *work done* and a box for *no work, reason* and I've never had to use the second one in thirty years because you don't stand a crew in a shed for nothing." He looked up. "You'd write *reach came back*. That's all it is. That's not a reason, that's a fact. You don't stand nine men in a shed because a fact turned round."

"That's what you'd write," said Marek Vale.

"That's what I'd write," said Petrie, and wrote it, and shut the book, and put it on the shelf behind the door where it lives.

He did not ask who sent for them. Nobody had sent for them. Nobody was going to be asked, and there was nobody to be asked, and the nine men went and stood a shift in the shed, and the timber stayed banded, and the pump stayed on its pallet under a cover that had been on since the eleventh of the month.

At about eleven Marek read the reach gauge on the stone apron at the foot of Sump Lane, which was his errand and which he did properly, with the strip, and wrote the figures on the back of the docket, and then went up the hill and put the tube in the coat and the coat on a hook and stood for a moment with his hand on it.

---

Neris Kade's runner came down to the office at about half past twelve with a message that consisted of four words and a street.

*Fourth block. Doorway. Nobody.*

---

The fourth block of Low Spindle is a row of stone-fronted houses that were built with their ground floors below the street because that is what the ground is. The wardens had been through the whole of the lowest four blocks in the small hours of the night and had got everybody out of every ground floor in the block into the upper rooms or the assembly rooms or the second pump house, and it had taken all night and it had worked, and there is a line in the district's own wet notebook that says so in Sergeant Ampow's words, which is *all floors clear by twenty past four, no one left on the low side but the water*.

At about twenty past one in the afternoon somebody said at the district office that a boy had gone back.

Immy Vant was six. His family was in the second-floor rooms of number fourteen, and at about eight in the morning he had gone back down into his own ground-floor room, which was under nine inches of water and had been since before first light, to fetch a bird cage, which was in the corner, and which his mother had told him to leave, and which he had wanted very much, and which he was still holding.

The doorway of number fourteen is a channel. The house is a hundred and thirty years old and the flags have come up a quarter of an inch and the door is a door, and the low sill of it is four inches under the pavement, and by twenty past one in the afternoon there was a foot and a half of moving water across the pavement of the fourth block and going through that doorway at a rate that a man of any size would feel.

Marek Vale was on the pavement because he had come up the street from the apron with the docket in his hand and a lamp and no other reason, and he was the only person on that pavement for about eleven minutes.

There were crews out on the drain run. The second pump house crew was in the third block. The fourth block was a street of six doors and it was between shifts. Ampow was four streets away with about sixty people and two buses to move and was not going to be back inside four minutes, and the ward's own second pump was at its limit, and the water in the fourth block was not going to wait for anybody to come.

He could have run. He ran for about eleven seconds, in a good coat, with a docket in his hand, and he understood inside four steps that he could not be back inside four minutes, and that a six-year-old boy holding a bird cage in a doorway in a foot and a half of moving water could not be in that doorway for six minutes and keep standing up.

He got the lamp off his belt and put it on the sill inside the door where it would show, and he went in.

---

It was cold in a way that has nothing to do with the season. That is the part that everybody who has been in moving water remembers and nobody can explain: it is not that it is cold, it is that the cold comes up through the legs and the chest and meets a warmth in the middle of the body and makes a ring of it, and a body in cold moving water starts making decisions that are not decisions.

He got four minutes.

He went in to the sill, and the doorway is four feet wide and the room is a front room with a stone floor, and the boy was eight feet in, standing on a chair that had been against the wall, holding a wire cage with a canary in it over his head with both arms up, and the water was at his knees and rising and had been at his knees for a while.

Marek took the cage in one hand off the top of the boy's arms and put it under his arm and put his other hand on the boy's shoulder, and said, in the voice he used on a floor full of strangers in the rain, "Immy. I have you. Look at the lamp. Come on."

They went out in about two and a half minutes, which was the easy part, and then the doorway and then the pavement, and the pavement at four in the afternoon in the last week of a month with four inches of new water over the old, and a step up onto a stoop that a hundred and thirty years of the ninth ward's low ground had made a foot and a quarter high.

The last four inches were the worst. He got the boy up and got his own knee on the stoop stone and had to stop for a second with his face about six inches over the water, and the water went into his left ear on the way up because his head was turned and because the boy was struggling and because a man who is carrying a six-year-old does not get to choose the side he leans on.

It was about a second and a half of it. The sergeant's own boys had it in a bucket for the child's feet and the district's first-aider had it on a stove for the boy afterwards and had it in his own ears for three days after the eighth of the month, and everybody said the same thing about it: *you get used to it*.

You do not get used to it. The ear is not a hole. The ear is a very fine instrument and it is full of water at about the temperature of a district's drain run in a wet month, and a second and a half of that is enough.

---

Ampow got there at about ten past two with two men and a door and a blanket, and the boy was on a stoop in a blanket with a canary under a coat, and was furious and unhurt, and Immy Vant's mother came at about half past and did not say anything to Marek at all, which was correct and which he was grateful for in a way he could not have said out loud.

The ringing started about twenty minutes later.

It is not a loud thing. It is a very high thin unbroken note on the left side, sitting on top of everything else, and it does not go away, and after about a week you stop noticing it and after about a month you notice that you have stopped noticing it, which is worse.

The dullness was the part he was worried about, and the dullness is specific and a man who reads sequences for a living knows exactly what it has taken: about a third of the fine range on the left. The low end is fine. The middle is fine. The fine end — the part where a lamp behind a plate and a lamp not behind a plate are two different readings, the part that separates a smear from a dropout, the part that is the whole trade — that has gone quiet, and it is not coming back, and four minutes in cold moving water is the entire of the reason.

He got the district office's back room at about half past three and sat down on a bench with his coat still wet to the knee.

Ott Belm, the district copy, was at the long table with a wet notebook open and had been since the small hours, and he looked at Marek for a moment and then went and got him a cup of tea and a towel and did not ask, and did not write it down, and the not-writing was the single kindest thing anybody did for Marek on the thirtieth of that month and he has never told anybody about it.

Ampow came in at about four and put a folded sheet on the table.

"Immy's fine. Wrist's not broken, it's a bad one, he's seen worse from a gate. The Vants want to thank you and I've told them you'll not want it, and if that's wrong you can tell me and I'll go back."

"Thank them from me," said Marek, "and tell them to leave the bird alone."

"Done." Ampower stopped at the door. "Mr Vale. Weren't you on that watch last week? The ward floor. Nineteen of us."

"Yes."

"Then you know what I mean," said Sergeant Ampow, "about a thing being a bit much to be writing down."

"You do," said Marek Vale.

---

He got back to the ridge at about six and he was wet to the knee and he had a docket with two figures on the back of it and a tube in his coat and a lamp and nothing else at all.

He did not go up to the chart room and he did not go to the bench and he did not take the plate out. He sat on a stool in the maintenance shop for about a quarter of an hour with his hands on his knees while the high thin note on the left side of his head went on being there, and then he got up and did the ordinary backlog, because there is always work in a maintenance shop, and he did it.

At about seven he put his coat on and went down the corridor past the field office to the locked drawer, because the record is on the ridge and the record is a thing he was still, technically and in every way that mattered, the man who put his name into, and he wanted to see it.

The ward ledger sheet for the twenty-ninth was in its place in the tray and the arch wire had taken it and the tray was squared.

The emergency ledger had a new line in it in Tovan Iles's hand, without lifting the pen, and it said: *Claim entered, 29th. Conditional. Kestrel corridor passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth. Sponsor M. Vale, reader. Entered under the Director's authorization. Not certified.* And below it, in a different ink, a later addition: *Condition accepted and triggered, thirtieth, 23:11. Line at the yards' mark. Kestrel corridor held to expiry. — T. Iles, entering clerk.*

And the weather entry for the thirtieth said: *Bellweather, body. Sustained western draw. Peak rain 02:00–05:00. Rainfall on the ridge, 4.1 in.*

And that was all of it. There was no line in either book about a window on a reach, and there never was going to be one, because there is no instrument in the Weather Office of this city that has ever looked at a reach.

Marek Vale read the two books in a locked room on a ridge with four inches of dry in his hair and a lamp he had not switched on, and then he shut them, and the ledger in the Weatherglass was identical to what it was at six o'clock that morning.
