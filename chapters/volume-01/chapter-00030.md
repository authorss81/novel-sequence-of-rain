# Chapter 30 — The Line at the Mark

The *Petrel* took the point at half past three in the morning on the thirtieth day of the Bellweather autumn with the band under her right wing and eleven hundred feet of it out in front, and the yard's three ships out of the way, and a fourth ship — the *Marrow*, out of Nacre apron, eleven days out and carrying nothing anybody would thank her for — coming round the point behind her at four minutes' spacing, and Ilva Sorn's launch book said the *Marrow* was in the lane at a quarter past eleven and after that the lane was the yard's.

Tarin Senn had the mark at about ten minutes to eleven.

She had said, at the boards on the Friday evening, that the yard would be under sail at a quarter past four and it was, and the wind window had been there at four, and she had been in the band since half past three, and the three hours of it had gone exactly the way her own box had said it would go, and the third column on the board in Kestrel Yards was, at that moment, about half an inch wide, and would be about half an inch wide on Saturday morning, and the carriers would read a seal and not a column and would be satisfied and would not know what the half inch had cost.

Nobody was on the bridge with her but a boy of nineteen called Wen Ardery, who had been in the yard four months, and who had the board on his knees and the sounding line in his hand and had not said a word for two hours because he had been told not to, and whom she had therefore not looked at, because a captain who looks at a nineteen-year-old in front of four carriers is doing it for the passenger, not for the boy.

---

Here is what a claim does to a lane, and Tarin Senn had known the first half of it for eleven years and the second half of it was going to arrive at a quarter to midnight without any warning at all.

The first half: a lane is not a road. A lane is an hour you can put a hull in, and the hour is made of a gradient standing between two airs, and the gradient is a physical thing with a shape. A gradient with a *bend* in it is a good gradient. The bend is where the air eases. You take the long part of the band and you hold the hull in it, and the bend is the piece of the hour that lets you put a ship's head off and come down onto a mark without spending everything you have.

She had the bend. She had flown on it a hundred and fifty times to Hearthward and she had put four ships onto wet grass because of it and had been paid for it every time.

The second half arrived at about a quarter to midnight and it arrived like weather, and it was this: the bend went flat.

Not slowly. The band did not ease. The long band came up out of the west with a pressure in it that was absolutely steady — not steady the way a river is steady, steady the way a *rule* is steady — and the hook under it, the quarter of an hour where the air lets go and a ship can turn and a hull can come down onto a mark, was not there.

Not weaker. *Absent.* As though it had never been offered.

The *Petrel* held her line. That is the thing about a held turn: a hull in a held turn does not have to be flown. It has to be *steered*. There is nothing to ride. She came down the last two miles of the lane like a train on a rail she could see, and it was the most beautiful air Tarin Senn had ever been in, and she was not enjoying it, because a corridor that is *that* dependable is not a corridor. A dependable corridor is a corridor with nothing left in it.

At about ten minutes to eleven the *Marrow* came up her port quarter, right on spacing, and came level, and Wen Ardery put the board down and said, in a voice with a crack in it, "Captain, she's on the same line."

"I can see she is."

"That's not—"

"I can see she is," said Tarin Senn. "Midships. And do not say it, and do not write it down, and do not say it in front of him." She put her hand on the rail. "Because it is true, and because if I say it out loud I will have to look at it, and there is no time."

---

The yards' mark is a painted bar about nine feet long on the apron at the head of the slip, and a ship in the lane takes a line from the apron crew, and the apron crew stands where the apron crew has stood for forty years, which is eight feet back from the mark with a coil each.

Wen Ardery went down at ten minutes to eleven, because he was the junior and it was his turn, and the *Petrel* took the bar at eleven minutes past eleven, which was inside the hour, and he had the coil out and the line away and the turn coming on.

Tarin Senn was on the bridge with the *Marrow* a length and a half on her quarter, and she saw the whole thing from above, and it took about two seconds.

On an ordinary night the line would have gone slack. That was the ordinary thing about it and nobody had ever written it down: a line on a moving apron with a shore crew on it in a crosswind off the water goes slack in the lulls and lies along the apron and lets a man put his foot in it and shift it, and it is the lulls that make a mooring safe, and the lulls are the gusts' business.

The gusts had stopped.

The turn was held. Every part of it was held, and one of the parts it was holding was the ordinary business of the wind, and the ordinary business of the wind had been a line going slack every forty seconds, and it was not going slack, and the line was under load and square and coming off the coil at a rate that Wen Ardery was holding with both hands on a knee.

He let go of it at about eleven minutes past eleven, which was correct, and it went across his right wrist as it went, and the sound of it was a sound that Tarin Senn heard over the engine noise from thirty feet up and will hear again.

The ship held. The bollard held. The line held. The yard was not hit and the apron was not hit and the *Marrow* was a length and a half astern and did not have to alter a thing.

The cost was one wrist.

---

Afterwards there were four people who each did the right thing, and Tarin Senn went over all four of them for about eleven years afterwards, and the only one she could not forgive was herself and she never entirely worked out why.

The yard's rigger took the line and made it fast and did not shout at anybody.

Wen Ardery sat down on a coil and held his wrist against his chest and did not say anything at all about it, which was the correct professional response and which Tarin Senn has thought about a great deal since.

Ilva Sorn came up the slip and took the *Marrow* in, because the *Marrow* was a length and a half on the quarter and there was a junior on the apron and the route officer made the launch call, which she had made four times in nineteen years and never once to a ship, and she made it correctly, and the *Marrow* was in at eleven minutes past midnight and did not touch anything.

And the fourth was the lift.

Tarin Senn put her own hands on the deck rail and went up the lift, and the lift was for the rigging, and the rigging was thirty feet up, and what she actually needed was not the rigging at all: what she needed was a pair of hands on the loaded line at the mark while Wen Ardery was taken in, and the only pair of hands that was not busy was the captain's, because the junior was the one who had let go of the line and the route officer was on the *Marrow* and the rigger was the one who had it and could not let go of it.

She was on the lift for four minutes and eleven seconds. It is in the yard's book, because lifts are logged.

---

The two minutes of the forty she could remember in detail and she was not going to describe them to anybody ever, and they were this: that for about two minutes on a held turn, with a band under her that had nothing left in it, a ship with a claim on her in a yard that had a column of differences on a board was the most beautiful object in the basin, and that she was aware of this at the time, and that the awareness arrived in the middle of a mooring, and that it did not go away when she got her hands on the line.

That was the discovery, and it was a small one, and it is the one she has never said out loud.

A claim under a Director's authorization does not tell you what it has done to the thing you are standing in.

A claim is a sheet. The sheet has a place and a trigger and an effect and an alternative and an expiry and a sponsor's name on it, and Marek had written the alternative himself and written it well, and there is not one line on that sheet about the wind on an apron at eleven minutes past midnight and there is not going to be one, because a claim's whole reach is what it says, and what it says is a corridor.

He had taken a quarter of an hour of eased air out of the last evening of a month, because a quarter of an hour of eased air was the turn that most directly contradicted an hour that was held to four o'clock. That is the mechanism. That is what he did, and he did it in front of a clerk who writes without lifting her pen, and it was true, and it saved the corridor, and it also took the lulls out of a mooring on the yards' apron, and neither of those two facts was going to appear on the sheet and one of them had cost a nineteen-year-old his wrist.

She thought about the four lines on the slate on the Thursday, and about the words *a term sheet protects a yard's reputation and not a crew's body* which she had said out loud in a yard a day before, in front of nine people, and had not known she had said it until she had said it.

She had known it. She had known it and she had said it and she had then let a claim go into the world on the strength of four chalk lines and a route officer's proviso.

---

The *Marrow* was alongside at eleven minutes past midnight. Wen Ardery's wrist was put in a splint by the yard's own first-aider at about half past, and the first-aider said the two words everybody says, and Wen Ardery said thank you, and asked whether he could have his hours in the launch book credited, and Tarin Senn said he could and wrote it in herself.

At about a quarter past one the lane began to close, exactly as the claim's alternative said it would if the condition had not been accepted, except that the condition had been accepted, and the yard's own figure had said half past three.

The *Petrel* lay alongside for two hours with the band going slack overhead and the yard lamps on, and Tarin Senn sat on the after mooring bollard with her coat over her knees and did not sleep, and at about half past three she went and looked at the board.

Three sheets, and a fourth, and the fourth had been up since Friday evening in a careful hand with no instruction and no note.

*Claim entered, 29th, ward ledger bench. Conditional. The Kestrel corridor remains passable to and through 04:00 on the thirtieth. Sponsor M. Vale, reader. Director's authorization. Not certified.*

Nessa Tarrow had drawn a line under it at some point in the night and written one word above the line, in the same careful hand, with no instruction:

*BIND.*

Nobody had said anything to her. Nobody had rung a bell. There was no wire and no violet square and no second sheet on the arch wire in the basin house, because a claim under a Director's authorization is not certified and does not have to be reported to the Council, and the only apparatus in this city that can show that a claim has become true is a *trace*, and the only trace in this basin that could show it was on a plate in a tube in a coat on a hill.

Tarin Senn stood in front of a board in a yard at half past three in the morning and read one word in a night watchwoman's careful hand, and understood that the man who had put his name on it was at that moment asleep or not, forty miles away, in a bed or at a bench, and had been told nothing, and would be told nothing, and that there was no mechanism anywhere in the whole of this city by which anybody would ever tell him.

She found that she did not much mind. That was the surprising part and she looked at it for a while.

Then she went and found the plate-writer's slate and chalk and wrote underneath Nessa Tarrow's word, in her own hand, with the hour on it, which was the third line and had been the third line since the fifth of the month and would be the third line in this yard as long as there was a yard:

*Line at the mark 11:11 p.m. Accepted. Lane held to 04:00. Difference vs Office: none. — T. Senn.*

And then, because a column of differences that only ever reports good news is not a column of differences, and because she had been up all night on a hull that had held, and because she had just spent four minutes on a lift that should have been a junior's:

*Injury on the apron at 11:11 p.m., mooring hand, right wrist. Cause: no lulls in the crosswind while the turn held. Cause is the claim's second effect and is not written on the sheet and there is no part of the sheet that it could be written on. Not the yard's error. The yard published its hours and the hours were right and the yard's own errors have been published every day for eight days and this is not one of them, because the yard did not get it wrong. Published anyway, on the third line, because it happened here.*

---

Marek Vale came down the hill on the Saturday morning at about eleven, on foot, without a docket and without a reason that was his job, and found forty people on a yard apron and a board with a word on it and a nineteen-year-old with his wrist in a splint sitting on a coil in the sunshine arguing with a rigger about the price of canvas.

Nessa Tarrow handed him the slate.

He read the two lines and then he read the second one again, twice, and then he stood in front of a board in Kestrel Yards with his coat open and a tube in it and understood, completely, for the first time in his working life, what the word *second effect* means.

It is not a hidden cost. He had been ready for a hidden cost, and he would have known what to do about a hidden cost, because a hidden cost is a thing you can look for and a thing you can write down afterwards with the hours on it.

This was not a hidden cost. This was a *listed* cost. It was on the board. It was in chalk, in a captain's hand, with the hour on it, and it was published under the heading *not the yard's error*, and it had cost a boy of nineteen his wrist in about a quarter of a second, and the whole of Marek's claim had put the word *corridor* on a sheet and the word *corridor* does not contain a mooring.

Tarin Senn came out of the route office with the launch book under her arm and did not say a single word about it, and did not have to.

"It's on the third line," she said.

"Yes."

"You put it there."

"No," said Marek. "You did."

"That is not what I meant and you know it is not what I meant," said Tarin Senn, "and I am not going to argue about it at eleven in the morning in front of my yard. Go up the hill. Sleep. And Marek —" and she stopped, and looked at him, and then said the thing that was the whole of the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth and a good deal of the month before it: "It held. The corridor held. That is not a small thing and you should not be standing here looking like a man who has been handed a bill. It held."

"Yes," said Marek Vale. "It held."

And that was the whole of what either of them said about the claim, at any time, ever, and it was true, and it was the reason the claim went into the record exactly as it did.
