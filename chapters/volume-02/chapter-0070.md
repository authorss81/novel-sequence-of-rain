# Chapter 70 — A Fair Hand

The reach came down on the Friday morning of the eighteenth day of Drownmonth at about ten past five, and it was down to the mark by twenty past five, and it held until about ten to six, and it was the best morning this basin has had on a reach in nine years, and the ninth ward did not get a flatbed on it and did not try, and about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands stayed in a shed under a painted board with no name on it for a second consecutive season, and that is the sentence, and it is not said again.

There was one man in the water.

He was a barge operator from the north side of the city who had bought the morning of the seventeenth on a photograph on the fifteenth of Drownmonth, and had been refunded in full at three and nine on the Wednesday, on a printed receipt, without argument, and who had come up the reach at half past five in the morning on the strength of a refund because a man cannot help it when the water is off.

There was also a man of about sixty-eight standing on the flat at about ten past six with a chain and a staff across his shoulder, reading a post, because it is his job and because nobody asked him to and because nobody ever has, in about thirty years, on any morning, about anything.

Petrie read it and wrote it in his own level book in the column for the level, and then in the fourth column, in his own hand, badly, and then he shut the book and put it under his arm and stood on a flat with four inches of water in a reach and about three hundred yards of good kerbstone in it, for about eleven minutes, on his own.

**Forty minutes. A flat needs a low flat, a load, nine crew, and a district with a reach window, and there is no window, the load is in a shed under a board with a date and no name on it, and forty minutes is not enough to bring a flatbed round from the shed and load it and get it back up the reach, and no one in this basin has ever had to prove that arithmetic twice.**

He wrote two words in the fourth column and they were *forty min* and he did not write anything else, and the book went back on the shelf in a cold shed a hundred yards from an apron at about half past six that morning, and a door was shut and a chain was put on it.

---

**Sabin Orle had the memorandum, the form, and the line, and she had had them since about ten o'clock, and she sent for him at half past eleven.**

Her room is at the north end of the ground floor of the Council's basin house and it has a desk, two chairs, a window that looks out of the eastern ridge and at nothing else whatsoever, and a clerk.

He was in the chair on her left, which is the wrong side, and it is the wrong side because it puts her on his right, and he has sat on the right-hand side of a bench since before the censure without once saying why, and he did not notice that he had done it until she moved her chair about four minutes in without a word and put herself on his left instead, and he noticed then and did not understand it and did not ask, and it is the only favour anybody in this building has done him since the censure that he knows of and did not ask for.

"Three papers," she said. "I will give them to you in the order they matter, which is not the order they are in."

"Go on."

"The first is a memorandum from a commercial underwriter of the Cloud Exchange, dated the seventeenth of Sleetmonth, in his own hand, containing two figures and an instruction that it never be printed. He has sent it to me with no firm mark on the envelope and one sentence saying it is not a claim and not a complaint. **I ruled it admissible as a document of a party's belief and inadmissible as evidence of any cost, and I told you that on the Thursday in the corridor and you did not seem to think I was joking.**"

"You were not joking."

"The second is a form issued by this Council's technical cell on the third of Drownmonth, in answer to a request of the thirteenth of Sleetmonth, with the heading filled in and the season filled in and nothing whatever underneath it, returned to the firm by a clerk of nineteen with a satchel he bought himself out of four weeks of counter wages."

"And the third."

"The third is a line in a book." She put her hand on it. "It went into the Standing Response Register at about half past ten this morning, entered by a records officer against the ninth ward's column for the season of Drownmonth, and it is the first thing under that column since the season of three hundred and fifty-five, and it came in on a form this Council never sent to anybody, written out by a clerk of a different district who walked it in off the street and did not ask permission, and nobody stopped her because there is nothing to stop her with."

She turned the book round.

It is a fair hand. It is a clerk's hand, twenty years in a ledger, small and even and it does not hurry, and it is not a warden's hand and it is not a district's hand, and it is the first piece of writing anybody in this basin has produced this month that is about what a district can do about its water and is not a price for it.

*Nine men to a flat by nine in the morning and not by six. Six catch pits, a barrow, two wardens, a water steward. A pump house with a hand on it and a second on a call and nobody else may make the call. And the same line: nine hundred pounds of timber in four bands in a shed, an order of the water office's, paid for, and a table on a wall two years and about nine months off.*

"There is no figure in it," said Sabin Orle. "**There is no figure in it at all, and that is the first time since the twenty-first of Sleetmonth that anybody has put a schedule of a ward's responses in front of me, and there has been nothing to pay for.**"

---

"I am going to do the arithmetic now and I am going to do it in front of you and in front of Mr. Peascod here, and it takes about ninety seconds, and I want the ninety seconds minuted."

The clerk took a pen.

"There are three numbers in this basin for forty minutes at the bottom of the Bellweather reach, and I am going to use two of them, and I am going to say why I am not using the third, and I want that in the minute as well."

"The Exchange's published price for a reach window at the autumn low is **seven and six.** The refund on a window not opened is **three and nine**, because the refund rule halves it, and the Exchange has paid that refund three times in one quarter without argument on printed receipts. And the ninth ward's own figure for the same forty minutes, taken from a memorandum written by a twenty-nine-year-old man in pencil on the back of a struck-through line, is **one and four**: six men at the district rate for a day, a gang for the pump and its gear, and nothing at all for the timber and nothing at all for the pump, because the timber and the pump are already bought and paid and their marginal cost of being there is the cost of turning up."

"And neither of those is a cost," said Sabin Orle. "I am going to say that out loud before I use them. The seven and six is a price for a hull, a barge hire, a chain and a gang of four and a foreman, and it is a fair price for a hull, and the hull is not a wall. The one and four is a fair figure for the work and the work is not the loss, because the loss is the year and the year has never been priced by anybody in this basin and I am not going to pretend that a day's gang of men is the price of a year."

"Then the Council is not paying either of them."

"No." She wrote on the margin of her own paper, in the same fair hand, in about twenty seconds, and turned it round.

"Three and nine, which is forty-five pence, is the price of a morning the market did not sell. One and four, which is sixteen pence, is the price of a morning the district did not use. **Add them, and halve them, and take it to the nearest penny downward. That is thirty pence. That is two shillings and sixpence.**"

The clerk wrote it down. Marek Vale looked at it.

"And the third number," said Sabin Orle. "The seven and six, which is the published price, and which I am not using, and I will give my reason for the minute. **Because the published price is a price for the publisher's buyer, and not for the thing.** A price published without a schedule of the use is a price for whoever bought it, and the Exchange's own position in writing is that it cannot compare two prices for two objects, and it is right, and its rightness is why it has no figure for this."

"And the fair hand," said Marek Vale. "The line in the book. You've not used it."

"**I have used it and I could not use it and both of those are true and I want them in the minute.**" Sabin Orle put her hand flat on the open register. "That is the only schedule of a district's available responses that has ever existed in this basin, and it is the only piece of evidence in this room that is about a ward rather than about a purchaser, and it has no number on it anywhere, and I have had it since half past ten this morning and it is the reason the other two figures are admissible in a room at all, because before this morning I could not put a price to anything to do with a district, because I had nothing to attach one to. **That is what an hour and a half of a clerk's work does. It does not give a figure. It makes a figure arguable.**"

---

"And now I am going to rule, and I am going to rule against the only thing in this room I would like to grant, and I am going to do it in the ordinary order of business and I am going to do it quickly so that nobody has to sit with it."

She turned to a fresh page.

"**A compensation schedule requires a claimant.** A claimant requires a claim. A claim requires a sponsor, and only a district may sponsor a claim in this basin, and a claim directs a party while it is in force, and it is spent the moment it is discharged, and this Council has never once treated a loss as a claim, and the reason is not a rule. **A claim is an instrument for preventing something. There is no instrument here for something that has already happened, and I have looked, and I am telling you that I have looked, and the looking is the whole of my ruling.**

"The ninth ward has sponsored two claims. On the twenty-first of Sleetmonth and on the sixteenth of Drownmonth, and it took both, and it is the only party in this basin that has ever spent a possibility on purpose, and both times it wrote the cost into a box in its own words and both times it was correct, and the second of them was discharged four days ago, on the expiry that I put on the same line as the effect, and **a claim that has been discharged is not a claim that can be entered again for the same thing.**

"**So there is no claimant, and the reason there is no claimant is not an injury to anybody and it is the reason I am going to be allowed to do what I am about to do.** There is a figure and there is a schedule and there are two hundred and forty children at that school and the three things do not meet, and I cannot make them meet, and I am not going to pretend that a district which has cost itself something on purpose has a claim for it, because there is no box anywhere in the Charter of Public Notice, or in this building, or in this basin, for that, and I have looked, and the reason there is no box is the reason there was a form with one box on it in a cellar, and the reason there was a form with one box on it is the reason I have a register with a heading and nothing under it for forty-five years, and I am not going to be permitted to pay a district for a decision it made correctly in public with a bell on a wall."

"Orle."

"Mr. Vale, I have not finished." She put the pen down. "I am going to do the fourth thing, and it is the thing that is actually before me, and I have not told either of you what it is, and I am telling you now because I would rather you heard it from me at a desk than in a corridor afterwards, which is what I did on the twenty-first of Sleetmonth, and I was not thanked for that either."

She turned round a fourth sheet of paper.

"The Council's assessor may open a **temporary compensation ledger** where a claimant cannot be identified, on the ground that a document nobody can be produced is not thereby a loss nobody has had. It is temporary. It is provisional. **It expires at the end of the financial year.** It pays a claimant whose figure is unknown, at an interim figure adopted by the assessor, and it is **a ledger and not a fund**, and the difference between those two words is the whole of what I am able to do this month, and I will not have anybody in this room call it anything else, including me, including in the minute.

"And it has a sponsor's line, because a ledger is a public document and a public document in this basin has to have a name at the top of it or it is a piece of paper, and there is exactly one man in this city who may lawfully stand in that line, because a censured reader may not be named in a sponsor's line of a claim and **this is not a claim**, and the whole of my reason for asking you is that it is not a claim, and I would be obliged if you would read the whole of it before you answer me."

---

He read the whole of it. It took him a while, because she did not read it out loud for him, because on the twenty-first of Sleetmonth she had read the sponsor's cost out loud in front of nine people and this one had no room in it, and because a thing you are about to sign is a thing you read twice and slowly and in the wrong order, at the end first.

The clerk, whose name is Peascod and who has been a clerk of the technical cell for nine years, sat with a pen and did not have to move it.

The last sheet ended with a line that said:

*Sponsor (a person who may lawfully stand in this line, the document not being a claim): ___________________*

and underneath, in smaller type, in the assessor's own drafting:

*To be entered only on the assessor's request. To be accompanied, in all cases where the sponsor cannot supply a figure, by a statement that the sponsor could not supply a figure, and the reason.*

"The Office does not pay this," he said.

"The Office does not pay it and the Office has no instrument which looks at a reach and has never had one and does not consider the absence a defect, and I have read that paragraph about ninety times this year and I agree with every word of it, and it is not going to stop me putting your name on a piece of paper in my own building." She looked at him. "**You are the only reader in this basin who has ever been on the ground of a claim's cost, and you were on the ground of both of them, and the ninth ward's warden is going to be paid out of a book with a line in it and the line is going to say *paid from the sum advised by a reader who is not an officer of this Council*, and that is the best sentence this building can produce and it is not good enough and I am going to sign it anyway.**"

"Then I want to do it properly," said Marek Vale, "and properly means one thing, and I am going to say it and you are going to stop me about nine seconds in."

"Go on."

"Two and six is a number I cannot defend to anybody who has not been in this room, including the man it is paid to, and it is going into a book that somebody in a year is going to hold up in a room and say *this is what the Council decided a district's reach was worth*, and there is no way on earth anybody in this basin can check it, **and that is the whole of the Bellweather month and the whole of the thirtieth and it is going to be in a book instead of on a wall, and if I sign that without saying so I am the man who has spent the last three months arguing about it.**

"So I am asking you to draw the line at a figure I can name."

"Mr. Vale, I have just told you there is no such figure."

"I know you have." He put the paper down. "I am asking you to write down that I could not supply one. In the book, on the face of it, in the same register, in the same hand, on the same day. **That the sponsor could not supply a figure and why.** And I am going to give you the why, and I am not writing it myself, and I am going to say whose it is, and I am not asking you to be kind about it."

---

Then he said it, and it is worth setting down exactly, because it is the sentence the last two months were built out of, and it was written in the Bellweather month in a room over a market hall in Hearthward, in one hand without lifting the pen, on a page that the woman who wrote it destroyed from memory afterwards because she is a clerk and not a lawyer and a clerk does not keep a draft of a clause that has been refused.

*That a party may record in writing that the cost of a thing cannot be made public, and the reason why, and that the recording of that is not a refusal to pay and is not an admission of the figure and is not a waiver of any right, and that where the reason is that no schedule of the party's preparation exists, that is itself a finding and is to be minuted as one.*

He said it from memory, in a cold room, on the ground floor of a building he has worked in for eleven years, to a woman who read that clause in a refused application in the Bellweather month and refused it in four minutes on a records ground of her own.

Sabin Orle did not move for about eleven seconds.

"That," she said, "was refused here in the Bellweather month, in writing, by a printed sheet with a manager's name on it, and the sheet is in the file, and the file is on that shelf, and I am the assessor and I did not have to be named on it and I was not." She got up and went and got the file.

She read the printed sheet standing up, and she read it a second time, and she came back and sat down again on the same side of the desk, which was now his left.

"I refused that in four minutes," she said, "on the ground that a schedule of a party's published practice used in a commercial product is not a matter this Council has a duty about, and I was right about the duty and I was wrong about the sheet, and I have not had anybody make me say that in a month, and I am not going to thank you for making me say it."

"I am not asking you to."

"I know you are not, that is what is remarkable about it." She put the paper in front of the clerk. "**Enter this: that the sponsor could not supply a figure, and the reason. Set the figure at two shillings and sixpence as an interim figure adopted by the assessor, and state in the first line, in full, that the claimant's figure is unknown.**"

---

The ledger was opened at about twenty past one on the Friday afternoon of the eighteenth day of Drownmonth in a room on the ground floor of the Council's basin house, at a table, by three people and a clerk.

It is a bound book about two inches thick with a ruled page and a printed head. It is not a fund. It is provisional and temporary and it expires at the end of the financial year, and the assessor said so out loud, twice, and wrote it on the inside cover before anything else went in it, and the first entry in it is nineteen words long and it is the assessor who wrote them.

**The first thing written in the ledger:**

*The first claimant's figure is unknown. The Council pays two shillings and sixpence as an interim figure, because two shillings and sixpence is the only figure in the room, and the reason it is the only figure in the room is that no party in this basin has ever asked a district what it can do about its water.*

**The second thing**, about forty minutes later, in a warden's hand, in a fair hand, without lifting the pen:

*Received. Nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four bands and a four hundred and ten pound dewatering pump are in a shed on the south bank under a painted board with no name on it, and the order is one hundred and fourteen of the water office, and the load is paid for, and the crew of eleven is stood down and paid for the day and a half, and the wall is in its second season, and the water office's table says two years and about nine months. The ninth ward claims nothing further at this date and will say so again in writing if it is asked.*

**The third thing** was made at about ten minutes past two by a copy clerk of twenty-seven in the west end of the records room, in her own hand, dated, timed and initialled, and it is a true copy of the schedule as adopted, and it went into the tray, and the tray went to the technical cell, and that is the end of the procedure and the procedure is correct and always has been.

**Nobody asked her to make it. Nobody in that building thanked her. Nobody in that building has thanked her in seven weeks. And there is no form in the Charter of Public Notice, or in this building, or in this basin, under which a copy clerk's private copy of anything may be produced to anybody at all, and the key to that box has been in her pocket since the third day of the Bellweather month, and it was still in her pocket at half past two on the eighteenth of Drownmonth, and there is nobody in Veyra who could say how many things are in it, including her.**

---

He stayed about nine minutes after the three of them had gone, in a cold room, with a copy of a document in his hand about the length of a chair's back and about four hundred words long, and the permanent costs of a fortnight, and none of them refundable.

**A fee of eleven shillings, in coin, in advance, on a plank, and a retainer of a shilling a week. A barrister's guinea, in shilling notes, on a table on the Nacre Gate, with a receipt. A form with a heading and a season and nothing underneath it, in a boy's satchel, dated in the boy's hand. A memorandum in pencil, sent in an envelope with nothing on it. A shed foreman's question, seventy seconds, in a doorway. A second reader's two corrections, in her hand, with the hours on them, on a shutter. A bleed in the middle of a two-ended read, seen once, put away. A crew of eleven stood down for a day and a half and paid for a day and a half. A reach. A wall, a second season. A table, two years and about nine months.**

**And a signature.**

He had not been on a watch, a roster or a field list since the eleventh of the month before last. He could not sign a reading for that office, could not attest in its service, and could not be named in a sponsor's line of a claim; and on the afternoon of the eighteenth of Drownmonth he put his name at the top of a line in a public book in his own building, in a fair hand, on his own account, and **it was the first thing he had put his name to since the censure that was going to pay somebody.**

Then he did the last thing he did that day, and it took four seconds, and it is the reason the rest of this is going to happen.

He turned the copy over, and on the back of it, in the margin, in his own small hand, he wrote the number and the date and the fact, the way he has written on a docket and a bench sheet and a cuff since the eighth of the Bellweather month.

*Reach, Bellweather, 40 min, Fri 18th, 05:10–05:50, mark showing, no window, no flat, load in shed. F. — read it, wrote it in his own book, told nobody.*

**And a cuff does not have a fourth block, so the next block of pencil in his working life went on the back of a provisional compensation ledger that a man of twenty-nine had caused to be opened, in a hand that is not a small neat hand and is about half a size too large for the ruling, because it was the paper in front of him.**

---

Two lines struck through in one quarter, on a wall in a public room, at the same price, on the same reach. A memorandum in pencil with two figures in it and nobody's schedule. A form from a Council's own technical cell with four lines of fair hand at the top of it and nothing underneath, in a boy's satchel. A lighter's answer in pencil on the back of a Council form on a Sunday morning. A fourth ward that says *this ward has no return of this kind and declines to invent one*, minuted as a refusal, because a Council gave its word on the face of a letter. A schedule in a register, forty-five years late, written on Friday morning by a clerk of another ward and walked in off the street on a form this Council never sent, with two signatures at the bottom and no number on it anywhere.

**And a book that expires at the end of the financial year, with one line in it, and the line says that the first claimant's figure is unknown and that the Council is paying two shillings and sixpence because two shillings and sixpence is the only number in the room.**

Nobody in this basin knows yet who stopped writing to the ninth ward in the season of three hundred and fifty-five, and that is not because it has been hidden. It is because it was a job, and the job had a name on a stub in pencil, and the man went, and the second-writings box under the press has a card on it and is empty, and there was no rule requiring anybody ever to report an empty box to the Board.
