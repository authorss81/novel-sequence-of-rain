# Chapter 53 — What a Window Costs a Crew

There are eleven crews on the Line's name and on a Monday in Sleetmonth the yard is a place where a captain has to be in two places and know which one is lying.

Marek Vale came in at seven with a toolbag and did the service on the copper box on the yard's own bracket, which was a quarterly job and which the Line paid for at the private rate, and he got it down and up again in forty minutes without saying very much, and it was a good job and everybody in the office knew it and nobody said so.

He was working from the right-hand side of the box, and had been since the autumn, and had the lamp on that side, and if you had asked anybody in that yard why, they would have said the light was better there.

At about ten past seven he wanted to know the yard's own figure for the lane's mark on the twenty-second of the Bellweather month, because he was checking a plate he had cut in the autumn against a number in his own hand, and Tarin Senn told him, from the launch book, without turning round: *eleven minutes past eleven, night before the collision, and it was eleven minutes past eleven because the line came in slow and Ovett had the log and I had the mark.*

And Marek said, "Eleven past eleven," which was the number.

And she said it again. "Eleven past eleven."

And he said, "Thank you," and shut the bag.

Nobody in that office said one word about it. Tarin Senn noticed, in the way you notice a thing that is not a sound, and filed it, and did not ask, and the not-asking was not politeness. It was that she had four things on her desk at a quarter past seven on a Monday and a boom cable to price, and because she is a person who pays attention in one place at a time and does not have a spare place.

She thought about it again on the Thursday and did not ask then either.

---

The contract was on her desk by nine and it was a good contract, and she read it three times and the third time was the one that did it.

It was a **forward window contract, Bellweather reach, autumn low.** Four hours, on a stated level, at a stated ebb, priced at eleven pounds four for the window plus a standing retainer of two pounds a month against the instrument. The Exchange would supply, free, for the term, a level instrument and its maintenance: a float, a chain, a drum, a staff, and a seated datum.

Eleven pounds four was more than the Line made in a week on the Ternway run.

And she had eleven hundred pounds short of the quarter, which is not a mood and is not a figure of speech; it is the number by which the yard would not be able to pay itself, on the first of next month, out of what was owed to it, and the boom cable — three hundred pounds of it, put in on the thirtieth of the Bellweather month in about four minutes of somebody else's bad decision, on a lifting boom under a freight apron, in eight feet of reach water — was the item at the top of the list of things that were owed for and not affordable, and it was on the list because the Line had published it.

She had published the cost herself. In chalk. On the yard's own board. Under the heading *not the yard's error*, which is a heading she had insisted on, and which had been read by two underwriters and one man from a northern office and which had cost the Line a discount that nobody had ever sent an invoice for and which had also, in the end, brought her a contract.

There is a kind of accounting that a small yard does, which is that it never quite decides whether the things it publishes are a cost or an advertisement.

---

Ilva Sorn came in at half past ten with the read.

"Six clauses. Four of them are the ordinary ones. Two of them are new and I've marked them." She had the sheet on the desk face down, which she always did, and Tarin turned it over and read, and the two new ones were underlined in her own pencil, and the second new one is the whole of the argument.

**6.** *The Line does not undertake, warrant, or agree that the operation to which this window relates will be performed, or will be safe, or will be practicable. The Line undertakes only that its own published reading will be available to the buyer in the hours named, on the Line's own board, with the hour and date on it, and the buyer is not purchasing weather.*

"Nothing there," said Tarin Senn.

"There's no clause about your crew," said Ilva Sorn.

"There's nothing there at all."

"That is what I'm saying. There is nothing there about any human being. Read clause 3."

**3.** *The buyer may not treat the window as an instruction to any servant, employee, or member of the crew of any vessel, and no such person is a party to this contract, and nothing in this contract may be produced to any such person as an instruction.*

She read that twice.

"He wrote that himself," she said.

"He wrote that himself. In about eleven seconds, Slate says, and then it went to a lawyer and came back with a paragraph about servants and employees in it, which is a lawyer, and the sentence underneath is the man's own." Ilva Sorn sat down on the edge of the desk, which she did not do. "Marek."

"Marek."

"He read the draft the way he reads a plate. He did not touch the fourth clause, which is about the instrument, and he put his finger on the third and said the crew is not a party to it, and then he said, and I am quoting because I wrote it down at the time, *then the line is not a party to the window, and if the line is not a party to it, the line is not covered by it, and Captain Senn would be selling a thing she is not in.*"

Tarin Senn looked at the sheet.

"Say the rest of it."

"There isn't any more. That was the whole of it and he went back to the coil and left."

---

She refused it at about eleven, and it took nine minutes, and she was in the office with Slate on the telephone and Slate was in the yard and neither of them was enjoying it.

"You are the fourth yard to be offered it this month," said Slate. "Two of you have taken it."

"And what do the other two say when the tide comes in wrong?"

"They say the refund is prompt," said Slate. "Which it is."

"Yes, and then their masters come to the wharf in the morning and find their own people in the wrong place, and the yard has published a window, and the yard's name is on the window now — it is on the list, it is on the list in large letters since Tuesday — and the man's lawyer will hold the list up and say *you said this hour was dependable.*"

"The list says no such thing. The list says a window is an undertaking that an operation may be depended upon in the hours named, and the operation is the barge, and the barge is Slate's client's, and the crew is mine, and clause three says so, and you drafted it."

"Clause three says so," said Slate. "And I drafted it because the person who buys a window does not think about the crew, and he pays his premium to not think about the crew, and that is the product. You are not refusing a contract. You are refusing to be the reason a man is allowed not to think about his crew, and I do not think anybody can do that twice in one quarter and stay in business."

"Then that is what I am refusing."

"Captain—"

"I am not refusing for the principle of it," said Tarin Senn, and she heard herself say it and let it stand, because it was true. "If it were the principle I would put it on the board. I am refusing it because the boom cable is three hundred pounds and the yard is eleven hundred short of the quarter and the wrist is Wen Ardery's and I sent him up a lift that should have been a junior's, and if I sign that clause I am selling, on a board in chalk, the right of every person in this yard to be unlucky, and I told the whole yard in the autumn, standing about there, that our four conditions are about the yard's name and not one of them is about our crew. I was right. I would like it minuted that I was right and that it did not help."

---

She took a smaller charter on the Wednesday. It was a coal run to Ternway with a firm she had never flown for and which would have her hull back in the yard in nine days and would pay on the day, and it was dull, and it was not going to cost anybody anything, and it took eleven weeks out of the season that she had been planning to spend on the autumn, and it is the single worst commercial decision of her working life and she made it in nine minutes and would make it again.

The consequence of refusing, in money, inside a month: about four hundred and six pounds. That is the retainer gone, the premium gone, and the difference between the two charters, and Ilva Sorn wrote it on the board copy in a column headed *cost of the refusal* and dated it, and did not show it to anybody for a week and then showed it to everybody.

"You could have had the retainer and the instrument free," Ilva Sorn said, on the Friday.

"I could have had the instrument free anyway. That's the bit I can't get past."

And then, on the Friday afternoon, Slate came out to the yard with a crate.

It was the size of a milk crate and it was on a hand-cart with a strap over it and a waxed cloth on top, and Slate had it up the two steps himself, and Tarin Senn came out of the office to ask what it was, and Slate said:

"It's the instrument. It's yours, for the term, on the retainer, whether you buy the window or not."

"It's the *offer* instrument."

"It's the offer instrument. It's a float and a chain and a drum and a staff and a datum, and the datum is a glass cylinder with a ground flat that a man on the Weatherglass ridge ground twice, and he has written on the docket that he did not anneal it and that the man who did is a Sallow at the yards and that the glass was checked and not made, which is more than any instrument in this Office carries on its own docket, and you can have it free, and I am to say that it costs us nothing to say so, because it is the truth, and because the whole of the instrument's worth to us is not the instrument."

"Then what is it."

"It's that a yard that will not use a level gauge because a firm gave it one is a yard that will be told what the water is doing by a page of a price list," said Ivo Slate, "and every condition on that board of yours says your instruments get published, and that applies to it, and I would like it to apply to it from the day it is installed and not from the day you buy a window from us, because otherwise it is a purchase and not a condition, and you will not have that on your board."

Tarin Senn looked at the crate.

Then she said: "Get Nesbitt to bring the flatbed."

They had it out of the crate on the yard apron in about ten minutes. The well was not cut yet — the well was going to be cut on somebody else's wall, a hundred and forty miles south, and this one was going into the low side of the yard's own slip, on a stand, on a level with a brass plate, and a datum, and a book — and the chain came off the drum and paid out true the first time, because the datum was a length and not a sight, and Ilva Sorn put a finger on it and looked at Tarin Senn and said nothing at all.

The third column on the Line's board had been empty for four days, because the Line's glass and the Office's glass had agreed, and there is no column and no price and no refund for four days of agreement, and nobody in this city is going to be able to sell that, and in about a year somebody is going to try.
