# Chapter 67 — A Shed Is Not A Document

The rule is Petrie's and it is two sentences and he has never explained it to anybody and he has been right about it about four times a week for thirty years.

**A shed keeps a load. A load is not a document. A document is not a shed.**

It is not in a book. It is not on a form. He made it up himself, or it was made up for him by a predecessor who could not read, and he has it in his head and it has never once been wrong, and on the Monday afternoon of the fourteenth day of Drownmonth he applied it four times in about seven hours and it was correct four times, and the fourth time is the reason a reach is going to come down forty minutes' worth on Friday and nobody in this basin will be standing in it.

---

**The first was at about eight in the morning, and it was a young man from the water office with a satchel and an April.**

The water office's works panel schedules money and does not cancel, and anything the district has not done by the year end reverts to the office, and what reverts with it is the material, and about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands is material.

"It's not being done," the young man said. "I want to be fair about that, Mr. Petrie, I'm not asking you about the wall. I'm asking you about the timber."

"You can have the order."

"That's not—"

"You can have the order number, which is one hundred and fourteen, and you can have the freight in the district's account, and you can have me in April, and if you want the timber itself you will come and take it off me and I will help you, and I shall be here and I shall hand it over." Petrie did not stop working. "And I'll tell you what happens if you do."

"You'll hold on to it."

"I'll not hold on to it. You'll have it in an afternoon and there'll be a cart on this apron and that's the end of my trouble." He put the plane down. "**I'm not keeping nine hundred pound of timber. I'm keeping a wall.** The timber's how you shored it. And if you take the timber in April then the wall's not shored a third time, and that's a thing you'll find out in about two years and not in April."

The young man stood in the doorway of a shed with a satchel and did not have anything to say to that at all, and Petrie went back to his plane, and the young man wrote *declined* in a book and then crossed it out and wrote *declined, see D.` and went away, and he was right to do that, and he did it in about four seconds.

---

**The second was at about half past nine, and it was six of the district's own, and it is the one that brought the book out.**

They wanted the level book copied.

"Copy it there," said Petrie, and gave them the shelf, and a stool, and a bit of chalk, and he did not offer them the book, and the reason he did not offer them the book is the second half of the rule and he did not need to say it out loud to anybody.

*The book is the shed's.*

Which is not a legal position and cannot be made into one. It is a position in a shed, and a man who holds it has a right to change his mind any afternoon, and the district's men had been in and out of this shed for two years and had never once been handed the book, and they knew the rule without being told it, and one of them said, "Copy, then," and copied it for an hour and ten minutes, four columns, about nine years of it, and a fifth column in a square bad hand that is not the copying clerk's, which is a keeper's on a tower a hundred and forty miles away, and which nobody in the district office has ever asked to see.

**And they copied a column too**, because the column is in the book and a copy of the book has the column in it, and the column is headed, in Petrie's own hand, thirty years ago, with a rule his predecessor could not have written —

*No work, reason.*

—and it is empty, in every year, for about thirty years.

They copied it because you copy a book.

---

**The third was at about half past eleven, and it was a man in a good coat from a firm, and he had a book of his own and a pencil, and he wanted one thing and wanted it very badly.**

"The board on your door."

"It's a board."

"It's got a date on it. The sixth of the month, and no name." He had the pencil up. "Is that the date the material came on site, or the date the work is programmed, or the date of a programme?"

"It's the day it came on the flat and it stopped raining."

"It's a date."

"It's a day," said Petrie. "There's a difference and it's about two hundred years and I'm the wrong man to explain it to. If you want a date with a name against it you want an order. Order one hundred and fourteen, water office, and the water office don't charge anybody for anything, and you can have it for nothing, and you'll find on it a district and a culvert and a quantity and about a price, and it's a form, and it's the only thing in this shed that's worth having and it isn't worth having to me."

The man wrote two words in his book. Petrie could not see them and did not ask, and the two words were *no name*, and they went into a forward board's margin, and they were correct.

---

**And the fourth was at about two in the afternoon, and it came from a young man with a camera and a notebook and a card from a licensed agent, and it is the reason a reach at the bottom of the Bellweather is about to be sold twice on a picture of itself.**

"Mr. Petrie. I'm instructed to ask whether the flat would be available for a photograph on the morning of the seventeenth, for the board, and I'm not asking for anything to be moved and I'm not asking for anybody to be in it. It's an hour. It's a picture of the water off the reach, with the mark in shot if you can stand a board by the post, and the board carries the line and the figure and the hour, and the figure would be the price, and the price is your price and the board's your board."

He had a printed card out and it was very good and it cost the firm about a shilling to print.

"No," said Petrie.

"Mr. Petrie—"

"Sit down, son, because I'll say it slow." He put the plane down, which he did not do for people. "**The day it's on a sheet of paper with a number on it, it's a cost. And the minute it's a cost there's a man somewhere wanted it cheaper, and then somebody comes down this road with a form.** That's it. That's the whole of it. I don't mind a photograph. I've had men photograph this shed before the war for a catalogue. I mind a number."

"It's an hour of a man with a camera—"

"It's forty minutes of a reach and a price on it, and you know it is, and you didn't say forty minutes because you thought I'd not been in this basin thirty years." He looked at the camera. "And you'll take it off the apron if I've a mind to, because the apron's public and there's nine hundred pound of timber on it with a district's order against it and no name on the board, and I'll tell you the two things I *will* do about it. I'll stand there. And I'll watch you. And you'll get a picture of a wall and a man and a tide, and there's four inches of mud over a mark cut in a post by men that are dead, and I should be very surprised if a lens could find it at forty yards, and if it can, that is between your glass and my basin and I never said it could be done."

Doak, the municipal apron man, who had been on that apron since five in the afternoon on the thirtieth of the Bellweather month and had gone into the water to his knees at half past three on the thirtieth of the Bellweather month and come back up on his hands, was standing about eight feet away with a barrow and he said:

"He's right, and I'll tell you the half of it you're not being told, which is that a picture's a document. A catalogue's a document and nobody's ever asked a shed to explain a catalogue. This apron has four photographs of that mark on it now and they were taken off the public road by a man with a camera for a county survey in 'fifty-one, and the survey's in a library, and I have read it, and the mark in it is the mark, and I checked it against the post with a chain and a staff in the spring and it is the same mark to the inch." He shouldered the barrow. "**Photographs are the only documents anybody in this basin makes without having to sign anything.** And they're the only ones anybody can sell."

The young man said, "I'll take it off the public road, then, and there'll be no shed in it."

"You will," said Petrie. "And there'll be no name in it either, and you'll not know what year the timber came, and you'll not be able to say whether that mark was off the post in October or in March, and you'll print a line on a board and the line will be true and it will be useless, and in a year somebody will price that reach off your line and they will not be the least bit wrong to."

---

**And then at about a quarter past three the young man did it, in about eleven minutes, from the public apron wall, and nobody in this shed could stop one bit of it.**

He walked out. He took four photographs of the flat with the water off it and the gauge in the wall and the mark post. He asked a barge operator who was tied up at the apron wall to hold a hand against the reach's edge for scale, and the barge operator did it, and laughed, and asked whether he wanted him in the water, and the young man said not today.

And then he asked the barge operator's mate what the reach had done on the twenty-third of Sleetmonth, and the mate said it hadn't come down, and the young man wrote it down, and asked *any particular reason*, and the mate said, *It was held up. Somebody paid for it not coming down, ask the water office*, and the young man wrote that down too, and Petrie heard every word of it from inside the shed with the door open, standing at a bench, and did not say a word.

Then he crossed the flat to get the low angle he wanted, and about forty minutes out from the wall he stepped on a plank the district had put down over the third block's outfall in the first week of the wet quarter, and it did not give, and he looked down at it and then looked across at the shed.

"**That's the outfall plank, son**," said Petrie.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

---

At about half past three the district's copy came back on a cart with a book in it, and a woman got off the cart and came into the shed and stood in the doorway, and she was the reason a column with a heading on it has one line in it.

**Neris Kade had written in the ninth ward's copy of a shed's level book, in the ward's copy and in a warden's hand, the first entry that column has had in about thirty years**, and she had done it on the twenty-third of Sleetmonth in a cold room over a locker's yard because she wanted a line somewhere in the basin that said a repair was not done and why, and it was not compellable and it was worth nothing and it was the only one of its kind, and she had brought the copy back because a copy goes back.

She put it on the bench and opened it at the week.

"He'd have to do it again," she said. "In his own book, not a copy. That's what I wanted. That's the whole of what I wanted and I did not ask him and I am not going to."

"Ma'am."

"I'm not asking you. I've not asked you for anything and I'm not going to, and if you never write in that column again in your life that's the right answer, and I have got mine, and it's in a copy on a cart and it's a district document and it goes in a file and it is worth nothing and I knew that when I wrote it and I wrote it at about eleven o'clock at night with the ward sitting in a room wondering what it had done."

Petrie looked at the column for a while.

"It's a good column," he said.

"That's it," said Neris Kade. "That's the whole of what I came to hear and I'll be going."

And Petrie said: "Mrs. Kade."

She stopped in the doorway.

And this is the thing, and it is about seventy seconds long, and it is the only question this man has asked anybody in about thirty years, and he had decided inside about a second and a half of the doorway and he had no idea at all that he was going to do it.

"**What happened on the thirtieth.**"

---

There were four answers available inside four seconds and he knew it, and he had made a rule thirty years ago about not knowing, and the rule was the best thing he owned and it had never once been tested.

She did not answer with a name. She did not answer with a form, and there was no form in that shed and there had not been since the thirtieth of the Bellweather month, and he had looked for one every morning since the way you touch a wall on the way past it.

She said, and she said it in the plainest words she had, and she said it to a man with a plane in his hand and a chain and a staff on a shelf and a level book open on a bench:

"**We stopped it.**"

She did not say who. She did not say how, and there was nothing in that shed about how, and there never had been anything in that shed about how, and if there had been it would have been a thing on a form and a form is a document and a document is not a shed.

"Aye," said Petrie. "Well."

And that was the whole of the conversation about the thirtieth of the Bellweather month, and it lasted about seventy seconds, and it is not evidence of anything, and it is not compellable, and it is not in any column, and **it is the only sentence anybody has said on the subject since the thirtieth of the Bellweather month, and it is in three words, and it is in the only vocabulary a shed foreman and a warden have between them.**

---

**He wrote one thing down that evening, in his own book, in the correct column, in his own hand, and it is the first entry in about thirty years and it will be the last one anybody puts there this season.**

*Reach asked for by three parties this week. Sold to none. No work, because nobody has anything I can sell.*

He looked at it, and then he put the book on the shelf where it has been for thirty years, in the cold, in a corrugated shed, a hundred yards from an apron, with a door and a chain on the door and a plank with white paint on it that says a date and no name.

**The wall is in its second consecutive season. The front is in two years and about eleven months, which is a Friday, and it is a table on a wall at the Council's basin house and not a threat, and he will say so to anybody who asks, and the man with the card has gone out onto a public apron wall with a camera in about an hour, and there is not one thing in this shed that can stop him, and the rule in here was never going to, and the rule is still correct, and that is the worst of it.**
