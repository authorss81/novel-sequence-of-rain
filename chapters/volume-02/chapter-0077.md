# Chapter 77 — Four Paragraphs

It was pinned to the bench in the maintenance shop on the Weatherglass ridge at about a quarter past eight on the Sunday morning of the twenty-seventh day of Drownmonth, by a woman of sixty-one who had been a foreman for nineteen years, and there is a pot of tea on the bench in the shop at all times and it was on, and she poured a cup before she read it, which is what she does.

**Circular 15. Windows, Levels, and Instruments of Record.**

*1. Circular 14 is not withdrawn, and the position stated in it — that a window is not a claim, and that a window has no place, no trigger, no witness and no entry, and is therefore outside the authority of this Office — is unchanged.*

*2. A level is not a sequence. A figure of depth, however obtained, however published, and by whomever, is evidence of the publisher's belief and not of the water. The Office has not at any time treated a number of inches as a reading of the order of the movements, and does not do so now.*

*3. This Office has no instrument which looks at a river or a reach, has never had one, will not build one, and does not consider the absence of such an instrument a defect in its work. Further: the Office will not cut, grind, anneal or supply a sequence plate for any party other than a ward tower; and will not permit office plate stock, office furnaces, or the services of any officer of this Office to be used for that purpose by any other party. A sequence plate is an instrument of record. A plate in a private hand is a reading that no public body can check, and this Office is not able to check it and will not pretend to.*

*4. Where a figure published by one party has been used in another party's product, this Office has no duty, no instrument, and no opinion. The use of a published figure by a party that had it and did not ask whether it might be used is a matter between the two parties.*

*C. ROOK, Director. — sealed and issued the twenty-sixth day of Drownmonth.*

Ilse Doff read all four paragraphs twice with her cup in her hand and then put the sheet flat on the bench and squared it, because she squares everything, and then she said the thing that was in her mouth before she got to it, which was not what she expected either.

"Third one's cheap."

---

**And then she stood there for a while with her hands on the bench, and the shop was shut because it is a Sunday, and there was a lehr that has been out since 'fifty-nine and a rack of thirty-one plates and about four hundred weight of cullet in a bin by the yard, and she thought about the sentence in paragraph three for a good deal longer than she thought she was going to.**

The first thing she did was to go and get the rack key, which she keeps in her apron and which nobody has ever asked her for, and to count the plates, and that is the first Monday of a month's habit and it is not a Sunday habit, and she found herself doing it, and it came out at thirty-one, and the work book said thirty-one, and she shut the door on the rack again.

**The second thing she did was to notice that she had not read paragraph four properly, and to read it again, and to work out that paragraph four is the one about her.**

*Where a figure published by one party has been used in another party's product, this Office has no duty, no instrument, and no opinion. The use of a published figure by a party that had it and did not ask whether it might be used is a matter between the two parties.*

"**He's put the ward in it**," said Ilse Doff. "Not by name. He's put the *use* of it in it, and he has done it in a way that is perfectly correct, and he has done it about a district with a dark glass that cannot read the sheet it is on and a council on the ground floor that will not read the register that names it, and the sentence is four lines long and it puts the whole of the trouble in the same place it has been in since the autumn, which is *somebody else decide*."

She thought about paragraph four for a while, on the Sunday, on her own, in a shop, with the kettle on the second time.

---

**A foreman of nineteen years in a glass shop is not a public officer and cannot be blamed in the ordinary way and knows a very great deal about the cost of glass.**

She had been taken over from a man who took it over from a man, and there were eleven people under her in the shop in 1968 and there are six now, and the shop cuts and grinds and anneals and beds and does the Office's tower work, and it holds in a rack the plates for the nine ward towers, and it holds a furnace, and it holds a lehr that has been out for two of those six since 'fifty-nine.

"There is no sequence glass in this basin outside this building and two towers and a coat," she said, to nobody, at about half past eight on a Sunday. "**And a Director has just written down in print that none of it is for sale.**"

And here is the part she thought about afterwards, and which is the part that made her copy it.

She was not frightened of paragraph two. Paragraph two was correct and it was a thing a foreman had said out loud in a shop about four times a year for nineteen years, and it had never once been in a circular, and she was not the sort of woman who is frightened of a thing that has been true in her own shop.

She was frightened of paragraph three, and she was frightened of the shape of it and not of the content, and she worked out why at about ten past nine on the Sunday morning and it took her two cups of tea.

**He did not have to do it.** That is what was in the sheet. A Director of the Weather Office may say *a level is not a sequence*, and may stop there, and has stopped there for four paragraphs in a second circular this month, and every word of it is true and every word of it can be checked by anybody.

Paragraph three was a *closing*. Not a law, not a rule, not a prohibition with a penalty: **a refusal, addressed to himself and to his successors, to do a thing he has the power to do and could have left alone.** And in about nine days it would be true, and then every sequence plate in this basin would be in a tower or in a coat, and no fire and no firm and no private person in the city could ever again obtain one out of office stock, and there would be nothing to appeal to and nothing to test and nothing to argue with.

"**There's no appeal in it," said Ilse Doff, to the shop. "There's no minute, no counter-signature, no objection period. A man can close a trade in glass with a seal and a date and never so much as send for a form.**"

And she sat with that for a while, and then she thought: *and there never was an appeal in it, in anything. That's the other end of the same corridor. That's the whole reason a district can't get a glass pane fitted in two years.*

---

**She has been watching men in coats do the decent thing for nineteen years, and the reason it is worth saying is that she has watched about sixty of them, and the tally she keeps is not a compliment.**

They come into a shop on the ridge and they want one plate, out of a tray, cut for them, ground for them, and about nineteen times in twenty years the plate has been in a tower and the man's coat has been off. A man in a coat on a Sunday is not in a great hurry, and they have all been in a hurry, and about half of them have paid.

She was asked, in the last two years, three times to cut a plate for money. Once by a firm, in an office in the west end, through a clerk who had a card. Once by a man from a photograph business who had money and no trade. And once, in the autumn, by a reader who came in on a Saturday with nine inches of Office stock in a piece of newspaper and a signature on a docket, and she did that one herself, and did not ask, and has never charged anybody for anything since.

**And there is a thing in the shop that she has not thought about for about a fortnight, and she thought about it on the Sunday morning, and it is not in the shop and it is not hers.**

---

**What she did with the circular was this, and it took eleven minutes, and it is why the third uncompellable book in this basin exists.**

She went and got the shop's day book. It is a foolscap book with a printed head, a ruled page, and a column for the day, the hour, the work, the plate's serial, and the lehr, and it is a working record and it is a foreman's book and it is a shop's private paper, and **no court, no council, no office, and no newspaper has ever sent for a shop's day book, because a shop's day book is a record of work done and not a record of anything that happened.**

It is not a record. A keeper's book is not a record. There is nothing a foreman keeps that anybody may have.

She copied the whole of Circular 15 into it in her own hand, in full, four paragraphs and the seal and the date of issue, and she wrote at the head of the entry:

*Sun. 27th. Cir. 15 came in on the pin, sealed, dated the 26th.*

*Copied here entire. J. Doff, f.*

And then she wrote three more lines, which are the only lines in that book in forty years that are not about glass.

*Par. 3 closes a trade. No appeal, no counter-signature, no form. I have watched about sixty men in coats do the decent thing in this shop in nineteen years and I have no way whatever of telling which of them would have, and none of them ever asked me.*

*Par. 4 is about a district and it is the only paragraph anybody in this building has ever written that says a district is to blame for what somebody did with its paper. I am not sure he means it to be kind and I am not going to go and ask him.*

*Entering it because a day book is not a record and this is the only place in this building where a thing that was done to us is written down at the time it was done to us, and if it is not here it is not anywhere.*

She wrote a fourth line and then stopped, and read it, and then crossed it out, and left the crossing-out in, because a foreman's day book is not a record and if it is going to say something then it may as well say that it nearly did not.

~~*I am going to write to the ward about this and I do not know the ward's address and it is four miles down a hill.*~~

*G. Ollis keeps a tower book, a mile and a half up the hill, and a shed foreman on the south bank keeps a level book that goes back to 1888. Three books in this basin. None of them is compellable. Nobody has ever asked for one.*

She filed it under glass with the lehr log, because that is where the shop's papers go, and she shut the drawer, and then she went to the rack of tower plates and counted them, the way she does on the first Monday of a month.

And then she stood in front of the rack for a moment longer than counting takes, because that is the thing about the rack, and it is the thing she wrote at the foot of the day book entry and did not write in the day's column.

**And she could not tell you where any one of them is.** Thirty-one plates of office glass, cut and ground and annealed for nine towers, and a hundred and forty years of day books saying who took which out of which rack and when, and there is not one of them in this building, and there is not a form for asking, and no clerk upstairs could produce one of them if a Director asked her this afternoon, and she has never once tested that and is not going to start on a Sunday.

She washed her cup, put it on the rack, and turned the light off over the bench, and left the circular on the pin, because a circular is a notice and a notice is the one thing in that building which belongs to somebody else, and then she went out and shut the shop and stood in the yard for a moment in the rain.

The yard is forty feet by twenty and there is a gate in it that is not locked on a Sunday and has not been locked since about 1961, and there is a bench, and a lehr house with a stove pipe in it that has not been used since 'fifty-nine, and about four hundred weight of cullet in a bin that a man comes for on the first of every month, and there is nothing else in it at all.

**And there are about nine people in this city who could be harmed by paragraph three, and six of them do not know it. The seventh came into this shop on a Saturday in the autumn with nine inches of glass in a newspaper and a signature on a docket, and she did that one herself, and did not ask, and has never charged anybody for anything since, and he has been a censured reader ever since and he is not going to hear about it from a circular.**

That is the whole of what a foreman can do with a circular, and she did it, and it is eleven minutes out of the forty years.

"Right," said Ilse Doff, to a yard. "**Now. What was I doing before Sunday.**"
