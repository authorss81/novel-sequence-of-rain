# Chapter 56 — Two Deliveries a Day

Gant Ollis had kept the ninth ward tower for eleven years and he had been sixty-three or sixty-four for most of that and had stopped correcting people about it, because correcting people about it is a thing you do twice and then you are a man who cannot remember his own age, and there is a great deal of that in this city already.

The tower is on the corner of Tannery Walk and Sump Lane and it is the second shortest of the nine. It was built for a signal pane to be read from the low ground, which is the entire purpose of it, and from the top of it on a clear day you can see the lake and the yard and, on a very clear day in the right season, the pale edge of the Nacre Range.

The work of the tower is not the frame. The frame is the bit everybody looks at.

The work is the feed.

The feed comes up out of the ground on a copper riser and goes into a seat on the second landing, and in the seat there is a plate: a working sequence plate, nine inches square, of the class the Office buys by the sheet and grinds to the thickness of a holder and seats so that it renders what arrives. It renders a trace. It shows the order of the movements and nothing about the weather's intentions. The plate in that seat was cut out of Office stock on a Wednesday afternoon in the Bellweather month by a maintenance hand on this ridge, fitted, and put a knuckle against it and passed in nine minutes. It came out of the seat on the Friday morning of the same month at ten past six, carried down a ladder in a sailcloth by a keeper who was told to ask nothing, and went back up at ten that night in the dark and was put in cold, and nothing was written about it in the tower's book, and it has been in that seat every night since.

It is good glass. It runs clean. Gant Ollis had seven days and ten hours of run through the collision on it and had gone over the leg twice with a lens and had found no fault of any kind, and he had written *clean* in the column for the leg, which is a word he has written eleven thousand times.

Above him, on the outside of the tower, at the height a person standing in the fourth block has to look up to see, was the public frame.

It was dark.

It had been dark since the autumn, when the permanent pane in it cracked on the frame side and came out, and what had gone up in its place was a bubbled half-size temporary that can carry a state for about a week before the bubbles break the line, and which cannot be read at four hundred yards in daylight by anybody. The surround round it was brass, cut and threaded and bedded by hand in a cold week in a wet autumn by a maintenance hand in a work coat, and it was very good work, and it was a permanent frame around a temporary, and it was going to be there for as long as the district could not afford a pane.

The ward read its weather off a board at the Weatherglass. It was copied by hand, four times a day, by four women, and put up in a copy on a shutter at the district office and on a second shutter at the Sump Lane school and on a nail in the tobacconist's and on a fourth that lived on Sergeant Ampow's kitchen table, and if a person wanted to know what the Office thought was going to happen in the ninth ward they could come and stand in front of a piece of paper with four different hands' worth of copying on it and a signature at the bottom that was not a signature but a rubber stamp.

---

The Exchange's sheet came twice a day.

It came on the morning carrier at about a quarter to eight and again on the afternoon one at about four, and it was not a poster and it was not a handbill. It was a folded sheet of the same stock as the yard boards, printed in two columns, and it was left at a counter in a shop, and anybody could take it, and there was no charge and no condition and nobody asked who took it.

**CLOUD EXCHANGE · DISTRICT SHEET · NINTH WARD.**
**Nine items. The first four are this morning. The rest are the season.**

**1. Nothing to make a decision on before noon.**
**2. The low lane will run from about two. About an inch on the low side. No pump wanted.**
**3. Bellweather reach: level falling about nine inches in twelve hours. Not at the sill. Not usable.**
**4. Office band for tomorrow's watch: steady rain, no heavy turn.**
**5. The reach window that was quoted and not opened on the thirtieth of the Bellweather month was withdrawn and the price is published. It was one pound. See the wall.**
**6. Our figures for this ward are published in the Exchange's forward list. The ward's own quarterly return is on file and can be had at the ordinary price.**
**7. Corrected this morning. Corrected yesterday. See the mark.**
**Basis of 3: our own level instrument at the south bank, read four times a day, and the wall behind the chandler's, and our readers.**
**Basis of 4: the Office's published band, which is the Office's and is not ours.**

Item two is the one that made the ward stop arguing.

It said how much and it said when and it said *no pump wanted*, and the Office's own bulletin on that morning had said *low ground, watch the drains* in the same words it has said every morning for four years, and there is no number in it and there is no hour in it and there is not one word in it about how much or when or what a warden ought to do about it.

The Exchange had a float in a wall and a glass datum cut by a man on the ridge, and it printed what it knew, and it printed *what it was based on* underneath, and it printed *this is the Office's and is not ours* on the one line that wasn't its own.

That last part is the part that took Gant Ollis about a fortnight to understand properly, and he never did get it fully, and he never said a word about it to anybody.

**Nobody had ever printed a basis before.** That was the thing he kept coming back to. In eleven years of towers he had never seen a notice say where it came from. The district's four women copied the Office's bulletins faithfully for years and the bulletins had never once said how the Office knew. And here was a firm with a counter in a shop, printing, free, twice a day, the instrument it used and the number it had and the fact that item four was somebody else's.

He asked the tobacconist about it, on the second morning, which was a mistake, because the tobacconist was Mrs Teodora Bain and she had been asked in exactly the same way by four other people and had already worked it out.

"They're not being kind," she said. "They can't be. It's a business."

"They're not charging for it."

"No," said Mrs Bain. "And I'll tell you what I told the other four, which is that a district that can't read its own notice can't buy anything off anybody, and a district that can't buy anything off anybody is a district nobody has to pay anything, and I'd not say a word against them, and I'd not spend a minute's thought on a tobacconist's counter on whether they're kind, because they printed the word *not ours* on the one line that isn't theirs, and that's not kindness. Kindness is charging for it."

---

He had a conversation with himself about the frame on the second landing at about two in the afternoon on the Wednesday, and it went like this, and it took about a minute and a half.

There was a maintenance hand in this city who had cut glass for him, and had fitted the plate in the seat, and the plate was good. There was a maintenance hand in this city who had bedded that brass, and the brass was good, and it was very obviously the work of somebody who had looked at a temporary in a frame and decided that the temporary was going to be there for a while and that the frame deserved better than it was getting.

Both of those men were the same man. Ollis had worked that out in about a second and a half, in the autumn, and had not written it down, and had not asked, and would not ask now.

Gant Ollis had not asked for eleven years, through one keeper before him and a decade of a bell he could not hear from the ground. He had a rule about it. The rule was that a keeper's business is the feed and the run sheets and the level, and glass is a matter for the district, and the district sends a docket and the district pays for it out of a line item that has not covered a man and a crate sent forty miles in two years.

He had a second rule, which was newer, and the second rule was: *he would ask when it was something he could do something about.*

He stood on a ladder at half past two on the Wednesday and established that it was not. There is no form for a pane. He had looked. There had been one form in eleven years, for the cracked permanent, and the district had filled it in and paid for it, and the pane had come by cart as far as the yard and had sat in a glassblower's shop ever since, and the district's line item had never covered the carriage, and there was no second form and no third form, and the pane was not late. It was somewhere else, and there was no hand in it, and there was nobody to be angry at, and there was not going to be anybody to be angry at in three years' time either when the front came back and the wall was shored a third time out of other timber or not shored.

He came down the ladder.

He came down the ladder.

---

Here is the thing about a keeper, and it is the only thing a keeper is, and it is why the nine towers are the only weather instruments in this basin that anybody can hold you to.

A keeper's book is a column with a date at the head of every entry.

It is not a record and it is not compellable and it is not evidence of anything and it is not in any index, and no council has ever asked for it, and the Office has never once in ninety years asked to see it. It is four columns: the hour, the level at the well, the state of the feed, and what the sky was doing, in the keeper's own words, badly.

Ollis's own entries begin in his first year. About a decade of them are not his at all, because Oren Vale sat at that bench and filled that column before him for a ward that had no money, and the figures are in Oren's hand, and there are a hundred yards of level in that book that a man in a cap worked out with a chain and a staff and copied in because nobody ever sent a book up a hill to ask him for it.

Ollis had never copied those entries and would not.

But he had a new column, because on the nineteenth day of Sleetmonth he had a sheet coming twice a day to a counter in a shop, and it had a number on it, and the number was in inches, and inches are what his own column is in.

He made a fifth column. He dated it. He wrote at the head of it, in his own hand, which is a small square hand and not a good one:

**Exchange sheet, 4 times a day. Compared with this column.**

And for three days he filled it in, and nobody asked him to, and it cost the district and the Exchange and the Office nothing at all.

The Exchange's figure and the tower's figure are not the same measurement and he knew it in about a minute. His well is on the tower and it is a rain gauge, and it says how much fell on the ninth ward's high ground. The Exchange's instrument is in a wall a hundred and forty miles south and it says how much is standing in a bucket. They are not the same quantity and comparing them is the kind of thing a young man does.

What Ollis compared was the fourth item, and the reason he compared it is that item four was the Office's own band, printed on a private sheet, and the Office's band for the ward and the Office's band on the ridge are the same band, and he knew the ridge's from the copy on the district office shutter, which Mrs Ivy Tranter had brought up at about eight that morning along with a pot of tea in a cloth, because she has done so for four years, and he has never once said thank you properly.

So: the Exchange was publishing the Office's band, unchanged, and calling it theirs in one column and the Office's in another, and Ollis set them side by side, in his own book, in his own column, at their own times, for three days.

They agreed. All three days. Four times a day, twelve comparisons, twelve agreements, to the inch and to the quarter of an hour.

And then on the third afternoon the Exchange's item three did not sit with the tower's own figure.

Not by much. The Exchange's south bank said the reach was falling about nine inches in twelve hours. Ollis's own well said the ninth ward's high ground had taken four-tenths of an inch since eight that morning, which is not the same quantity at all, and which is entirely consistent with a falling reach, and which is the kind of thing you can argue about for an hour and get nowhere.

What he did was go down and look at the Exchange's own correction mark on the sheet, which was item seven — *corrected this morning, corrected yesterday* — and he stood in front of a tobacconist's counter for about ten minutes and worked out, with the top of his head, that the Exchange was printing its own errors on the sheet, in a line of its own, with a date, and had not been asked to by anybody.

He wrote in his own book, in the fifth column, in his own hand:

*Wed. Item 3 not consistent with this well. I cannot say it is wrong; the wells are not the same water. But the sheet carries a line of its own corrections, dated, and nobody made them do it. Put the two in the book and let the book say it.*

And he did not send it anywhere. He wrote it in a keeper's book on a tower in a ward whose public glass has been dark since the autumn, and the book is not a record, and he is not a public officer, and nobody in this city has ever asked a keeper for a book and got one.

It is the first independent check on a private weather product anybody in this basin has ever made, and it was made for free, by a man of sixty-three or sixty-four, in a room the height of a table, on a subject he had no standing in whatsoever, and it is the only reason anybody will later be able to say, truthfully, that the Exchange's reach figures were good to a quarter of an inch and that the ward's warden was right to believe them.

Three days later he sent Mr. Sallow a note at the freight apron, which is a hundred and forty miles down the hill road, and it was two lines.

*Your float rides about a sixteenth high on the ebb. Three days running against my own column, which is rain and not level, so I may be wrong. It may be the chain. It may be me. I am sixty-three and I have a chair.*

Sallow came out on the Friday, looked at the datum for nine minutes without touching it, and then said, "It is the chain," and went away, and came back on the Sunday with a shackle.

He told Gant Ollis one thing on the Sunday while he was at it, and he told it without any particular weight, in the way of a man who makes instruments and does not think of them as news.

"The Office has one of these. Not on a river. The one in the west tower is on a reservoir and it's the same instrument and it was made by a man in my trade in the year my father was alive, and I have never once in my life been asked to make one for them and I have never once in my life been told they had one." Sallow packed his tools. "That's all. It's a shame."

Gant Ollis thought about that on the ladder that evening, and the frame above him was dark, and below him four hundred and forty people were going about an ordinary Wednesday, and a tobacconist on Tannery Walk was giving away the weather twice a day for nothing, and none of the three of them had any idea what any of the other two was worth.
