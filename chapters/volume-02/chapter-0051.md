# Chapter 51 — The Ward's Only Document

Low Spindle does not have a weather. It has a condition.

There are four hundred and forty people in the lowest four blocks and the ground under them is a bucket that the rest of the city empties into, and the bucket has been full in one direction or the other twice in every year that anybody now living can remember, and the difference between a wet Tuesday in Sleetmonth and a disaster is not the amount of water. It is whether anybody in the ward knows, four hours before it arrives, which four hundred people are going to be standing in it.

That is the whole job. Nobody pays for it and it is not a job anybody has.

The rain came in at about four on the Wednesday afternoon, out of the west, ordinary, and it did what an ordinary first rain of the quarter does: it found the low lane in twenty minutes, it found the fourth block's gutters in half an hour, and it put about two inches into the drains of the third block and stopped, because the third block's drains go to the second pump house and the second pump house has a pump in it.

Two wardens. One barrow. The clearing rota's Thursday had been moved to the Wednesday because the water steward told the foreman to, on the ordinary grounds that you do not run a rota in a rain, and the foreman had run it at about half past four with six men and the water steward, and it took them until about twenty to seven to clear the six catch pits on the third block's low side and the two grates on the lane, and the low lane was running clean at nine o'clock and the ground floors of the fourth block had never been wet at all.

Nothing happened. That is the report. *Twelve to four, nothing. Four to nine, rain, ordinary. Rotated the Thursday clearing to the Wednesday. Low lane clear at nine. Six on the gang and the steward. No cartloads, no undercroft, nobody in a doorway.*

There is no column anywhere in the world for that. It is not in a return, it is not in the Water Office's front-return table, it will not be in any ledger, and no insurer in this basin would take it as a document if it were printed. Neris Kade has known that for four years and has gone on writing the wet notebooks, because the wet notebooks are how a warden knows what her ward did, and she is the only one who will ever know it.

What she could not do on that Wednesday was enjoy it, and the reason was in a drawer in the district office, and it had been there since eleven o'clock in the morning.

---

At eleven, a councillor called Bram Slee came down to the district office with a piece of paper in his hand and asked whether Neris Kade had seen the invoice.

He was fifty-eight and he had been a Low Spindle councillor for thirty-one years, and he had a district council pension and a shop on Tannery Walk and eleven shillings' worth of something in his coat pocket that was plainly making him uncomfortable, and he stood in the middle of the office floor and held the paper out at her like a man offering a bill.

"There's no harm in it," he said. "That's what the clerk said."

"Show me."

It was a receipt. Eleven shillings. Issued by the Cloud Exchange's district research desk on the second of the month, for one copy of *Ninth Ward — clearing rota, shelter rota and pump order, quarterly return, season of Sleetmonth*, being a true copy of a true return received by the ninth ward's warden's council. Eighteen pages. The price was the ordinary price for a public document of that size, which is eleven shillings, and the Exchange had paid it, and had paid it promptly, and had paid it in advance of asking.

"It's a return," said Bram Slee. "It's not a secret. It's a *return*. It goes to the council four times a year and it's on a shelf."

"It's the ward's rain."

"It's the ward's rain and it's on a shelf in my house in a drawer with the rates in it." He put his hand up before she could speak, because he had been a councillor for thirty-one years and had had this conversation with a field officer before. "Now. Listen. I voted for it."

"You voted for it."

"I voted for it three to two and I voted for it because I have been thirty-one years in this ward and I can tell you the exact sum of money this ward has had from the Weather Office for glasswork in eleven years, which is nothing, and the exact sum it has had from anybody for anything, and the sum from the storm levy, and I will not say those numbers out loud in a room in case a clerk is writing. Eleven shillings is not a scandal. Eleven shillings is what a body of eleven hundred people pays to make its own rain into a document somebody can buy. That's what happens when you're a body of eleven hundred people."

"Three to two," said Neris Kade. "Who was in the room."

"Ampow and Quill and me and two of the Orrells. Five. It was a form, it was a form about routine returns and district research and it came round with the other forms in the ordinary post and there was nothing in it about anybody's rain, and if there had been anything in it about anybody's rain I would have read it out, and I'd have read it out in the meeting and not in a paper, and I'm not having you tell me I did it in a corridor."

"You were not in the room at four o'clock," said Neris.

"I am a councillor, and the council sits at four, and you are a field officer on a pump, and you did not come, and I would have had you called if I had thought for one second that there was a question, and I have stood in a hall and watched a man in a good coat take eleven hundred people's rain and turn it into a schedule and put it on a wall, and I did not call you, and I will take that, and I will take it before the council, and I would take it now if you wanted."

Neris Kade took the paper and read the eighteen pages, standing up, in about ninety seconds, and she read them the way she reads everything, which is fast and once.

Page one was the clearing rota. Four slots, named, with days.

Page two was the shelter rota, and it was one line, and the one line said: *low-ground shelter rota maintained by the district; occupants as the district's own list; no public schedule is maintained.*

Page three was the pump order, with the two pump houses and the times.

Pages four to eighteen were the water steward's figures, and the water steward's figures were the best-kept record in the ward and were Marda Loon's, and Marda Loon did not know that they were in a market.

And the whole of the eighteen pages said, in ordinary language, in the plainest possible form:

**This is what four hundred and forty people do about rain, and when, and how long it takes them, and what they will not do.**

"There," said Bram Slee, from the door. "Now you've read it. Say the thing."

Neris Kade put the paper down on the desk, square, with the top edge parallel to the edge of the desk, because she had been trained in a small office by a woman who cared about that.

"I'm not saying anything about the eleven shillings," she said. "You've told me about the eleven shillings and I've heard you and it's yours to answer for. I'm going to do something now and it's going to look like the opposite."

---

She did three things between twelve and two, and all three of them were correct.

The first was to the warden's council, and she put it in writing because she is a warden and things go in writing, and what she asked for was: that the ninth ward's quarterly return be withdrawn from circulation, that no further copies be furnished to any body not in the Charter, and that the Exchange be asked in the council's name to return or destroy the copy already held.

The answer came in four minutes, and the answer was correct, and it is the answer.

A return already furnished cannot be recalled. A council may stop sending. It has no purchase in a copy it has sent, no more than a man has purchase in a letter he has posted, and there is no form — there is not a form, there is not a form in this basin, in this Office, in this Charter, or in any ward's practice — for a public body to recall a true copy of a true return it has already given away.

The clerk who wrote it had been a clerk for twenty-six years and had been kind about it, and the sentence that ended it was: *The council can of course express a view.*

The second thing was to the Charter, because there is a line in the Charter of Public Notice and Neris Kade could recite it in her sleep, having spent a great deal of the Bellweather month being it at people:

*The Exchange is not a party to any district's preparations and is not required to price them.*

"It is not a party to our preparations," she said, to nobody, in the district office, at about half past one. "It is not required to price them. That's the rule, and the rule is the Deputy for Records' own handwriting on a sheet in a review, and he wrote it out for the Council to adopt and the Council adopted it. That is the actual rule and it is in the Charter and it is our own and we have never once been asked to say it out loud. And the rule does not say they can't read them."

The third thing was the best one and it cost her the afternoon.

She got on the tram to the yards and went and stood at a counter in a building she had never been inside, in a part of the city she had no reason to be in, and asked a young man in a good coat whether the Exchange would confirm that a schedule of a public body's published practice was not the property of that body, and whether the Exchange would accordingly return the ninth ward's copy, and whether the Exchange would put the ward's own figure beside the Exchange's on anything it published about the ward.

The young man was courteous. He was extremely courteous. He was, in fact, so courteous that at one point he apologised for the existence of a form.

He confirmed all three.

A schedule of a public body's published practice is not the property of that body. The Exchange would return nothing, because the Exchange had paid for it and had not been asked to give it away, and because returning it would be an admission about its own product. And the Exchange would be glad to receive the district's own figure, and the Exchange's district research desk was at that moment the only desk in the basin actively seeking district figures, and the district's own figure would be *set beside* the Exchange's in the ordinary course.

"That's what I asked for," said Neris Kade.

"That is what you asked for and it is available on the ordinary terms, at the ordinary price, and I would send the district a form for it this afternoon." He looked at her properly for the first time. "Would you like me to send the form?"

"Would the form be able to say that the return is not to be published?"

"It would not be able to say that. It is a return."

"Then send the form anyway," said Neris Kade, "and put my answer on it in the box."

And she wrote in the box, in a warden's hand, the thing that has been in her for a month and has not gone in anywhere:

*Published on request. Not withdrawn. This is not consent and it is not a notice.*

She read it twice, and it is the only sentence in the whole of this that she would have liked to be in a column, and there is no column for it.

---

She went out into the yard at about twenty past four and the rain was still on, ordinary, and she walked to the tram with her hood down because it had stopped mattering two hours ago, and she was wet through to the shoulder by the time she got to the stop.

The thing she had to say, and she said it to the wet and to a tram timetable, is this.

For four years she has kept a ruled page in a drawer and written on it, one line per notice, in the order the notices came, the things that the notice did not say. Seven lines. The seventh one is the load-bearing one and it is about a reach: *the thirtieth's return does not say the reach levels came down, because there is no instrument in the Office that looks at a reach, and therefore nothing that was lost on the reach can be said to have been lost by anybody. The notice is correct. The district cannot be shown to have been right about the levels and cannot be shown to have been wrong. A window the city cannot see is not a window the city loses.*

A window the city cannot see is not a window the city loses.

And on the second of the month, a firm in the western part of this city cut a hole in a wall on the Bellweather freight apron, put a copper float in it, and ran two hundred and forty feet of iron chain over a drum, and paid a censured man on the Weatherglass ridge to cut a glass datum so that the chain would pay out true, and the firm can now see the reach.

It sees it four times a day. It wrote the number down. And on the Wednesday morning it was in a yard on a second page of a price list, under a rule about withdrawn quotations and published prices, and it has been struck through once, in pencil, by hand, and there is a price in the margin in the same pencil, and the price is the morning of the thirtieth of the Bellweather month and forty minutes.

**Her own seventh line was right and it is now the market's business.**

---

The rain stopped at about seven.

At about eight she went back into the district office and wrote the day's report, which is three lines, and then she did what she has done at the end of every wet day for four years, which is to open the drawer and take out the ruled page and read the seven lines, and to look at the blank space under the seventh where the eighth would go.

She read them. She did not write an eighth line. She has not been able to write the eighth line since the middle of the Bellweather month and she is not going to be able to write it in the month she is in now, and the reason has not changed and has got worse, and the reason is a card with nine names on it in a different drawer, and the woman who wrote the card out does not remember writing it out and is not playing at anything.

She shut the drawer.

The lamp on the district office wall is a wall lamp with a mica cap and it makes a small yellow room about six feet across in a building that is otherwise dark, and above the low end of this city, two thousand feet up, on the corner of Tannery Walk and Sump Lane, the ninth ward tower's signal glass was showing nothing at all, as it had shown nothing at all since the middle of the month before last, because there is a bubbled temporary in a brass frame where a pane ought to be, and the pane the district paid for two years ago is in a glassblower's shop at Kestrel Yards, and the district's glasswork allocation has never once in two years covered a man and a crate sent forty miles.

Four hundred and forty people had their ordinary rain handled by two wardens and a barrow and six men and the water steward, on a Wednesday, in Sleetmonth, and nothing happened, and it was not written down anywhere except in three lines in a district report and in a wet notebook in Sergeant Ampow's hand, and in a ruled page in a drawer that is stamped and dated and not compellable.

The eleventh of the month. It was going to rain again on the Sunday, and again probably on the Thursday, and none of it was going to be a Bellweather, and the ward would handle all of it in four hours with two wardens and a barrow.

What had changed, at about ten past four that afternoon, in a small office above a locker's yard on Tannery Walk, was that the ward's rain now had a price, and the ward had sold it, and the price was eleven shillings, and the eleven shillings was nobody's business, and the important part was free.
