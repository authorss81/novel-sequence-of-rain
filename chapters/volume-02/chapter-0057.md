# Chapter 57 — The Ward Sits

The ninth ward assembly meets in a room over a locker's yard on Tannery Walk and it has a bench along each wall and a stove in the middle and a table that is two tables pushed together, and it has had the same stove since before Dessie Trask became chair of it, which was twenty-two years ago and she was thirty-six then and she has been the ward's chair since she was thirty-six and she is fifty-eight now and she is the only official in the ninth ward who gets nothing for it.

On the Friday of the twentieth day of Sleetmonth the stove was going and there were nine people in the room and two of them were there because they had to be, which is a thing that does not happen in this room.

The agenda was four hours long, which is unusual, and the reason it was four hours long is that at about four o'clock the previous afternoon a man had come to the door and asked whether the ward intended to purchase a reach window, and had said the price out loud, and had said the hour, and had said that the sales for the window of Monday the twenty-third closed at noon on the Saturday, which is the day a claim goes on the rail, and that nobody has yet explained to a ward what a window's sales closing on the same morning as a Council's entry bell is supposed to mean.

"There is no agenda," said Dessie Trask. "There is a thing Garrick Dellow's man has said in this room and about nine people have understood it. Go on."

---

Garrick Dellow was forty-four and he held the freehold of two hundred yards of the ninth ward's lowest ground, which is the two hundred yards that drain into the third block, and he was there because two hundred yards is worth four thousand pounds a year and the ninth ward is not a place where four thousand pounds a year is contested.

He was not a villain and he was not a fool and he was not lying and he sat at the table with his hat on his knee for eighty minutes and did not interrupt one thing.

He gave the account of himself the way a man gives an account of himself that has been true for twenty years.

He bought reach windows. He had bought them for eleven years, off the Exchange when there was an Exchange to buy them off and off a chandler at Ternway before that, and he bought them because the reach at the bottom of the Bellweather comes down about nine days a year and when it comes down there is copper and kerb and a foot of good gravel in two hundred yards of low ground that has been under water since the last time, and a man can get in there with a barge and a gang and be out in a day and a half.

"The kerb goes where?" said Dessie Trask.

"In the spoil," said Dellow.

"Where's the spoil?"

"In the drains. On the ground. Same as it's always been. My men's in the drains half a day and the drains are the ward's and I pay for clearing them afterwards and I have paid for clearing them afterwards every year since I started, and that's eleven years of clearing, and I'd like somebody in this room to write that down, because I have never once seen it written down anywhere and I have said it out loud in a shed and in a yard and in this room and it has never once been in a column."

It was true. It was on the ward's quarterly return, page four, in Marda Loon's figures: *third block low side, catch pits cleared on the district's rota, gang of six, three days' clearance in the season where a private gang has been at work.* It was in a document the ward had furnished to the Weather Office and sold to the Cloud Exchange for eleven shillings, and it was in no column, and it was the reason the third block's ground floors flooded in the first rain of the quarter and did not flood in the second.

That last part was not said in the room and was not going to be said in the room. But it was the true arithmetic, and the true arithmetic was the only argument anybody in that room had.

"Two things," said Dellow. "One. I will pay the Exchange's price for the twenty-third's window and I have paid it. It is seven and six and it is the same price anybody pays and I am not asking for a discount and I have never asked for a discount and I have had this ground twenty years and I have never once asked this ward for anything except the water to go off it.

"Two. That window is the first one this season anybody has paid real money for, and there is one of them, and if the ward wants it, the ward can have it, and I will not bid against the ward for it, and I will put that in writing this evening, because I do not want to be a man who took a window off a ward."

There was a silence of about four seconds.

"Why," said Dessie Trask.

"Because when I took eleven of them off this ground in the last eleven years, nobody ever once came to the door to ask me for one," said Garrick Dellow, "and if the ward starts coming to the door, then this ground is a place and not a resource, and I have got twenty years of the other thing and I have never lost a night's sleep on it and I would like to know what the other way round is like before I am sixty."

Nobody in the room believed him entirely and nobody thought he was lying, and both of those facts were correct and stayed in the room.

---

The Exchange's man came at about half past seven, and he came with a printed sheet and a fair hand and a licence.

He set it out in four minutes because he had been asked to, and here is what the ward was told.

The Exchange sells windows. A window is an undertaking that an operation may be depended upon in the hours named. The Exchange takes the risk. The price for a reach window at the autumn low is **seven pounds six**, the refund is at half if not opened, and the price of a withdrawn window is published on the wall.

The ward may have one.

"There is a condition," said the agent. "The Exchange sells a window to a party that can show a use for the hours. It is not a moral condition and it is not a credit condition. It is that we will not sell an undertaking to somebody who cannot tell us what the undertaking is for, because the refund rule works both ways and a purchaser who cannot use an hour cannot be refunded in any way that means anything."

"And if we cannot show a use?"

"Then the Exchange will not sell the window, and I will put that in a letter for you in the ordinary way, and there is no difficulty about it and it is not a complaint." He was very even. "May I ask what the ward would use it for?"

Dessie Trask said it in one sentence, because it is one sentence.

"A crew on the flat with the timber the water office ordered in the spring, about nine hundred pounds of it, banded in four, and a replacement dewatering pump at four hundred and ten on a pallet, and a crew of nine men, to put all of it onto the cracked headwall of the Lower Sill culvert before the next front, which comes back under four years on a table at the Weatherglass."

Nobody in the room said anything.

"Then that is a use," said the agent, "and that is the best use anyone can put to a reach window that I have heard this week, and I am not permitted to say anything about the condition of a culvert and I am not going to."

He put the sheet down and squared it.

"The Exchange will also point out, without being asked, that a window is not a forecast. It does not make the water go off. It says that if the operation can be performed, the hours are dependable, and if it cannot, you get your money back. It is a way of buying certainty, and certainty is what a district with a repair to do and no money to be wrong with is short of, and the price is the price, and it is the same price to a barge as it is to a pump."

"What's the ward's use worth," said Bram Slee, from the back, in a voice that had gone very quiet. "In your figure. Seven and six."

"I am not permitted to value a use. The Exchange prices a window and the price is one figure for every purchaser of that window."

"Then it is a rigged figure," said Bram Slee.

"It is a *market* figure," said the agent, "and I have read a great deal of what your ward thinks about markets in the last four weeks and I would point out, respectfully, that the ward has sold me a document describing exactly what its crews can do, at eleven shillings, in the ordinary course, and that a firm which knows what nine men and a flatbed and a pump cost is a firm that could put a number on them, and it has not, because nobody will pay it, and that is the same answer in a different room."

That was the sentence that did it, and it was true, and the person who said it was a young man with a good coat and a licence who had been in the room for eleven minutes and had no idea what he had done.

---

Neris Kade was standing at the back with her hood down and she came forward about a foot.

"Right," she said. "So the Exchange won't sell us a window, and it will sell him one, and both of those are the same rule."

"Ma'am."

"And if we can't have the window on the twenty-third, and he has it, then he strips the kerb and the copper and the gravel out of two hundred yards on the twenty-third and the spoil goes into the third block's drains, and then it rains, and then the third block's ground floors are under, and the ward's own return says — page four — that the third block's low side runs about three days in the season with a private gang in the drains. And the Office has never once had that in a return, and the Council has never once had it in a return, and this ward has never once been asked about it because nobody could ask us, because we had nothing to give them, and now we have something to give them and we have given it away for eleven shillings and I would like somebody to explain to me what a person does with that."

Nobody explained it to her.

She stood there for a moment, and then she said the other thing, and she said it to the room and not to the agent.

"Except that the Exchange will withdraw it," she said. "That's the actual answer and it's been in the room for forty minutes and nobody has picked it up. A window is a thing somebody sells. If the Exchange withdraws the quotation for that morning — strikes it through on the wall, publishes the price, keeps the pound on the board it already has — then there is no window, and there's no window to buy, and Dellow cannot buy one, and he cannot be a bad customer for a thing that does not exist."

Dessie Trask sat down, which she had not been doing.

"Go and ask him," she said.

Neris went out into the lane and came back in about eleven minutes and had been in the rain for all of them.

"He says no," she said. "He says he has paid seven and six and it is a fair price and a fair window and he is not going to be the man who makes a fuss in a market on the ninth ward's account, and he says — and he said it twice, and he said it looking at me — he says that if this ward wants him not to have the window it should come and ask him, and it should ask him properly, and it should tell him what it is going to do instead, and then he will decide."

"And if we can't tell him what we're going to do instead?"

"Then he is entitled to his seven and six," said Neris Kade, "and so are we, and the two of us cannot both have it."

---

**Sabin Orle came at about half past eight and it was not a scheduled attendance and she had a clerk with her.**

She is forty-four and she has been eleven years in the Council's technical cell and she is a woman who reads every term out loud in a room because a thing that is not read out loud in a room is a thing that four people remember differently, and she had said the sentence that beat the last reader in this basin in a shuttered room in the autumn, and she had lost nothing for saying it.

She was also the woman who put an expiry on the same line as an effect, on the twenty-eighth of the Bellweather month, at a desk, in a corridor, for no reason she gave at the time and would not give now.

"I am going to say four things," she said, standing at the end of the two tables pushed together, "and then I am going to go and sit down, and I would be obliged if the chair would let me finish all four, because in my experience the fourth one is the one people want to talk about and it is the one I am here about."

"Go on."

"First. A district may enter a conditional claim. It has one of the twelve seats of this Council and it has always had one and it does not need anybody's leave and it does not need a sponsor from outside itself. A Storm Warden may sponsor for her ward. The claim goes on the ward ledger, the entry bell is struck, and it binds. That is ordinary and it has been ordinary for two hundred years.

"Second. The claim has to be *certified*, and certification takes six things, and I will read all six because that is my job and not because I want to make this difficult. A named sponsor. A published expiry. A listed alternative. A concurring seat. A reader's attestation of the order of the movements, and of the order only. And a positive stated effect."

"Three is the difficulty," said Dessie Trask.

"Three is the difficulty," said Orle agreed. "Item six. And the difficulty is not the effect. The ward's effect is as plain as anything I have ever certified. The reach does not come down to the quoted mark in the quoted hours. That is a place, a level, a mark on a post that a district crew clerk reads with a chain and a staff every month of the year, and there are two instruments in this basin that can establish it, and the Exchange has one, and the ward has the other by way of the ninth ward's own glass, which I am told is unreadable and which I have therefore not counted."

Nobody said anything at that.

"Item three is the difficulty," said Orle, "and the difficulty is this: I have to certify that there is a positive stated effect, and a positive stated effect means the claim does something, and the only way a claim does something is if it is true, and I am the assessor and I certify that it will be true, and I have certified nine claims in eleven years and every one of them was true and I have never once been asked what any of them cost, and last month I sat in a room for five days and I wrote a ruling that the effect of a claim on a district's preparations cannot be shown from the record."

"Which is your ruling and it is correct," said Neris Kade, "and I put nine of my own sentences into that room and you read the seventh one out loud and it cost me something and I want to thank you for that and I want to say this as well. You are going to certify this one."

"I am going to certify this one."

"Then say the fourth thing."

---

**Orle put her hands on the back of a chair.**

"Fourth. This is a true claim and it will be certified and it will bind, and binding it will close one available turn, and I am required to record before I certify that the sponsor has been informed in writing of what the closing is, and the sponsor is Warden Kade and the ward is the ninth ward, and so I am going to say it out loud in this room in plain words and I am going to write it down afterwards and I am not going to soften it, and I would like it noted that I have never had to do this and that I think the reason I have never had to do this is that no district has ever wanted a claim before."

She read from a page, and it was the only page, and there was no drafting on it.

"The claim holds the reach up for the quoted hours. The turn it closes is the turn that would have put the water off. **What the ward loses is the ward's own reach window on the morning of the twenty-third, and with it the crew, the flatbed, the pump, and about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber and a four hundred and ten pound dewatering pump going onto the cracked headwall of a culvert that is failing for the second consecutive season.** The water office's own table says a Bellweather of this order returns under four years. It was three years and it is now two years and about eleven months. If the district works that wall in the twenty-third's forty minutes, it is shored from good timber with a new bed for the pump and it is good for the next front. If it does not, that timber stays in a shed under a painted board for a second season, and the wall is shored a third time out of other timber or it is not shored, and the money is not the money, and the money is nothing. The money is nine men standing in a shed for a fortnight for the second time in nine weeks."

She put the page down.

"That is the fourth thing," said Sabin Orle, "and I have said it, and I am now going to ask the chair whether the ninth ward wishes to proceed, and I would like the answer given in the room and not in a corridor, and I would like the vote taken separately from the discussion, because in my experience a room that is frightened will vote for anything if you let it discuss first."

---

There were nine people in that room and eight of them could vote, because a warden does not vote on a claim she is sponsoring, and Neris Kade said so before anybody had thought to ask her.

Dessie Trask has never in twenty-two years taken a vote without telling people what the vote was for, and she said it in one sentence, and the sentence was: *we are voting to hold the water up on the twenty-third, on purpose, so that Mr. Dellow cannot get in, and the price is nine hundred pounds of timber and four hundred and ten pounds of pump and nine men and a wall, and a year.*

Six for. One against, who was Bram Slee, who said afterwards that he had voted against it because he had spent thirty-one years selling the ward's rain for eleven shillings and had a right to be the one who said no once. One abstained, who was a man of sixty-five who had lived in the second block for forty-one years and who said afterwards that he had abstained because he did not understand a word of items three to six and had not understood them in nineteen years of council meetings and was not going to pretend.

Neris Kade said: "I'll take the vote as carried and I'll carry the thing and I'll read the cost into the sheet in the words the ward uses, because that is the only part of it that is mine."

---

The claim is drafted at the table until about a quarter to eleven.

Orle wrote the certification, which is three paragraphs, and one of them is a thing she has never written before and she wrote it slowly and then read it back and left it in.

*The assessor records that the sponsor has been informed in writing that a binding claim closes one available turn, and that the sponsor has named that turn, and that the turn named is the district's own window, and that this is the ward's informed choice and was taken in a room in its presence.*

The listed alternative is Orle's insistence and it took twenty minutes and it is the sentence the whole of the volume is built on and it does not read like a sentence that is built on anything:

*If the quotation for the morning of the twenty-third is withdrawn before the entry bell, no claim binds; the reach behaves as the weather decides; the district keeps its window; and the district is answerable to the people it did not move.*

The expiry goes on the same line as the effect, at the end of the quoted hours, because that is Orle's rule and she has never explained it and will not.

And the sheet has a line under all of it, in the words of Hearthward's standing requirement as adopted, headed **TURNS DISPLACED**, and the ward's clerk writes it and the ward's clerk is competent and does what she is told:

*Reaches come down about nine days a year. This morning's is the first. The water office's table says the front returns under four years; it was three years at the end of the Bellweather month. The load has been in the south-bank shed since the sixth and it is paid for and the pump is on a guarantee and the wall is in the reach. A crew is nine men. The ward's own return says the third block runs three days in the season with a private gang in the drains, and the Exchange's man said that out loud in this room on Friday and it is true and it is the reason for this claim. THE WARD IS CLOSING ITS OWN REPAIR WINDOW TO STOP A SECOND SALE OF IT. That is the cost and it is not money.*

---

Marek Vale was asked to attest at about half past ten, in the lane, because the room was full and he had been in it for forty minutes and had not said anything in it.

He said yes before he was asked the second half of it.

Then Orle said, from the doorway, without any particular weight at all, as a person checking a box: "You will need a second reader at the far end for the ebb, Mr. Vale. The ninth ward's own glass is unreadable and there is no sequence instrument on this reach, and the ebb is a sequence fact and not a level fact, and you cannot read a sequence across two towers. That is not a criticism. Is it going to be a difficulty?"

"That is going to be a difficulty," said Marek Vale.

"You would want a second reader."

"I can't," said Marek Vale. "I want a second reader at the far end and I can't do it alone. I did it once, in the spring, off the ninth ward's own feed, with one man at one end of it and me at the other, and it came apart in about forty minutes and it came apart sideways, and I will not put this ward's window in the middle of it. There is a woman in this city who can hold a local end and there are about four people in this building who could pay her, and I have not asked any of them, and I am telling you that I have not asked because you are going to ask me for it in a minute and I would rather you heard it from me."

There were four people in the lane and none of them said anything, and one of them was a Council assessor with a page.

Sabin Orle wrote it down.

"Noted," she said, "as a limitation on the reader, in the room and not in a file. You may go, or you may stay and help the clerk with the arithmetic on the levels, which is not a reader's work and which she is going to get wrong."

He stayed and she was right and he got it right.

At about ten past eleven, in a room over a locker's yard on Tannery Walk, nine people put their coats on over a vote they had taken by show of hands in about eleven minutes on a subject none of them had ever voted on, and outside the lane the rain started again, ordinary, and it was going to come back on the Sunday and again on the Thursday, and none of it was going to be a Bellweather.

The claim would be entered at the ward ledger bench on the Saturday morning at about ten, in one hand, without lifting the pen, by the ninth ward's own clerk, on the Council's form, with the Council's copy clerk behind her at the desk in the west end of the records room doing the one thing she is still allowed to do.
