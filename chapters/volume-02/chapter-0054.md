# Chapter 54 — A Man Who Prices Honesty

Tobias Mear was twenty-nine and he had been an underwriter for six years and he had been right about one thing in his life and wrong about another and both of the facts had come out of the same afternoon.

The right one: in a public hall in the Bellweather month, standing in front of a yard's framed column with a carrier's ledger open on the trestle in front of him, he had said that a yard which published its own errors was now an exposure, and that a market has one opinion about an exposure and the opinion is a price, and that he would have to go and stand in front of a man in a yard and tell him so. He had said it in about nine seconds and then had gone north on the night coach and had not enjoyed the eleven hours of it.

The wrong one: he had also said, to a woman in the fourth block, that a line of forty minutes on a reach with nobody's name on it was the most frightening thing anybody had said to him in nine years. He had been wrong, and the reason he had been wrong was that he had gone away and thought about it, and the fear had gone out of it, because a cost with no defendant is not frightening to a man who sells. **A cost with no defendant is a price.**

That was the whole of his education and it had cost him six years and a fairly good coat.

His work on the Monday was a pricing desk on the second floor of the Cloud Exchange, and the item on it was a **forward reach board**, which is to say a printed sheet of prices for windows on the Bellweather reach for the rest of the season and for the autumn low, with hours and levels and a refund rate, and which had to go up on Monday in the yard and in the hall and on the third board at the chandler's.

He had one historical reach window in the whole of the Exchange's book. It was struck through. Its refund had been a pound.

Two shillings was not a price for a miss. Two shillings was a number a partner had put down in a hurry in the days after the storm because a window had failed and a young man had been on the floor and it had to be published by Friday.

"Mr. Mear." Edda Tarn, from the end of the table. "You've got the reach again."

"I've got the reach again."

"You've had it four days."

"It's a reach," said Mear. "It doesn't do anything else. It comes down and it goes up and about nine days a year it's the best water in the basin, and everything I put on that board has to be a number somebody will act on with a barge, and I have one data point and it is a refund, and a refund is the price of not selling, and a refund tells me what the Exchange lost and nothing whatever about what the buyer lost, and those are not the same and only one of them is in my book."

"You want me to invent the other one."

"I want to go and look at it," said Tobias Mear.

---

The freight apron is a hundred and forty yards of concrete on the upstream side of a reach, and beyond the chain at the near end there is a flood-level gate and a shed at the far end, and a shed about forty yards from that one.

The near shed had a door with a chain on it and it was open, and inside there was a bench, a kettle, a level book on a shelf, and a man of about sixty-eight who put down a brush when Tobias Mear came in, and looked at him for about four seconds.

"Tide gauges," said the man.

"No," said Mear. "Reach gauge. But you're the only one, aren't you. You'll be Renn Sallow."

"That'll be me."

"It's the fourth on this wall going down," said Mear. "The well's cut about four feet above the sill and the float's in it and the chain runs over a drum on a bracket and there's a glass datum in a brass seat with three adjusting screws, and there's a copper plate with a number on it that says the wall's datum, and the plate is older than anything else here by forty years."

"It is."

"You cut it."

"I cast the collar that holds it and I ground the plate and I cut the threads. The glass isn't mine." Sallow did not offer a hand. "A man off the ridge made the glass. He's not Office, whatever his order says. He's got no chain behind him and he said so on the docket, which is more than any instrument in this Office carries on its own book."

"What was his name?"

"Marek Vale."

Mear wrote it down. He wrote it down the way he wrote everything, which was once, and legibly, and then read it back, which he did not do.

"Sallow. How long have you been making these?"

"Forty years. Tide gauges for the port and the two breweries and the Old Custom House landing and a grain dock at Nacre that shut in 'sixty." He nodded at the wall. "That's the first one I've ever put in a river."

"It's the best of them."

"It's not the best of them, it's the fourth, and it's the first one I've ever been asked to make repeatable to a quarter of an inch at an interval by two different people with two different chains, and the man who made the datum understood why, and he's the only instrument hand in this basin who's ever understood why." Sallow picked his brush up and then put it down again. "You've come about the price."

"I've come about the price."

"Then you'll want the shed."

---

Petrie's shed is at the far end of the apron, and there is a plank against the door with white paint on it, and Tobias Mear read it standing up, in the cold, at about ten past eight in the morning.

**SOUTH BANK DEPOT.**
**SHORING TIMBER AND REPLACEMENT DEWATERING PUMP, STORED AGAINST THE WATER OFFICE'S ORDER 114.**
**LOADED THE SIXTH.**
**NINE CREW ASSIGNED.**
**THE LOAD IS HERE.**
**PAINTED BY THE FOREMAN.**

It was very good painting. Whoever had done it could letter.

"Load's in there," said the foreman, from the bench outside his own door, with a cup. "You're not the first one that's stood in front of that and gone funny."

"I'm not going to go funny. I want to know what's in it."

"Four bands, a stack about nine foot, and a pump on a pallet." The foreman drank his tea. "Union guarantee on the drum, number's on it if you want it. Timber's been in here since the sixth of the Bellweather month, near enough two months, near enough three."

"Who bought it?"

"The water office. Order 114. It's against the Lower Sill culvert."

"How much?"

"Well, nine hundred pounds of timber, and the pump's four hundred and ten, and the freight's in the district's account, and the nine men are on the district's books from the sixth of the Bellweather month." The foreman looked at him over the cup. "It's the oldest deferred repair in this city and it comes back in three years, which is on a table at the Weatherglass and is not a prophecy, it's a table."

"Right," said Mear. He had a card out by then, and he did not write on it, because he had been about nine minutes away from understanding something and he wanted to hold still. "It's been in there three months."

"Three months."

"And the window to get a loaded flatbed and a pump onto the bottom of that reach is about nine days a year."

"About a fortnight a year when the low flat will take 'em," said the foreman, "and about nine days when it's any use, and there's a mark on a post at the turn with the levels cut round it, and if the mud's over it you can't get on, and if you can't get on you can't do it, and that's the whole of the trade."

Mear stood in the mud for about a minute.

"Who was the buyer of the window," he said. "In the Bellweather month. The Exchange quoted a reach window and it didn't open."

"I don't know about no window."

"Whoever bought it."

"Dellow," said Petrie. "Garrick Dellow. He's got two hundred yards of the low ground up the hill off this and he buys the windows to strip the kerb and the copper when the water's off it." He put the cup down. "And he had a barge hired for it and he stood on the apron two days and he did not go in, because the water didn't go off it, and he was very quiet about it, and he paid his refund like a gentleman, and he's never once said a word about it, which I'd think a bit of."

"Paid what?"

"Whatever the paper said." Petrie got up off the bench. "That's the thing about a price, son. There's a number on it and the number's a number, and the man who lost the two days doesn't get to be cross about the two days, he's got the number, and the number's in a book in a building on the west side, and a number's the whole of what anybody's been given."

Mear looked at the painted board again.

"You don't know why it didn't open."

"No," said the foreman. "I've been thirty years in this shed and I keep a book with a column in it for *no work, reason*, and it's been empty thirty years, and on the thirtieth of the Bellweather month I wrote *reach came back* in the column for work done, because a fact isn't a reason. And I wrote in the reason column nothing, because I haven't got one. And there was no somebody. Nobody stood me down. Nobody came down this road." He picked up his brush. "There's a front in three years and it isn't a threat, it's a table, and I paint this board every ninth year, and the sixth is on it and not the thirtieth, and I've told a woman from the yard that and she's told a great many people, and it's the correct arrangement."

---

Tobias Mear did his arithmetic on the back of the Exchange's weekly bulletin in a staging room at the Weatherglass's north end, sitting on a bench under a window that looked out at the eastern ridge and at nothing else at all, because that is what they all look out at.

The reach: a fortnight a year when the low flat will take a loaded flatbed and a pump, about nine days when it is any use. The window: the ebb under a long band in the western draw. About forty minutes. It was a fact he had been given by a man in a shed and he had checked it in the Office's own public return and the Office's public return did not mention it, which was itself a fact, and he had been careful not to enjoy that.

**His buyer's window.** A day's hire of a barge with chain and gear: three and six. A gang of four and a foreman, a day: one and nine. A hull with a tide it cannot use, six hours standing off a reach: two and three.

Total: **seven and six.**

**The other window.** Six men at the district rate for a day, the crew and the gang together: eleven pence. A gang for the pump and its gear: five pence. Nothing at all for the timber and nothing at all for the pump, because both are already bought and both are sitting in a shed and are being paid for at this moment by a district that owes for them. Nothing for the flatbed, because the district owns the flatbed and would have put it on the low ground on any road going to work.

Total: **one and four.**

Same air. Same water. Same forty minutes. **Five and a half times,** and every one of the figures on both sides is a true figure, and no person in this transaction has said anything untrue.

And Mear sat on that bench for a while with the two figures on the back of a bulletin, and worked out — because he was thorough, and because he had been six years in this trade and had a rule about it — **why.**

Not because anybody had lied. Because a barge operator's window contains a hull, and a hull has a hire, and a hire is money, and money has a price. A ward's window contains nine men and a flatbed, and the nine men are the district, and the flatbed is the district, and the timber is already paid for, and the pump is already paid for, and **the marginal cost of doing the work that the timber was bought for is the cost of turning up.** Which is almost nothing. Which is why nobody has ever priced it, and which is why — and this was the sentence that made him write both numbers down twice — **a ward is the cheapest place in the basin to lose, not because the ward is poor, but because the work a ward does is work it is already paying for.**

He wrote, in the margin of the struck-through line, in pencil, in his own hand, a memorandum. Not a price — he was not authorised and it was not that kind of note.

*Reach window, autumn low, 40 min. Bench value of the operation to a barge interest, as computed on this desk: 7/6. Cost of the same window to a district holding the load already bought and paid: 1/4 by my working and under 2/- whatever theirs is, and I cannot see their figure and have not asked, because we do not ask. These are not the same object. Publish the first. Hold this.*

And then, because he was that sort of man and because he had spent six years being right once and wrong once in the same afternoon, he added two lines under it.

*The refund rule pays the price of not selling. It does not pay for what the buyer would have done. If the Exchange is ever made to pay a second time, on a loss rather than on a sale, it will be paying on a figure of ours, and we have no schedule of what anybody was going to do, and we have a rule against asking. That is the hole and it is ours and it is not a lie.*

*Ask the technical cell what a ward can do about its water. Do not paraphrase the form.*

He left the sheet on the bench and the bench was in a room nobody used, and he went down to the floor and stood at the end of the long table and looked at row four on the board, which had no row number, which said a pound, and which he had looked at every day for four days and had not yet worked out the thing that was actually wrong with it.

It was not the pound. The pound was, in fairness, roughly defensible for a first sale in a hurry.

The thing that was wrong with it was that **he now had a number for what the reach was worth to the people who lived on it, and the Exchange was not going to print it, and the reason the Exchange was not going to print it was that a customer who reads two figures for the same forty minutes stops buying the expensive one.**

And that, he thought, standing at the end of the table with his hands behind his back, was the actual moment at which the business changed hands, and it had happened in a shed forty yards from a painted board, on a cold morning, to a man of twenty-nine who had said one true thing in a public hall six weeks ago and had been right about it.
