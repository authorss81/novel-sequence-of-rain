# Chapter 60 — A Partner's Mistake

Maro Hesk read the form for four minutes and did not turn it over and did not ask for the folder and did not ask Wend Lyle to sit down.

What he did instead was put it flat on the second floor's corner desk, in the middle of a quotation hall with four double-sided tables and forty people on them, and put his thumb on the ruled lines underneath the four lines of fair hand, and look at that space for about two of the four minutes, because it was a thing he had not seen before and he wanted the whole of it.

Then he said, "Thank you, Mr. Lyle. Keep the folder, keep the date, and if anybody in this building tells you the form was not received, tell them I have it and that I shall be able to say what day I got it."

The boy went.

At about half past nine Hesk sent for Ivo Slate, and then went downstairs and out, and came back at eleven.

---

Here is the thing about the Cloud Exchange's oldest rule, and it is nineteen years old, and it is on the wall in the quotation hall under the eleven lines, and nobody in the building has ever examined it, because a rule that is never examined does not attract attention and a rule that has been running that long has the authority of a wall.

*The Exchange is not a party to any district's preparations and is not required to price them.*

The Exchange has never asked a district what it can do about its water. Not once, in nineteen years, in nine wards, with about two hundred separate commercial transactions a year running through the floor. It has *assumed* what a district could do, extensively, from a dozen sources: a ward's own published return, a water office abstract, a councillor's answer to a form, an underwriter's note, a tender document, and one quarterly return in a file that a councillor of thirty-one years' standing sold it for eleven shillings in advance of asking.

Every one of those is a one-sided document. Every one of them says what the firm decided to write down.

The rule is not asked for and it is not defended. It is the reason the firm can be trusted with a refund. **If you have asked a district what its water is going to do and it has told you, then you know something about that district, and a firm that knows something about a district cannot be the only party in the basin that does not know.** So the firm decided, nineteen years ago, when it was eleven people in one room, to know as little as possible about its counterparties in the nine wards, and it has been the most trusted name in the basin's weather trade ever since, and it got that way by refusing to be a party to a set of questions.

On the Friday morning of the fourth of Drownmonth Maro Hesk found out what that was worth.

---

**Attempt the first, about ten past ten, in his own office, without leaving the floor.**

He went to the district research desk and had the ninth ward's file brought, and in the file were a quarterly return of eighteen pages bought for eleven shillings, an invoice, a copy of a Council's *local flood precautions* form stamped purple and returned, a one-line memorandum from a licensing clerk headed *schedule of ward response*, and a piece of foolscap with four lines of good fair hand at the top of it and nothing whatever underneath.

It took him eleven minutes to work out that he had, in his own hands, in his own building, on the Exchange's own paper, **a complete description of what the ninth ward is able to do about its water**, and that he could not use it.

Not *must not* — could not. It was not evidence of anything, because it was not evidence of anything: it was the Exchange's own file, produced by the Exchange, on the Exchange's paper, and it said what the Exchange had assumed. The Exchange's Act — its licence, its articles, the four paragraphs of a schedule it files with the Council every year and does not read — excludes its commercial questions from a district's returns. *No question put by this firm to any district in matters of price, term, or product shall be answered on a return made to a public authority.*

He read that paragraph out loud in his own office at ten past ten on the Friday morning and laughed, once, and it was not a good laugh.

**The first thing that stops a firm asking a district is that a district would have to answer on the wrong piece of paper.**

---

**Attempt the second, about half past eleven, to the second ward.**

The second ward was a good ward and its clerk was a good clerk and the ward's return was in the file four times a year and had been since before the Exchange was licensed.

Hesk went down to the district himself, which is a thing partners do not do and which he had not done in four years, and he asked for the schedule of available responses to a season's water in the form the Council used, and the clerk brought him a form, and filled it in, in eleven minutes, in a fair hand, and gave it to him.

**Then the clerk asked whether the Exchange was going to publish it.**

Hesk said that was the wrong question.

"There is a second question," said the clerk, "and it is the one that matters, and it is: whether the Exchange is going to publish it. Because we have four hundred people on that low side and a third of them are over seventy, and this is a market, and you do not have to be cruel to make a market do what it does."

"It is not going to be published."

"Then I will not fill it in." The clerk put the pen down. "There is a form for that. Our return has a box on page four and I will write you a letter and you can have the letter."

---

**Attempt the third, at about two in the afternoon, in the counting hall, and it is the one that was actually about the thing.**

He worked it out on a board, and it is worth having in the order he got to it, because he was not looking for it and it came out of a set of four things that all happen to be true.

One: a *schedule* is not an *answer*. A district will tell you, today, in a letter, what it can do about a rain that is coming on Thursday. That is an answer, and it is worth a great deal, and it is worth nothing at all in March.

Two: what the firm needs is the second kind of thing — what a ward holds, in a season, without knowing which season is coming. Crews on a book. A pump that works. A reach window it may have. A rota with names on it and a length of time in it.

Three: **the Exchange already has the second kind of thing about the ninth ward, and it is the only copy in the world, and it cost eleven shillings, and it was sold to it by a member of the ward's own council in an office in a coat.**

Four: **and it cannot be used, because a district's answer to a firm, once the firm has it, is a document the firm owns.** A ward that answers a question about its own preparations has, in law and in fact, given the counterparty a schedule of its own exposure. That is not a technicality. That is the reason the ward's clerk would not fill the form, and she was right and she knew she was right and she was doing him the courtesy of not explaining it for an hour.

**The firm that has spent ten years not asking a district anything had spent those ten years ensuring that a district could never hand it anything.**

---

**Attempt the fourth, at about four o'clock, and this is the one that cost him the day.**

The Exchange's own licensed reader — because the Exchange is not obliged to use the Weather Office and for eight months has not used it for anything at all — was on the second floor at four o'clock with an open job docket, having come in to sign off the reconciliation on a piece of glasswork at the Kestrel Line.

Marek Vale came in with his coat on and signed the docket, which is a page of arithmetic and a box that says what the work was, and Hesk noticed, the way you notice a thing when you have spent the day on a category and everything starts to look like an example, that the margin of the docket was in the man's own small hand and was not about the work.

"Two pounds four," said Marek Vale, "for the yard's instrument, and the two shillings for the second seat. The datum is yours and the seat is yours and the readings off it are the yard's, and the yard has said in writing that a reading cut from this yard's published figures carries this yard's name on it with the hour and the date of the line."

"Accepted," said Hesk, and meant it, and it cost the firm nothing and it was the best clause the firm obtained all year.

Then he said, "Sit down, Mr. Vale."

"I have not got till five."

"Sit down for a minute." Hesk did not look up from the docket. "I am going to ask you something and I want you to refuse it before I finish asking it, because I have had four refusals today and I would like the fifth to be quick."

Marek Vale sat down.

"You want to know what the ninth ward can do about its water."

"I do."

"Then here it is, and I have had it from three people this morning, and you have had it from four documents, and none of the seven is worth anything to you." He put the docket down. "If I answer you, I am a party. I attest for nothing in that office's service, I am not on its watch and I am not on its roll, and a private reader's answer about a district's preparations is a thing a district can be shown. You would keep it. It would be in your file. And in eleven years somebody who has been refused by that ward would ask the ward what it told the Exchange, and the ward would have to say, and there would be no answer in the room that is worth a penny to it. That is the first thing and you know it and you have known it since you took this job."

"Yes."

"And the second thing, and this is the one you will not have thought of, and I have had it written on my head all day." He turned the docket round and squared it. "You did not come in here to ask me. You came in here because you ran out of documents at about four o'clock. And I have been in this building for eleven years and I know what the last one of me costs, and the answer you would have got would have been good, and it would have cost a district something that is not yet visible, and neither of us would have been able to name it at the time, and I would have gone out of that door at ten to five and put it in a drawer, because I am very good at putting things in drawers."

Marek Vale looked at him for a moment.

"You've been on the second floor all day asking four people whether you were allowed to ask," he said.

"I have."

"Was the fourth refusal free?"

"About two minutes," said Hesk. "There is a lawyer on the Nacre Gate who charges a guinea and he was very good, and that is the sixth refusal, and it cost a guinea, and it is the only one of the six that I shall be able to put on an invoice."

---

**The lawyer was called Auber Fenner and he was about fifty and he had a room over a chandler's on the Nacre Gate with a window that faced the water and a shelf of statutes he had read and one he had not.**

A guinea, in advance, on the table, in a shilling note, and a receipt for it, and Fenner wrote the receipt himself and dated it and did not put it in a drawer.

Hesk had come with a folder and got about four sentences into it and Fenner stopped him at the fourth.

"Stop," he said. "You are about to tell me you have a document."

"I have a document."

"Whose document?"

"The ninth ward's."

"You have the ninth ward's own schedule of what it can do about its water, in your own file, in your own hand, on your own paper."

"Yes."

"Then you have a list." Fenner took his pen off the desk and put it down again, which is a thing a careful man does when he has decided to say something he has said before. "Mr. Hesk, listen to me carefully, because this is the one sentence I am being paid for tonight and I have given it four times this year and twice I have given it for nothing. **A schedule of a party's capabilities, given to a counterparty without that party's knowledge, is not evidence of the party. It is a list.** It is a list of a party, made by you, and you may keep it, and you may price against it, and you may act on it for nineteen years, and it is worth exactly nothing to you in a room, because any judge in this basin who is worth listening to will ask you the same four questions in the same order. How did you get it. When did you have it. Who gave it to you. What did you pay for it. And on the fourth answer you will say eleven shillings to a councillor who was not the council, and the room will be finished, and the document will be an instrument of the firm and not a record of the ward."

"Then what is the lawful route."

"There is exactly one lawful route and there is no version of it you will like." Fenner wrote on the back of his receipt. "A request. In writing. To the district. Named. Stating what you want it for, that the district may refuse, that the district's refusal will be recorded as a refusal and not as a failure to respond, and that the firm will pay the ordinary price for an answer. And then you wait, and then the district decides."

"They will refuse."

"They will refuse, and you will be right, and you will then hold a refusal in your own hand in your own building, and it will be worth four times what you are trying to buy, because a refusal in the ward's words on a date is the only document in this basin that anybody can be shown."

Hesk sat very still.

"Whose lawyer is it?"

"Yours, if you want one. Mine is on this table, and I have taken your guinea, and I have not given you an opinion that is for anybody else."

"Could the firm pay for the ward's lawyer?"

"You could. You could put a hundred and fifty pounds into a fund today and a competent man on the Nacre Gate would take the ward's file within a week." Fenner put the pen back in the inkwell. "Why are you asking?"

"Because there is a solicitor on this gate who has written to the ninth ward about a different matter, and he has told them not to spend anything on it, and I would like to know whether the reason he told them that was the money or the case."

"Both," said the lawyer. "Always both, and the money is nearly always first."

---

**He did the cheapest possible thing that was also the right thing, and he did it himself, and nobody in this trade has ever done it.**

He did not go to a lawyer. He did not put it through counsel. He did not have a clerk make it fair. He wrote it at the corner of the second floor's counter desk at about half past seven in the evening with a pen he had to ask for, in his own hand, four paragraphs, and he wrote it in the first person and he signed it with his own name and no second name under it, and the reason — which he said out loud to nobody, and which is the whole of it — is that **a letter a district is entitled to refuse has to look like it came from a man and not from a firm, because a firm is a thing with a schedule of terms and a man is a thing you can be rude to, and the ward's right to say no is worth nothing at all if the thing it is said to is a company.**

It went out in the ordinary post on the Friday evening from a box on the ground floor of a building with a brass plate on it, and the date on it was the fourth of Drownmonth, and it cost eleven pence, and the eleven pence is the only part of that day anybody can prove.

*Sir or Madam,*

*The Cloud Exchange asks the ninth ward for a schedule of its available responses to a season's water: crews held, what the ward can put on a reach, and what the ward can be relied upon to have and to be able to do, with a date against it. We hold your quarterly return of the second of Sleetmonth and we set out in the margin what we have assumed from it, so that you may correct us.*

*The ward may refuse. A refusal will be recorded as a refusal, in our own hands, with its date, and will be produced to any party that asks us for this ward's file. It will not be held against the ward in any future transaction with this firm or with any agent of it, and no agent of this firm is instructed on this point and may be instructed on it.*

*We will pay the ordinary price for an answer, and the same ordinary price for a refusal, which is nothing.*

*I am not a public officer and I am not acting for the Weather Office and nothing in this letter is a claim.*

*Maro Hesk.*

---

He read it over once, on the second floor, alone, at eight in the evening, in a building with eleven people in it, and what he was thinking about was not the market.

He was thinking that the firm had gone out and bought a poor district's rain off a member of its own council for eleven shillings, in advance of asking, in a coat, in an office, in forty seconds — and that he had sat in a counting hall and found four lawful documents on his own floor that proved exactly what that district could do and were worth nothing to him in a room — and that the one thing his nineteen-year-old rule could not do was put the two facts on the same side of the table.

**He had built a firm that could not be shown anything by a district, and he had done it on purpose, ten years ago, and it was the best commercial decision he ever took, and it had just cost him the ability to price the only product he has.**

At the bottom of the second floor, in a corridor, a maintenance hand on this ridge was putting his coat on to go home, and had asked, twice, for a figure to be said again, and had taken it twice, and had not once been told why.

Hesk watched him go and thought nothing at all about it, and put the docket in the file, and locked the file, and went home.
