# Chapter 64 — Who Stopped Asking

The memorandum came into the basin house at about a quarter past nine on the Thursday morning of the tenth day of Drownmonth, in an envelope with nothing on it, and Sabin Orle read it in her room off the corridor because she reads everything in her own room, and she read it twice, and then she went out and found the assessor on duty and told him three things in about nine seconds.

"First. It is not evidence of anything. It is a piece of paper with a price on it and a sentence in it saying the price is not the price, and there is no claimant in it and there is no sponsor and there is no party. A price is not a claim. A claim is not a purchase. A purchase is not a preparation. A preparation is not a cost. **He has put the last two inches of that sentence in the right order and the whole of the first three in the wrong building, and he is twenty-nine, and he is right, and none of it is admissible in front of anybody.**

"Second. It is admissible as a document of a party's belief. Not of a fact. Of a belief, held on a date, by a named man in a trade, in his own hand, and he says in it that it is not to be printed, and he has then sent it to me, and the sending is part of the belief and I am going to rule that it is. That is one sentence and I am not going to be asked for it twice.

"Third. Somebody fetch the Standing Response Register out of the press, and somebody else find out whether the Council has ever issued a schedule of a ward's available responses, and if so when, and to whom, and if not, say so in a way I can read out loud in a minute."

The assessor on duty was about thirty and he said, "That book has not been out of that press in my time."

"Then it will have to come out of that press in yours," said Sabin Orle, "and I want the room shut, and I want Skell in, and I want to be disturbed as little as possible before eleven."

---

**It took her under a minute to get the year, and it took Skell about four seconds to stop hedging it, and neither of them had any difficulty at all.**

The bound volume in the press is divided into sections by years and every section has a flyleaf, and the flyleaf over the section gives the seasons it covers, and the season lines inside are dated by season. The current season is four hundred. The last entry in the ninth ward's column is in season three hundred and fifty-five.

*Three hundred and fifty-five*, said Orle, writing it down. "That is forty-five years back. Mr. Skell, you told a boy from a firm on Thursday last that it was the four-and-fiftieth, maybe."

"It's the three hundred and fifty-fifth, yes."

"Maybe."

"I've never had cause to look at the flyleaf."

"You have now," said Orle, "and you have looked at it, and I am going to write down that you have, and the writing down is the only part of this that anybody can use."

Then she asked the second question, and it is the question that everything since is built on, and it is not *what happened*, and it is not *why was it left*, and it is not *who benefits*.

**It is: who filled it in, and who stopped.**

Skell went to the book and put his thumb on a line, and stopped, and said, "It's not a person, ma'am. It's a stub."

"Show me the stub."

The stub is a third of a page on a perforation at the head of the season's line, and the ninth ward's stub for the season of three hundred and fifty-five was still in the book, which is what a stub is when nobody has detached it.

There were two things on it. On the printed side, nothing at all. On the cell's own routing side, which is the inside face and which nobody in a hundred and forty years has had a reason to read, a hand that is not the clerk's and is not a district's, in a good copperplate, in ink:

*Issued to the ninth ward's district office. J. Marchant to follow.*

"That is a cell hand," said Skell, after a moment. "That is a routing note. That is what we did. We wrote the name of the man who was going to chase the stub."

"Where is Marchant's name?"

"In the duty book."

The duty book is a thin volume on a shelf and it is a list of who was in the cell in which season, and it is kept because a person in a public building has to be findable, and it ends.

*... Marsh, Jno. clerk (temp.) — discharged, end of season 355.*

**"The stub says Marchant," said Skell. "The book says Marsh. One of them is a man's own name and one of them is a clerk's shorthand for it, and I could not tell you which, and I have not got four hours and neither have you."**

"Then put that in the minute as well," said Orle, "and do not rule it out. A man looking for a reason to object is going to find it, and I would rather he found it here."**

**Discharged, end of season three hundred and fifty-five.** Which is the whole of it. Not a death, not a scandal, not a dismissal with a reason. A temporary appointment of about four years, at a rate of pay a technical cell could afford, doing the work of chasing up nine ward stubs once a season, and in the season of three hundred and fifty-five he was not discharged at the end of it because he had gone, and the season of three hundred and fifty-six came round, and there was no note, and nobody wrote to any district.

"Now," said Sabin Orle. "Is there a rule."

"There is a rule."

"Read it out."

Skell read it out, from a volume under the press that was not the register, in the voice of a man reading a thing he had read once.

*That the stub of each season shall be followed in the term following, and where a stub is not returned the district shall be written to twice.*

"Twice."

"Twice. And where a stub is not returned after the second writing it shall be reported to the Board."

"When was a district last written to about a stub?"

"I don't know."

"Mr. Skell."

"There is a box," said Skell, "for the second writings. It's in the press under the register. Nobody's ever asked me for the box."

It took her four minutes to go through it. It was a wooden box with a lid and a card on the lid and the card said *stub followings — second writings*.

**It was empty, and it had a card on it, and the card was not a form, and there was no rule requiring anybody ever to report the box being empty to the Board, and the rule that required a report was the one rule in that volume with no book behind it.**

---

Here is the shape of it and it took her about four minutes standing in a cold room and it is worse than a conspiracy and she wrote that down on the back of the memorandum in a hand that was not her official one.

**Nobody conspired.**

A man was employed for four years to write to nine districts once a season and to chase up nine stubs and to file nine replies in a press. He left — for a wage, for a posting, for a wife, for a nursery, for anything, and the duty book says *discharged* because that is the word the book has and not because anybody decided anything.

The season came round. The book came out of the press, because the book comes out of the press in the autumn and it is not a special event, it is a Tuesday in the autumn and everybody who has ever worked in that building knows the day. The stub went to the district office. It came back in a fortnight or it did not come back. And on the season after that, the same thing, and the season after that, for forty-five years, once a season, nine districts, and four hundred and five stubs that did not come back.

And the reason nobody has ever noticed, and the reason it is worse than a conspiracy, is that **every single person in that chain was paid to do their part and did their part.**

The clerk issued the stub. The clerk's temporary colleague did not chase it, because he had gone. Nobody noticed the colleague had gone, because the duty book is a list and a list is not a search. The box was for the second writings and the second writings were in the second writings box and the box is on a shelf and a shelf in a press is a place where a thing goes to be correct. The Board was not told because reporting a district that had not returned a form is not a matter for a Board, and the rule was written in a volume about procedure and a rule about procedure is a rule about procedure.

Nine districts, forty-five years, four hundred and five stubs, one box, one line of a duty book, and one copperplate hand that wrote *J. Marchant to follow* and never once had to.

**And nobody wrote down that they had stopped asking, and that is the whole of it, and there is no form on which to write it down, and if there were a form, the person who had filled it in had gone.**

---

**And then, at about a quarter to eleven, she found the other half of it, and it is the half that made it the middle of a fortnight.**

The Council's technical cell keeps an incoming tray. It is a shallow tray on the end wall by the door and everything that comes in the gate addressed to the cell goes into it, and it is emptied on a Monday by whoever is on, and anything that wants a form gets a form.

In the tray, under a fortnight of the Council's own circulars and a water office abstract and a thing about a broken skylight, was a folded request on the paper of a firm, dated the **thirteenth day of Sleetmonth**, which was three weeks old.

*We ask the technical cell for the standing schedule of what each ward is able to do about its water.*

And on the underside of the same tray, in pencil, a clerk's own routing mark, in the ordinary way, in the ordinary hand, and it said the request had been put to the press and answered and sent, and the answer had gone out on the **third day of Drownmonth** in a form, in a fair hand, and had been collected.

Which is correct. Every step of it is correct. A request came, a form went, a boy collected it, and the boy's firm has it in a folder in a satchel with the date on it in his own hand.

**And it is a form with the heading filled in and the season filled in and nothing whatever underneath, because the cell's procedure for a request it cannot answer is to send out the form, and the form is a stub in a book that has never been in the same room as a district, and so the answer is the question, and the answer went out into the west of the city in a boy's satchel on the Friday morning, and the boy's firm has four hundred yards of paper about nine wards and not one line that a district wrote about itself.**

Orle sat down on the edge of a table in a cold room and put both hands flat on the top of it.

"There is a sentence," she said, "and I am going to write it down, and I am going to write the same sentence twice, and I am not going to editorialise either of them, and I would like Mr. Skell to notice that I am writing them in the same hand and about two institutions, and that neither of them is a ward."

She wrote:

*It is a finding of this Council's record that the Council has not asked any district in forty-five years.*

And underneath it, about a minute later, on the same page:

*It is a finding of this Council's record that the Cloud Exchange has not asked any district in nineteen years.*

And then she read them both back to the assessor who had been standing there for an hour, in the ordinary order of business, and said, "That is the shape of the next three years and I have not got anything else and I am not going to try."

---

**She did three things that morning and all three of them were correct and one of them was against her own house, and she has never denied that the third was the expensive one.**

**The first, at twenty past eleven, in one sentence, as promised.** *This memorandum is ruled inadmissible as evidence of any cost and admissible as a document of a party's belief, and the two rulings are made in the same breath and the second is not a softening of the first.* The memorandum was read into the file on its own paper, in an envelope, with the sender's name on the index, and the file number went into a book, and it was the first item in that book in eleven years that had come from a person rather than from a public body, and there was no form for that either, and she used a form for something else and wrote the reference in by hand.

**The second, at about half past eleven, and it is nineteen words and it is in the same register, in the same hand, on the same day, and it is the first time a technical cell has ever reported on itself.**

The ninth ward's line has been unfilled since the forty-fifth. This is a finding of this Council's own record.

Written on the flyleaf over the section, above the season three hundred and fifty-five, where anybody who opens the book for any reason whatever has to read it, in the same fair hand, in the same ink, in the same book, about three inches from a copperplate note that says *J. Marchant to follow*.

**The third, at about one o'clock, and it is the first thing the Council's technical cell has sent out of that building in a generation, and it went to three districts, and it is four paragraphs and one of them is the sentence that makes the other three possible.**

*Sir or Madam,*

*The technical cell of this Council holds a bound register, kept by hand once a season since the year the basin survey was made, with a column for each of the nine wards, in which a district is invited to set out its available responses to a season's water, with a date. The register is now produced for your inspection on request.*

*The cell is required to state that it has not asked any district to fill in that register since the season of three hundred and fifty-five, and that the fault is the cell's, and not any district's.*

*The cell further states that it is aware that a district is under no obligation whatever to answer, that the register is a record of what has been written down and is not a record of what was available, and that this is stated here in terms so that no reader of the register in future may be misled.*

*A district may refuse. A refusal will be minuted in the register as a refusal, with its date and its words, and will be entered against the ward's line and not elsewhere.*

*Sabin Orle, assessor, technical cell.*

Fourth ward. Sixth ward. Ninth ward.

---

**And then at about twenty past one, in a corridor on the ground floor, she said two sentences to a reader, and neither of them was about the register, and he had not asked.**

He was on a bench with a coat on, because a maintenance hand on this ridge has a bench in a corridor in any building he is standing in, and he had come in about a quarter past one to see a flat sheet of Council paper with nothing on it, which is not his business, and which had been the business of a warden and a boy of nineteen and a records officer.

"Mr. Vale. Two things and then I have a review."

"Go on."

"I minuted a thing about you on the twenty-first of Sleetmonth, in a lane, outside an assembly room, in front of four people. I wrote it down as a limitation on the reader and not as a finding, and I have not minuted it as a finding, and it is not going to become a finding, and you did not have to tell me it and I would have had it either way, because it is on the record of what a reader said in a lane and a lane is a place where people say things. That is the first thing and it is the whole of it."

"Thank you."

"The second." She did not slow down. "**If I sent for you, you would give evidence about a form you have never seen.**"

"Yes."

"I know you would," said Sabin Orle. "That is exactly why I am telling you now, on a bench, in a corridor, rather than asking you for it in a room on a Tuesday. If I had asked, you would have said yes, and the yes would have cost you the only thing you have left, which is that nobody can say you said yes to anything. I am not going to take that off you because I have got a memorandum with a number in it and no claimant."

He looked up at her for a second.

"The memorandum's from an underwriter," he said.

"Yes."

"Twenty-nine."

"Yes."

"Then he's about four weeks ahead of me and he does not know it," said Marek Vale, "and the number in it is the only one anybody in this basin has, and if it gets into a review with a schedule attached to it, somebody is going to find a way to make a ward sign for it, and the ward will sign, because the ward has never once been asked for anything and it is very tired."

"That," said Sabin Orle, "is the second sentence I have been waiting nine days for, and I am not going to repeat it, and I am not going to minute it either, and I would be obliged if you would not tell anybody you said it, because if you tell anybody it was said then it has been asked for, and I did not ask."
