# Chapter 85 — A Ward's Own Hours

The ninth ward works its own hours on the fourth day of Miremonth the way it has worked everything since the autumn: by copying them out in a fair hand and putting them where the children walk.

The trace result is on four shutters by seven in the morning — the tannery side of Tannery Walk, the plate glass case at the Sump Lane school, the district office shutter, and the low blocks', where Ivy Tranter put them in four minutes on the Tuesday and has kept them ever since without being thanked.

The bend on the low ground on the tenth day of the new month is at about half past one. It arrives at the south end of the freight apron at about eleven minutes past four. The two are four hours and ten minutes apart on the water. The nine minutes about Route, doubled or single, is on every copy, because Neris Kade is not having anybody in the third block thinking they have got a clean hour.

**Both readings stand. Neither has been withdrawn. The board is not a notice, and the size of a disagreement is published for the first time in the history of the ward.**

At about eight forty adults stand in front of the school gate shutter in the rain, which is not doing anything remarkable, and read each other's copies to each other, and a woman says to a man she has known thirty years that nine minutes is not nothing, that is the chain, and he agrees, because it is.

Beside them a man of about fifty has the tannery copy in his hand and is not agreeing to it.

"Eleven minutes past four," he says. "I have been at the Sump end since nine o'clock for nine years and it is never four minutes past anything. It is four, and it is four because the water is four."

"Then the nine minutes is the chain," says the woman, "and I am not saying it is not."

"It is the chain at the north end and it is nothing at the Sump end and you have printed both on one shutter as though a man could be in two places."

He is not being clever and he is not being difficult. He has read a thing that says the reach arrives four hours and ten minutes after it leaves, and he has a business that opens when it leaves, and the two are on the same piece of glass, and he has understood both of them, and the understanding is now a matter of public record on Tannery Walk in the rain, and nobody corrects him, because he is right about the four and the shutter is right about the eleven minutes, and the ward printed both.

"Write it down," says Ivy Tranter to him, with a pencil, which she produces from the cloth with the tea in it, and he writes his name and his nine years and the four o'clock on the back of his own copy, and the copy is his, and the district has no power over it and does not want any.

It is still on the shutter an hour later, under the hours, in a different hand, and it is the first thing on any board in this ward that a man has added to the ward's own notice, and the ward has left it up.

Neris comes down at half past eight with a pencil note in her pocket and reads the shutter on the way without needing anybody to say anything. She does not touch it. Her rule about touching a notice in public still holds: if she puts her hand on a thing she owns it in front of a room, and she cannot afford to own anything this month.

"Miss Kade." Ivy Tranter, with the tea in a cloth. "The low blocks' wants the hours again for the new month?"

"Yes. All four. The hours and not the movements, and the nine minutes as well, and if anybody adds to it again, leave it there and tell me, and do not take it down." She looks at the shutter. "And get them to the school before eight, because the children walk up."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Thank you," says Neris Kade, and Ivy says you're welcome the way she says it, as though it were a small joke, and it is, and it is the only joke anybody makes in the ward before nine.

---

Sable Orin comes down the ladder at about nine with her satchel and her strip of glass, and counts the week's shilling on the top step without hurrying, because she has stopped hurrying about money in front of him.

Marek Vale is at the foot of it with his hands in his pockets, about one pound twelve in his coat if anybody counted, which nobody does, and eleven shillings and a shilling gone out of it in coin on this ladder.

**She declined a second payment inside the same week because the retainer covers the week, and she said so in one sentence, and the consent problem is not solved by being paid with a term, and she knows it.**

"The reading goes on a board a warden is welcome to read, free," she says, "and I do not pass it to anybody who has to pay for it, and it goes up before the claim. That is the term and it is not an argument and I am insufferable about it."

"It is your term," says Marek. "And I have stopped arguing with it and I have not stopped believing it is wrong, and both of those are on the sheet, and a form that says both is worth more than a form that says one."

"You have written down the fee," says Sable Orin. "You have not written down one of my jokes and you intend to keep all four of them."

She has been on that ladder since the second of Drownmonth and the top step is a cast step with a rail on three sides and the tower is four miles from the shop, and she comes down it in the rain with a strip of glass in a sleeve because a strip of glass is worth more than the walk and she has never once dropped one.

They walk down Tannery Walk together past the shutter with both their names on it, and neither of them touches it, because neither of them can withdraw it and both of them are named on it, and it is the first thing either of them has written down that could be produced against them.

**A reader is a person, and a person has an address, and now she has one at eight o'clock in the morning by an appointment, and there is no version of this month where the appointment is not in a book, and the book is in an office, and she has never in her life been in an office and been hard to find.**

"You do not get to be a private man in the middle of somebody's trace," she says, not unkindly, the way she said it on the Monday night. "That is the whole of the second reader."

"I know," says Marek Vale. "Two channels and somebody else's competence at one of them. I have been trying to do it with my hands since the spring."

---

In the district office at about half past nine, after she has gone up the ladder for the morning, Ivy Tranter puts the tea down on the edge of the clerk's desk and asks the question she has been carrying up and down Tannery Walk for four days.

"Miss Kade. A man come round the low blocks on Friday with a list of shutters."

"What did he want?"

"He wanted to know who paid for the plate at the school and who was named on it." Ivy Tranter has the pencil in her hand and the tea in the cloth and neither of them is doing anything. "I said the ward. He said the ward pays nothing, because the ward's not on the list, and the list is the firm's, and the firm charges threepence a week for a shutter, and I said the ward's not buying a shutter, and he said the plate's on the firm's list, and the firm charges for a plate with a name on it, and it isn't the ward's name, it's yours and the reader's."

Neris Kade does not look up from the return.

"What did you say?"

"I said the ward doesn't buy anything off a firm that comes to a door," says Ivy Tranter. "And he wrote the school case down anyway, in a book, with the week on it, and he was very pleasant about it, and I did not know whether to be pleased or not and I have not slept properly over it."

"He was doing his job," says Neris Kade. "Threepence a week is thirteen shillings a year. It is one pound six for two years for a piece of glass with two names on it, and the names are on it because we put them on it, and the day somebody asks me whether two people may be named on a shutter in my ward I will not have an answer ready and that is a thing I ought to have had an answer ready for since the Bellweather month."

"Am I to tell him nothing?"

"Tell him the ward buys nothing off a door," says Neris Kade, "and do not tell a reader anything, and do not tell me anything else, and I will deal with it after the hearing or I will not deal with it at all, and both of those are the same thing this month."

She says it to a clerk's desk and not to Ivy Tranter, and Ivy Tranter, who has been on four of these shutters and been thanked on none of them, nods and takes the tea away, and does not think about it again until a Tuesday in a different month, when a firm with a list will be somewhere it should not be.

---

On the south bank, at about ten, Garrick Dellow oils the chain across his shed door and checks the papers on the paint drum under it.

The two sheets are still there, in a fair round hand, on ordinary paper: the crew of four and a foreman, the tackle, the two carts, the order of the ground from the N end, the spoil into the third block's drains, the clearing at 3/-, 3/6 and 4/1, a hundred and sixty yards on a good forty minutes and a hundred and twenty on a short one, and at the foot the line about what forty minutes is worth, eleven times, no more.

**Nobody has asked him for them. No clerk, no assessor, no reader, no ward has mentioned them. They are two sheets of ordinary paper on a paint drum, and he has sent them to nobody.**

He shifts them half an inch so the damp does not take the edge, and puts the chain back across the door, and goes out onto the flat with a staff, because the flat is a thing he can stand on without asking anybody.

His gang is four and a foreman, and the ward's is six with a water steward, and Hearthward's is four men and a foreman, and Ferrygate's rota has six names and a gang of generally five, and the sixth ward's is a lighterman and two men and four lighters and a chain barge, and a gang of eleven is a contractor's, and none of those numbers has ever migrated and none of them migrates today.

The load in the shed is unchanged: about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands, in its second consecutive season. The Lower Sill wall is in its third consecutive deferred season. The table on the water office's wall says two years and about nine months, and the line between the two pegs cannot be produced by anybody.

He stands there for a while with the rain coming off the apron roof, and says nothing to the empty shed, because he has already said the thing he has to say, and it is on the paper: because the day somebody asks me for it, it stops being mine.
