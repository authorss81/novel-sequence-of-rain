# Chapter 73 — What A Preparation Costs

The book is on a shelf in a cold shed on the south bank and you have to go down about eleven steps to get to it, and the door is chained, and a man of sixty-eight came down the steps on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-third day of Drownmonth with a chain over his shoulder and let him in without asking a single question, which is the reason Tobias Mear had come and which is also the reason nobody had ever asked him to.

"Mr. Petrie."

"You said you'd come and read the book."

"I did say I'd come and read the book. I want to be careful about what I said, though, because I've been thinking about it for four days." Mear took his hat off and did not put it back on, which is a thing a young man does in a shed when he has been told to be careful. "I said I'd come and read the book. I did not say I'd take a figure off you, and I have got two figures in my hand already and I want you to know before we start that one of them is mine and one of them is the Council's, and neither of them is out of that book."

Petrie put the kettle on at the end of the bench, which is a cold morning.

---

**The level book is a working book and it is about nine inches by six and it is a hundred and forty years old and it has been in this shed the whole time, and it is not a record and it is not compellable and it is worth nothing.**

It has four columns. The first is the date. The second is the reading off the post, taken with a chain and a staff by whoever is on the flat, and it is a number of inches, and the numbers go back in an unbroken run to about 1888 in a hand that is not Petrie's and are legible for all of it. The third is what the sky was doing. The fourth is a column headed *work done, or reason no work*, and for about thirty years it had been empty, and a month ago Neris Kade had written one line in a copy of this book, in a warden's hand, in the *no work* half of it, which is the first line in the basin that says a repair was not done and why, and Petrie had not taken the copy and had nowhere to put it.

Mear read the second column from the front.

He read it for about an hour and a half. He did not do it quickly and he did not say anything for the first fifty minutes, and Petrie sat on an upturned crate and drank his tea and let a stranger read his book, which is not a thing that has ever happened.

The reading on a Wednesday in the winter of 1958 is eleven inches over the mark. The reading on a Tuesday in the spring of 1963 is nine. **The reading on the morning of the thirtieth of the Bellweather month, four rows from the back, in a hand that is neither Petrie's nor his father's, and which somebody in a district office has since copied into a fair hand, says *forty min* and nothing else — and there is nothing under that line in any column, and the column under it is the *work done, or reason no work* column, and it is empty in that row, and it was empty in that row in 1888.**

He read that four times.

"Twentieth of November, sixty-eight." He turned back about a hundred pages. "Eleven inches. And a fortnight later, nine. And the one after that, ten. And the one after that." He looked up. "Mr. Petrie, this book is not a plan."

"No," said Petrie. "It never has been. It's the water."

"I know it's the water. **I'm saying the water is in here nine hundred times and the plan is not in here once, and I have been in this business six years and I have never seen a single person in this basin write down what they were going to do.**"

Petrie took the glasses off and cleaned them on his sleeve, which he does about four times a day.

"You've come down that hill road twice asking me what a reach is worth and I told you both times to come and read the book, and I did not tell you what the book was for."

"Then what is it for."

"It's for afterwards," said Petrie. "It settles a row. There was a row in 'sixty-one about a cellar and it was settled because there was a number in a book with a date against it, and a chain and a staff in the hand of a man nobody could argue with. That's the whole of what that book does. Nobody's ever put a plan in one and nobody's ever asked me for one and there is not a form."

**And Tobias Mear sat on a paint drum in a shed on the south bank at about eleven o'clock in the morning on the twenty-third day of Drownmonth and understood the entire shape of the last eleven weeks of his own life, and it took him about four seconds, and then he thought: say it to the man first, and then say it on paper, and then send it to the assessor, and do all three before this afternoon is out.**

He asked for a form. Petrie said there was not one. He asked for the back of anything at all. Petrie gave him the back of a Council form, a 1961 season's, that had come into the shed with a load of timber in the autumn and been used as a pallet cover, and which still had about a third of a printing on it.

---

**So he did it on the back of that Council form, in pencil, over about four hours, sitting on a paint drum in the cold, with a girl from the district's office bringing him tea he did not want and taking it away again and coming back with it, and Petrie going in and out of the shed all afternoon for reasons of his own and not once looking at what was being written on the back of a form in his own doorway.**

*The cost of a preparation.*
*1. The men. Six at the district rate for a day. Call it eleven pence.*
*2. The gear. A gang for the pump and its gear. Call it five pence.*
*3. The load. Nothing. Bought and paid. Its cost of being there is the cost of turning up.*
*4. The thing you did not do.*

He came back to number four eleven times and it is the whole of the argument that is going to run through the rest of this quarter, in this basin and in the room upstairs.

*4. The thing you did not do. Which is not a sum. Which is a number of mornings, and the mornings do not have a price and the price of them is not zero, and I cannot write it, and I have tried four times this morning and the best I have got is a word and the word is season.*

*So: a preparation costs what you spend on it plus what you did not do. The first term is in Mear's memorandum of the seventeenth of Sleetmonth and is one and four. The second term is the wall, and the timber in the shed, and the order one hundred and fourteen, and the table on a wall that has moved from two years and about eleven months to two years and about nine months, and the crew of eleven stood down at dawn and paid for the day and a half, and about nine hundred pounds of timber in four iron bands in a shed for a second consecutive season.*

*And here is the whole of it and I have checked it four ways and it does not change:*

***A preparation is a plan. A plan has a name on it. So a preparation can only ever be priced by the party that intended it.***

*In this basin that is four sorts of party. A ward. A yard. A lighterman. A salvage man. Four.*

*Every one of the other hundred and forty thousand people in this basin is in somebody else's plan. The two hundred and forty children at the Sump Lane school are in four plans and are named in none of them. The woman on the ground floor of the third block is in the ward's plan and is named in it, and the ward's plan says nine men to a flat by nine in the morning, and it does not say her, and that is not an oversight, that is what a plan is. Forty of those children walk up to that school from the low blocks. I have counted them three times in a book of my own because I did not believe the first figure, and I have got forty every time and the district's own figure is forty and I have put the two side by side in my book and they are the same number, which in this basin is not a thing you can say often.*

*So the compensation the Council is looking for — and it is looking for it, I have seen the minute, it is in a file on the ground floor and it says a document nobody can produce is not thereby a loss nobody has had — the compensation it is looking for is money for a season. And there are four parties in this basin who can put a season in a book, and one of them is a man of sixty-eight who has just given me a level book and would not give me a figure out of it if I asked him twice, and the other three have not been asked.*

*This is not a claim and it is not a complaint. I am not a district and I am owed nothing. I am twenty-nine and I have been an underwriter for six years and I work for a firm that has just put a number on a wall for a river because a market which has struck a line through twice cannot sell what is behind it, and I am not going to pretend to you that the number on that wall is right. It is a fair price for a hull. I priced it. It is five and a half times the district's, and the district's is a fair figure for the work, and the work is not the loss.*

*Send it anywhere you like. I do not mind.*

---

**And then he did the part that was actually work, and it took him the best part of an hour, and it is why the paper is four hundred words and not two hundred:**

*What would a thing that works have to look like. I have written it out to see whether I can write it out.*

*One. Somebody who lost something, named. A ward is named. A man is named. A road is not named.*

*Two. That somebody's own account of what they meant to do, in a hand, with a date on it, produced before anybody needed it and not produced because anybody asked.*

*Three. A price. Whose price? That is the whole difficulty and I have got round it. Not the Council's and not the firm's and not mine. **The loser's own. There is no other number in the world and there never has been, and every argument I have had for four years with myself about this has been an argument about how to avoid it.***

*And the loser may put down a number that is not a price, and then the Council cannot pay it, because a Council pays a sum.*

*So: four. Either the loser's figure is a sum and it is his, or it is a plan and there is no sum, and the moment you make him produce a sum you have made him into a party and a party can be refused and can be cross-examined and can be asked what he meant, and the moment you accept a plan you have accepted that you are going to compensate a man's season rather than a man's morning, and the season is a year and there is no rate in this basin for a year.*

*I do not think that is a reason not to do it. I think that is what it costs, and I think the person who is going to have to sign it is a woman with eleven years in a technical cell and a book that expires at the end of the financial year, and I would like her to have about six weeks to look at it before anybody asks her for a ruling.*

---

Petrie read it standing up, with his reading glasses in his hand, which he has not done since about 1963.

He read it twice. Then he said the only thing he said in about four hours, and he said it to the arithmetic and not to the boy.

"The child that walks up," he said. "Forty."

"Forty walk up. There are two hundred and forty in the building."

"Aye." He put the paper down on the bench and squared it, which he does with everything. "There's a reason there's no register of that either, son. There's a reason there's no form. It's not that nobody's missed it out." He was quiet for a bit. "It's that a thing you write down, you can be asked to produce. And a thing nobody writes down, you cannot be asked anything about, and that's the safest place on earth to keep a boy of nine."

Then he said the second thing, at the door, with the kettle in his hand, and it was not about the paper at all and it is the reason Mear came back to this shed twice more before the end of the month.

"You'll not put my book in anything."

"No."

"You'll not put my name in anything either."

"Mr. Petrie —"

"**I know what you're doing and it's all right and I'm not stopping you, and you'd do it with or without me and I'd rather you did it with the book on the bench and me in the room**," said the shed foreman, "because a man that takes it out of here and writes it up in a warm room has had to write down where he got it, and where he got it is a shed with a chain on the door, and a warm room is not going to be able to check that and you know it as well as I do." He put the kettle down. "You'll not be the first one that's wanted a figure out of me and you'll be the first one that's gone away without one, and I'd say that to you in front of the Book, and the Book's not a person so it doesn't count as me saying it."

---

**At four o'clock Mear walked up the hill road to the ridge with the Council's postmark still on the form because he had not had time to go down to the post, and he put it in the box at the basin house and it went to Sabin Orle on the Monday morning at about half past nine, in an envelope with nothing on it, for the second time in a month.**

It sat in the incoming tray of the technical cell for about thirty hours, which is nothing; the tray of a building where nothing has come from a person rather than a public body for eleven years is not checked.

And then Sabin Orle read it in her room with a window that looks out of the eastern ridge and at nothing else whatsoever, and she read it, and she said a sentence out loud to a clerk, and the clerk wrote it down because that is what a clerk does.

"He is not a district," she said. "**He is the first person in this basin who has put a figure in a document that is not a price and not a claim, and it is going to be the most useful paper in this building, and I am not going to be able to use it for about six weeks.**"

She put it in the file with the memorandum of the seventeenth of Sleetmonth, and she wrote on the tab, in ink, in her own hand, the first line and nothing else:

*What a preparation costs. T. Mear, in his twenty-ninth year. Not a claim. Not a district.*

And under the tab, in the same ink and smaller, because she is a woman who has read four refusals in a month and does not intend to be refused by a clerk of her own building:

*The fourth party has not been asked.*
