# Chapter 91 — The Asking

On the morning of the fifteenth of Miremonth Tobias Mear walks up the hill road to the south bank with a Council form in his pocket and a sentence in his head that he has counted three times because he did not believe the first figure.

The sentence is eleven lines on the back of a 1961 Council form in pencil: a preparation is a plan, a plan has a name on it, so a preparation can only ever be priced by the party that intended it — a ward, a yard, a lighterman, a salvage man — and everybody else in the basin is in somebody else's plan.

**The fourth party has not been asked. That is written on the tab in the assessor's file in her own hand, and it is the reason he is walking up the hill.**

Petrie is in the shed with a chain over his shoulder and a kettle on at the end of the bench, reading a level book the way other men read a newspaper.

"Morning."

"Morning. Is Mr. Dellow about?"

"He's on the flat." Petrie does not look up. "Second drum from the end's got his chain over it. If it's papers you're after, they're his, and you'll want him, not me."

Mear stands there for a moment with the form in his pocket and the whole of the compensation problem in his head, and then he says it, because there is no other way to say it:

"Mr. Dellow. May I ask you for your papers?"

**That is the asking. Somebody asks for them, and the asking is what stops them being his, and he has said so out loud, and now it happens, in a shed, with a clerk's pencil note behind it and an assessor's file behind that.**

Dellow comes in off the flat at about ten with mud on his boots and a staff in his hand, forty-four, twenty years of asking a ward for nothing except the water to go off its land, a bad back, two hundred yards, eleven windows in eleven years.

He looks at Mear for a long time. Then he lifts the chain off the second paint drum from the end and picks up the two sheets of ordinary paper in a fair round hand, and holds them for a moment the way a man holds a thing he has had in his head for eleven years and in a book for one night.

"Because the day somebody asks me for it, it stops being mine," he says. "You'd better take them to the room. And tell the woman I'll wait."

---

In the hearing room at two o'clock the assessor puts the two sheets on the trestle beside the ledger, and the clerk dates them, and the room is quieter than it was yesterday, because everybody in it knows what a priced preparation looks like and nobody has ever seen one.

**Here is the whole of it, quoted in full, because it is the only document in this volume that prices a preparation, and it is a salvage man's shopping list:**

*What forty minutes is worth to me, and what I do with it, being the Bellweather reach at the south end of the freight apron, in the time the water is off the flat.*

*Crew: four men and a foreman. Mine, not the ward's, and the ward's is six with a water steward and that is a different number and I have never once pretended otherwise.*

*Tackle: chain, winch, two bars, the lot's on the flat in a hut and it is roped.*

*Two carts, my own, and a boy on a cart if there's time, and there is not time for a boy.*

*Order of the ground: N end first, always N end first, because if I go in at the S end I put the silt over the copper and then I've got the copper in the cart and the copper in the cart is worth nothing. This is the whole of what forty minutes is for and it is not about the yardage.*

*Spoil goes into the third block's drains and I pay for clearing them after, every time, to the district's man and a cart, and it has been 3/-, 3/6, 4/1 over eleven years and I have written all forty-one of them down.*

*Yardage on a good forty minutes: a hundred and sixty, if the water is off at the top and the chain's hooked at the S bend. Less if it's short. It is a hundred and twenty when it's short and I have had both in one month. Eleven years, forty-one times, a hundred and sixty at the best of it and a hundred and twenty at the worst, and the average of it is about a hundred and forty and I have never once been asked what a reach is worth and I could tell you what my own is worth in a minute.*

*So: forty minutes is worth to me a hundred and sixty yards of the lowest ground in the ninth ward, stripped and carted, with the drains clear by the following Tuesday. At the worst of it, a hundred and twenty. In eleven years, forty-one times.*

*The firm's price is 7/6. My own figure for the work is the clearing, the crew and the tackle, and it is nowhere near 7/6 and it is not supposed to be, because 7/6 is a price for a barge coming and a crew standing off and a hull's hire and I am not buying that, I am not using it. I have never said that to anybody and I have thought about it every time I have paid it.*

*This is the first time I have written any of it down and I have written it because a woman said in a room on the fifteenth that the only time anybody lets you write it down is before you need it, and she is right, and I would not have remembered the sentence if it had not been said.*

*— G. Dellow.*

The clerk finishes, and puts the pen down, and the assessor rules:

"Admissible. An ordinary commercial document, in an ordinary hand, on ordinary paper, showing exactly what a man intended to do in a reach with forty minutes. The only priced preparation anybody in this basin has put on paper."

**And then, in four minutes, correctly, with the clerk writing it down, because a claim requires a sponsor and a sponsor must be a district and a freeholder is not a district:**

"Mr. Dellow cannot be a claimant in any of the three definitions in use in this basin. There is no box in the Charter for a fella who isn't a ward, and no form for it, and no bell for it, and no copy but the one. The schedule is retained as the measure. The man is refused."

Dellow, standing at the back in a good coat with his hat in his hand, nods once. He said on the fifteenth, at the back of a room, that when he took eleven windows off this ground in eleven years nobody ever came to the door to ask him for one. Now somebody has come to the door, and the paper has stopped being his, and he is refused, and he does not make a fuss on the ward's account either.

**He is the only person in the basin who has voluntarily said he would rather the ground were a place than a resource, and he is a man who makes his living from the other thing.**

Neris Kade, two seats down, looks at her own hands. Her seven lines were admitted and not compellable. His two sheets are admitted and priced. Hers cannot move the ledger. His can measure it and cannot be paid from it. That is the volume's last joke and it is not funny.
