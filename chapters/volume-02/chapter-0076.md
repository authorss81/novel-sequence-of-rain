# Chapter 76 — The Yard's Own Figure

The notice went up on the counting floor on the Monday morning and the free district sheet went out on the Tuesday morning and the quotation hall got the plate on the Tuesday afternoon, and by the Wednesday the Kestrel corridor was on it in three places and a window was being sold cut out of the yard's own published figures, exactly as it had been in the autumn when Tarin Senn stood in a freezing yard at twenty to eight in the morning and said four things and then said a fifth, and the fifth was:

*A window cut from this yard's published figures carries this yard's name on it, and the hour and date of the line it was cut from, and neither can be removed by any party.*

She said it in about a quarter of an hour and the firm accepted it in a quarter of an hour in a hand that was not theirs, and it cost the firm nothing, and it went on the list under the eleven lines, and it has been the reason for every decent thing that has happened to that yard for four months and it has never once cost her a penny.

**And on the Friday morning of the twenty-fifth day of Drownmonth she read the corridor line on the free sheet, in a tobacconist's on Tannery Walk, where it was posted at eight, and she read the attribution line under it, and she had a very quiet half hour in an office over a chandlery.**

*KESTREL CORRIDOR, morning of the 27th, 9:40. 11/4 plus 2/- a month. Cut from the line marked KESTREL YARDS, board of the 3rd, column 2. Yard's name and the hour and date of the line it was cut from are carried by the firm and cannot be removed by any party.*

There it was. In a fair hand, in a printing house, on a press a council is not allowed to inspect, four streets from a poor ward that cannot read the Office's own bulletin.

**The yard's name. The hour. The date of the line. Not removable.**

She had spent the autumn being told, by everybody who came into her yard, that publishing your own figures hands the market its raw material and there is no law against it and no way to stop it and you did it anyway because you had a fifth condition and a pride. And she had said, in a yard, to a shift foreman, *the four lines are about the yard's name and not one of them is about our crew.* She had said it and she had been right and she had never once been thanked for it and she had not wanted to be.

And there it was, printed, four streets away, in the middle of a Monday, in front of anybody who wanted to read it.

---

**The line she had not drawn, and had known for four months that she had not drawn, is the one that pays.**

A condition about attribution is a condition about *naming*. It says: this number is mine, and here is where it came from, and you may not pretend otherwise. It does not say a word about the only thing that has ever moved in exchange for a fact in this entire business, which is money, and **a name on a window is not a fee, and a yard that has published its figures for four years for nothing and has had its name printed on a market's product for a month for nothing has just discovered that its fifth condition has been earning nothing on its behalf for every working day since the tenth of Sleetmonth.**

Nora Bragg came in at about half past nine, having been the yard's clerk for eleven years, and she knows what Tarin Senn's face does at half past nine.

"I've read it," said Bragg.

"So have I."

"It's the first time I've seen our name on that firm's paper for anything that isn't a refusal."

"It is." Senn stood up and turned round and looked at the five conditions on the board, in chalk, in the yard's own hand, which have been there since the autumn. "It is the first time in nineteen years that anybody has put our name on a piece of paper as a *source* instead of as a *refuser*."

"Will you get paid for it?"

"I don't know," said Tarin Senn. "**And that is the interesting part and you may write it down: I have spent four hundred and six pounds of coal-charter money in the Bellweather month on the specific position that this yard is not a counterparty to anybody's schedule. If I invoice them I am a counterparty to somebody's schedule. That is the whole of what I bought.**"

"That's a good position, that is." Bragg waited, because she had served this woman for eleven years and knew what the pause was for.

"Right," said Senn. "Then we find out whether it can be done without a schedule."

---

**She did it in eleven minutes and it is four lines and it is the first invoice ever issued in the history of that firm, and the amount is two shillings, and the amount is not the point.**

*Cloud Exchange, quotation hall.*
*One window, corridor, morning of the 27th, cut from the Kestrel Yards board of the 3rd, column 2, in accordance with the fifth condition carried by the firm.*

*For the use of this yard's published figures, being the cost of the clerk's time to publish them: **2/-**.*

*The yard's name, the hour and the date of the line are carried by your form and may not be removed by any party. The yard will continue to publish, on the same terms, at the same rate, and will not supply a figure for any window not cut from its own board.*

*Kestrel Line, by T. Senn, route captain.*

*Enclosed: the board extract, the hour and date of the line, and our clerk's time sheet for the week of the 3rd, which is four hours, at sixpence.*

She read it out loud to Bragg and made her change two words in it, and it went on the Saturday morning on the ordinary post, and it is four lines and an enclosure and it cost twopence.

**And the yard sent it, which is the sentence to hold on to, because four hundred and six pounds earlier in the year was spent on not sending one.**

---

**And then she did the second thing, which is the part that is not on the invoice and which is why the invoice is not a stupid document.**

She went out to the board and stood in front of it at about ten past ten on the Tuesday morning, in the rain, with about nine men standing about, and a shift going over at the far end, and she said it out loud:

"**The fifth line is now worth two shillings.**"

Nesbitt, who has been in the Line's service for nineteen years and signs nothing: "Two bob."

"Two bob, and that is the first money that line has ever made anybody, and it is the whole of what four months of arguing has come to, and I want that written on the board next to the conditions so that whoever comes in here next can see what a condition is worth." She took the chalk and put it under the five lines in her own hand. *Fifth line: 2/- received. The condition is about the yard's name and not about payment. Both of those are on this board because they are the same board.*

"That'll go soft in a fortnight."

"It'll go soft in a fortnight," she agreed, "and it will still be there in the spring, and the point is not that the writing survives. **The point is that the writing is a receipt, and there is now a piece of paper in this city that says the Cloud Exchange paid the Kestrel Line, and there is going to be a first one somewhere and I would rather it was us and it was two bob.**"

Nobody said anything to that, and two men waiting on the slip worked rather faster for about twenty minutes afterwards, and that is the whole of the response she got, and she did not want more.

---

**The reply came on the Wednesday morning of the thirtieth, in four days, on a printed receipt with a stamp on it, and nobody argued about any part of it.**

It came up with the morning post in an ordinary bundle of about eleven letters and the yard's office has never once in eleven years had a stamped receipt come up in it, and Nora Bragg looked at the envelope for a moment before she opened it, which she could not afterwards account for.

*Received. 2/-.*

*The firm's schedule of charges has no line under which a publisher's use of a published figure falls, and the firm has considered whether to add one and has decided not to, on the ground that it has not been asked to and does not know what the line would be for.*

*A party may name its own figure. We do not compare it to ours and we will not.*

*E. Tarn, for the firm.*

**Four days. Two shillings. A refusal to add a line to a schedule of charges.**

And then, on the back of the receipt, in the same fair hand and a different pen:

*Mr. Senn — the yard will be aware that the firm's notice of the 21st concerning the Bellweather reach was made at the request of nine districts, and that the yard is named in that notice as one of the two which answered.*

*The firm has printed its own figure for the reach on three public boards and has printed the district's figure on none of them, because the firm does not have it and has never been given it, and the district which does have it is not the district which answered.*

*That is not a complaint. It is a description of the position and it is on this receipt because you are a party who will understand it.*

---

**Tarin Senn put the receipt on the board under the five conditions, in the yard's own hand, with the hour and the date on it, at about eleven o'clock on the Wednesday morning, and then stood in front of a working slip for a while with two men waiting and a shift going over and did not say anything for about forty seconds, which is long.**

Here is what she had, and she could not put any of it in any order:

The first time in nineteen years that a firm had paid a yard for anything. The first time in nineteen years that she had sent a bill. The first time that a condition she had invented for the reputation of a business had turned out to be worth exactly two shillings and could not be made worth more without becoming a schedule. **The first time in four months that a market had put her name on a thing as a source and had then told her, in writing, on the back of a receipt, that it did not have the district's figure and that the district with the figure is the one that refused.**

And a thought she did not let finish, and which arrived anyway, and which she handled the way she handles a slip: **somebody in that building has now made a note to a stranger because the stranger would understand it, and about nine hundred yards of that stranger's published figures are the reason a poor ward is on a board that a firm publishes, and the firm knows it, and has said so in writing, and the district it will not name is the one that cannot read its own notice.**

"Tarin." Bragg, at the slip. "The horn's going."

"Right." She turned round. "Put the receipt on the board and leave it there. If anybody from that firm comes in here, they can read it, and if anybody from the Exchange asks why the yard's on their sheet, they can read that too."

"What do I tell them?"

"**Nothing**," said Tarin Senn. "That's the answer to all of it. Nothing, and leave the paper up."

---

**And here is the thing she did not do, and it is worth an hour of anybody's time who has ever been in a yard at eleven o'clock on a Friday with two men waiting and a shift going over.**

She did not go down to the counting floor. She did not go and stand in front of a counter clerk and say *thank you*, which would have been the correct thing and which she had thought about for about nine seconds, and she decided against it in about four, because **a yard that says thank you to a firm on the firm's own floor has told the firm that the yard has noticed it needs to say thank you, and the firm will want to know what else the yard would say thank you for**, and by September there would be a man in that building who came to her yard once a quarter about a *retainer*, and she had spent four hundred and six pounds in the Bellweather month so that there would be no man in that building who came to her yard once a quarter about anything.

So the receipt went on the board, and the two shillings went in the tin that pays the yard's paper bill, and nothing was said, and the Line's column of differences went up on its own board that afternoon on its eightieth-odd day with fourteen days on it where the third column is not empty, and the corridor's difference that week was one hour and thirty-one minutes against the Office's 9:40, and both figures were on it, and the date was the date, and none of it was a notice.

**And the two shillings did not change one single thing about how the yard worked, and about four hundred and six pounds' worth of decisions, and it is the cheapest thing that firm has ever bought and it is the first thing anybody has ever sold it, and neither of those facts is going to be in a minute.**

"There," said Tarin Senn, at about half past four, to nobody, in an office over a chandlery with a coal charter to Ternway on the books and a second float in a hole in her slip and about two pounds eight of Marek Vale's money eleven miles away in a coat he has not been wearing since Saturday. "**That is what a fact is worth. Two bob. And I have been giving them away for four years because I did not know there was a price, and the price was never going to be offered, because nobody has ever asked.**"
