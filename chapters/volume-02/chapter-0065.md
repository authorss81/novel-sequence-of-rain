# Chapter 65 — Two Channels

The bench at Kestrel Yards is a plank on two trestles under an awning at the back of the slip, and the yard works round it, and on the Saturday morning of the twelfth day of Drownmonth Marek Vale put two glasses on it and found out that there is such a thing as being the best reader in a basin and being no use at all at the only job anybody wanted him for.

He had come in at six with Tarin Senn's permission and the yard's own instrument and a case of plate, a lamp and a cover, and the two ends of it were the yard's slip and the Exchange's reach gauge at the south bank, a hundred and forty miles down the hill road, and between the two of them was most of the water in this basin.

He had been trying to hold a trace across the two of them since the autumn of the previous year. He had tried it once, in the spring, alone, with one man at one end of it and him at the other, and it had come apart in about forty minutes, sideways, which is a specific and frightening kind of failure and not a vague one. He had said out loud in a lane outside an assembly room, to four people including a Council assessor, that he could not do it alone and would not put a district's window in the middle of it, and the assessor had written it down as a limitation on the reader and had not made it a finding, and it was on the page and it was not in a file.

It was now the Saturday, and the seventeenth of Drownmonth was five days off and the season's second back-cut was the biggest of it, and the ninth ward had a reach it was not allowed to buy and a reach it could not time.

**So he did the only thing left, which was to pay for the other end.**

---

He got it wrong twice in the first hour and both times about the ear, and both times he said nothing about it, and neither was a reading error.

A two-tower trace is two channels. You hold the local end and you hold the far end and the sequence has to run through both of them and come out the other side in an order you can name, and the whole of the difficulty is that the two ends do not agree and neither of them is the sequence.

He has one channel. That is the first thing and it is the whole of it and he said it to Ilva Sorn at about ten past seven in the morning, in a yard, in about four words, because she asked him a direct question and because he is a man who answers direct questions.

"I have got one channel, Miss Sorn."

"You have two hands."

"You have two hands and two eyes and you cannot watch two men at two ends of a rope. It is not the ear. It is a channel and it is not there, and it is not a new thing and it is not going to be a new thing, and I have known since the spring and I have said it out loud in about four rooms this quarter and I would be obliged if you would put the kettle on, because I have been on since five and the yard is extremely loud when a shift goes over."

Ilva Sorn put the kettle on. She is the yard's route officer and she signs off every launch and every window and she has been signing off other people's arithmetic since she was twenty-six, and she wrote nothing down, and she told the shift foreman about twenty minutes later that the reader on the plank had one channel and not to offer him a second pair of hands, and the shift foreman said *right*, and that is the whole of what the Kestrel yard knows about it.

And then he said the second thing, and this is the part of it that matters, and he said it in the same four words' worth of time, standing at a bench, at seven in the morning, to a woman who had asked him a direct question.

"It is a technical fact and not a body, and the two do not go into the same sentence, and I have watched four people this month put them in the same sentence and get it wrong for me."

---

**Sable Orin came out to Kestrel Yards at about half past nine on the Saturday morning on a hired horse from the low end of the city, which she had never done before, and which she mentioned once.**

She had the reading on the reach from the ninth ward's own feed and from the Exchange's level, and the two do not agree, because one is a number and the other one is a turn, and the bend on a reach is a bend in the **Release**, and a Release is a sequence fact and a level is a level fact and the only man in this basin who can name the order of the Release in the third watch on the seventeenth is a censured reader working off two instruments he does not own.

She came with a satchel and four notebooks and a strip of sequence glass she had bought rather than cut, and she put the satchel on the bench and did not sit down.

"Eleven shillings a read," she said, "and a retainer of a shilling a week, in coin, before I start, and the term."

"The term stands."

"I should think about it again, because you have not heard the rest of it." She took a slip out of the fourth notebook. It was not new. It had a crease down the middle from about four months of being carried and the ink had gone slightly brown on the folds. "**The reading goes on a board a warden is welcome to read, free, and I do not pass it to anybody who has to pay for it.**"

"And before the claim."

"And before the claim, yes. Which is the half of it you are not going to like, and I have known that since about the middle of January when I copied it out of a woman's letter to a yard, and I have decided to be insufferable about it, and I would rather you heard the whole sentence now than discovered it in a yard."

Marek Vale stood at a bench with his hands on a plank and did not answer for about eleven seconds.

"That is not a term a reader can meet," he said.

"That is a term anybody can meet."

"No. Listen to me properly, because you have been paid and I would rather not waste your morning." He turned round. "A reader's method is to see the order first, hold it, and then decide. That is the whole of it. There is no version of that where you publish at the moment of seeing. **A reading published the moment it is seen is a reading I have not yet checked**, and I would be publishing an assertion I have not made, on a shutter, in a district, to two hundred and forty children, and if it is wrong it will have been wrong for six hours while somebody acted on it, and I do not know whether a district is entitled to that. I think it probably is. I do not think I can do it twice."

"You are not deciding who is entitled to what. That is the whole of the difference between your office and mine and it is the only difference." She did not raise her voice and did not put the notebook down. "I have published a corridor and an hour and no place for two years and four good people have refused me and one of them refused me by a name and a date and the right words, and I wrote it all down in the fourth book. And I have never been wrong in public because I have never been in public. Do you know what the cost of that is? **Nobody in this city has ever seen a reading of mine that was wrong, and that is not because I am careful. It is because I have never let one out.**"

"That is the best argument anybody has made against me in four months."

"It is a trade, not an argument," said Sable Orin. "And I know what I sound like. I have thought about it, and I have decided I would rather be eleven shillings with a term on it than free with none, and the reason I have decided it is that being free with none is what I have been for two years and it got me four refusals and a fourth notebook. **And I will say the part nobody ever asks me for. A term is not a favour and a term is not consent, and I have written the term down, and if you or I or a clerk in that building ever come to believe that a term has discharged the other thing, then the term is worth nothing and so is the eleven shillings and I will take my board down myself and you may watch me do it.**"

Marek Vale took his hand off the plank.

"Get your bag open," he said. "I have got four pounds eleven in the world and eleven of it is yours and a shilling a week after that, and I am going to count it out on this bench in coin because I want you to have it in your hand before I have said the other thing."

---

He counted out eleven shillings and a shilling for the week, in sixpences and threepences and a penny, on a plank on two trestles, in coin, in advance, and she counted it and put it in the satchel and did not say thank you, and he added a third condition of his own which she did not ask for and did not want.

"The board carries the hour and the date and the place," he said. "And it carries my name and yours. And it is not a notice. There is nothing on it that says anybody must do anything, and it is not a notice and it must not become one, and if anybody in that district puts a must on it, I withdraw the whole thing and I do not care what it has cost me and I do not care what it costs you."

"That is a condition for the Office's benefit, not mine, and it is the only one anybody has ever put on a published reading in this basin, and I am not going to argue with it, and I am going to make you put it in the same place as mine, in your own hand, with the hour on it." She was already writing. "Because in about four months somebody is going to come and ask me why I put my name under a reader's working on a shutter in a poor ward, and I am going to have to be able to point at the place where he wrote down what the working was for."

**It is the first thing he has ever written down that could be produced against him, and he knew the price of it while he was saying it, and he did it anyway, and he has never in his life been able to work out afterwards whether that was courage or a filing habit.**

---

**It was wrong twice, and she caught both, and that is the reason the board exists at all.**

The first was at about eleven o'clock and it was an hour, and it was the worst kind because it was not a disagreement about a turn at all. He had the Release of the seventeenth in the third watch and he had put the bend four hours after the drop in the upper reservoir, off two level instruments and a plate, and the level instruments and the plate did not care what time it was; what cared was the *far* end, which was a glass datum in a wall a hundred and forty miles away read by a man in a shed, and a glass datum cannot be asked what hour anything is.

Sable Orin had her own instrument, bought rather than cut, and a wet cell, and forty years of nothing, and she did it by a different route entirely: she did it by reading the **Order**, and the order on the seventeenth says the Release finishes early, and an early Release that has a bend in it puts the bend where the bend goes and not where the level says it should be.

"Yours has the bend in the third watch," she said.

"Mine has the bend in the third watch."

"Your bend is at four o'clock. It is at about half past one." She put the strip down flat beside his. "And I am not a better reader than you. I have less practice. I have got a different pair of eyes and forty minutes of an order you did not read twice."

The second was worse, it came at about half past one in the afternoon, and it was a movement.

He had it as Weight arriving, late and soft, coming in on top of a Release that had not finished. She had it as Break arriving, and arriving on top of a Weight that *had* finished, which is the ordinary shape of a clearing in this basin and has been for as long as anybody has kept a record of one.

"They are not the same reading," he said.

"They are not."

"Both of them will hold a bend."

"That is what I do not like about it," said Sable Orin. "**That is the whole of what makes this hard. Yours and mine differ, and neither of them is the sequence, and the sequence is the thing that is going to be in the water on Thursday.**"

He looked at the two strips for a while.

"Two," he said.

"Two, and one of them is mine, and it is not corrected in my favour."

She wrote both corrections in her own hand on the board, with the hours on them.

*The reading above was wrong. It placed the bend four hours after the drop. The bend is in the third watch. — S. Orin, second reading, this day, this hour.*

And under it, in the same hand:

*The reading above disagreed on one movement. Weight or Break. Both readings stand. Neither has been withdrawn. — S. Orin.*

And underneath both, in a smaller hand, which is his:

*Both of the above are correct and the disagreement has not been resolved. This board is not a notice. Nobody must do anything because of it.*

**Which is the Kestrel Line's column of differences, put on a shutter in a poor ward, by a counter-reader, for nothing, and with a fee and a term on it, and neither of which anybody in this basin has ever thought of asking for.**

---

**And then, between about four and half past five on the same afternoon, in a room over a drying floor above the Ninth Baths, holding a local end and a far end at once, he saw a movement that was not his.**

It was in the middle. That is the only way to describe it and it is not a good description and he wrote nothing down about it at the time because there was nothing to write down.

Two ends held at once put a thing between them that is in neither. He had read that in a book about a stage above his own when he was twenty-four and had not believed a word of it, and he had assumed for six years that it was a figure of speech for the ordinary fact that two people watching the same water do not see the same water, and that a bend in the middle was just a disagreement with an extra step in it.

**It was not a disagreement.** A disagreement has two ends in it and he had two ends and he was holding both.

It was about a fifth of a second long, it was between the two, and it arrived before the movement it belonged to and went when the movement went, and it was the shape of a turn and it was in neither of the two sequences he was holding and it was as clear as anything he had read in his life.

It came and it went and he did not tell Sable Orin, who was in the room, and he did not write it on the cuff, and he did not put it in a drawer.

He sat with it for about four minutes, and then he got up and washed his glass and put the cover on it, and then he thought, in the ordinary way he thought about a hundred things a week, that he would tell her when he had a second opinion on it, and that there was no hurry about it, and that it was very probably nothing and was certainly not evidence of anything, and that there are about four hundred things a reader sees in a year and about four of them are real.

**He has a form in a drawer in his own rooms with nothing in it about this, and it is the third block of the cuff with no fourth block to put it in, and it is the first time that he has looked at a true thing and put it away, and he did it in a room with a woman in it, and she was working.**

---

The strain came on about the second hour of the second afternoon and it was not the same as it had ever been before, because it was two channels on one good ear and one good ear is not a channel and the other one is not there.

There is a metallic taste at the back of the tongue that lasts three days. There is a pressure in the left ear that sits about half a tone above the right and does not go away, and it has been there since the thirtieth of the Bellweather month and it will be there for the rest of his life, and after about an hour and a half of a two-ended read on the Saturday it moved, and it moved the wrong way, and he did not test it and did not tell anybody and has not tested it since.

The left side is quieter now. He knows that in the way you know a thing about a room you have been in every day for eleven years — not by measuring it, by noticing that a shelf that used to be in a certain place relative to a conversation is not.

**He did not test it. He worked out that evening that a man in his trade cannot afford to test a thing like that every week, because a man who tests it every week stops being able to do the work, and then he worked out, about a minute later, standing up with his glass in his hand, that this is precisely the argument a man tells himself when he does not want to know, and he noticed himself telling it, and he did not act on it, and he has not acted on it since.**

---

The board went up on the Saturday evening of the twelfth of Drownmonth at about seven, on the shutter of a vacant shop on the tannery side of Tannery Walk, which the district could paint on for nothing because it is a shutter and not a shop, and which is four doors from a tobacconist's at whose counter the Cloud Exchange gives away the weather twice a day for nothing.

It is two feet by three and it is painted in three hands, and none of the three is his, because he was not asked to paint and did not offer and had no right to.

Mrs Teodora Bain, the tobacconist, gave them the paint. Sergeant Ampow gave them the plank. Four women with pen and ink — one of them Mrs Ivy Tranter, who has brought a pot of tea in a cloth up a ladder at eight for four years and has never once been properly thanked — copied it out twice, once for the Sump Lane school's plate glass case and once for a shutter at the district office, in a fair hand, with the hour on it.

**A district read a reader's working for the first time in its life.** Not a forecast. Not a window. A working: an order of movements, two hours, a place, an argument about a bend, two corrections in another woman's hand with the hours on them, an unresolved disagreement about a movement, two names, and a line at the bottom saying that nobody must do anything because of it.

Forty children walk up to that school from the low blocks. Two hundred and forty are in the building. And about half a dozen adults stood in front of a shutter on the tannery side of Tannery Walk from about half past seven and read it to each other in a way that had nothing to do with the weather, and one of them was a woman who had said, in a room over a locker's yard, that she had a form she could not fill in, and she read that one all the way to the bottom and then said, "Then that's the first thing anybody's ever given us that says we don't have to do anything," and went home.

Nobody paid for it. That is the whole of the commercial arrangement and the whole of the violation of it: nobody has paid for it, the ward does not know the reader is in it, the ward has never been asked for a shilling, and the reading is going to be on a shutter tomorrow morning and a piece of glass on Monday morning, and the firm that gives the same truth away twice a day at a tobacconist's counter is going to see it on Monday, in a printed column, on a wall.

And about two hundred yards up the hill, on the ground floor of a building he has worked in for eleven years, a man in an office coat was standing in a corridor at ten to eight in the evening with a bench under him and a folder on his knees, waiting for a hearing on the Thursday.

**He did not hear the bell.**

The ward ledger rail's brass hand bell is on a bracket about four feet above the rail on the end wall of the records room, and it is struck when a claim goes on the rail, once for every ward the claim touches, and the ward's head on the wall lights for the length of the strike and then goes out, and the ninth ward's head has not lit since the middle of the autumn before last.

He had not thought about that bell in a month. He thought about it standing in a corridor at ten to eight on the Saturday evening of the twelfth of Drownmonth, with no reason at all, and then he thought about a review, and then he thought about a man of twenty-nine who had sent a number to an assessor in an envelope with nothing on it, and then he went up to a room and worked until eleven.
