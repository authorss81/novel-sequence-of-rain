# Chapter 84 — The Asking Price

The counting floor is quiet on the second day of Miremonth in the way a room is quiet when everybody in it has read the same paragraph and nobody wants to be the first to say what it costs.

The paragraph is on the wall in three places, and the figure under it is one and four, and underneath the figure, in the firm's own hand and smaller, is where the firm got it: computed on the seventeenth of Sleetmonth out of eighteen pages, furnished on the second of Sleetmonth for eleven shillings by a councillor of the ninth ward, out of a book, in a coat, on a vote taken at four o'clock on a Wednesday.

Edda Tarn stands in front of it with a proof in her hand and does not look at it, because she printed it herself and has read it forty times.

"It is the only number in this basin that we own," she says to the room, "and we bought it for eleven shillings, and the district was not asked whether we might print it, and was not paid again, and the receipt is under it with a date on it. If anybody in this room can find me another figure of that reach that a district wrote about itself, I will print that one beside it."

Nobody in the room can. That is the whole of the counting floor's morning.

**The Exchange's own price was seven shillings and sixpence, struck through twice in public in one quarter at one price, and withdrawn for the season with no price on it at all, because a line withdrawn with nothing against it has no price and the refund rule does not reach it. The refund on a window not opened is three and nine, exactly half, and it has been paid three times.**

Wend Lyle, who is nineteen and reads the board every morning because it is the only thing in the building that changes in a way he can be blamed for not knowing, comes down the floor with a sheet of paper and shows it to her.

It is his own hand, dated and initialled, copied entire from the withdrawal paragraph on the middle desk. The second uncompellable record this month and the better of the two, in a drawer on a middle desk in a nineteen-year-old's handwriting, because a paragraph somebody can unpublish is not a record and a paragraph in a nineteen-year-old's hand is.

"Keep it," says Edda Tarn. "Date it and bring it. That is the procedure for a form with nothing in it, and it is the procedure for a paragraph with everything in it, and they are the same procedure."

---

Tobias Mear comes in at about ten with a Council form used as a pallet cover and a pencil, and spreads it on the counter, and does the arithmetic that the whole volume has been walking round for a month.

He writes 7/6 in one column and 1/4 in the other, and converts them, because an underwriter who cannot convert shillings to pence in front of a room is an underwriter who should not be in the room.

Ninety pence against sixteen pence: five and a half times, to the nearest half anybody would say out loud.

**Both figures are defensible, and the difference is the whole of what the Exchange knows about its own buyers: a carrier wants a lane and a barge and forty minutes of flat, and a ward wants a flat for a pump and timber and nine men, and the same forty minutes is two entirely different objects.**

He writes it under the figures in pencil and instructs, in the same pencil, that it never be printed, because a market that printed two prices for two different objects side by side would be pretending they were comparable, and a market that pretended that would be lying.

Then he takes out the sentence he sent to the Council by hand a month ago — *the fourth party has not been asked* — and checks that it went, and that the tab in the assessor's file carries it in her own hand, and puts it away, because the salvage man is still unasked and the line still holds.

The fourth party is the salvage man. There are four sorts of party in this basin who intended a preparation — a ward, a yard, a lighterman, a salvage man — and three of them have written something down, and one of them has written forty-one entries in a school exercise book and two sheets of good paper and sent them to nobody.

**The two hundred and forty children at the Sump Lane school are in four plans and are named in none of them, and forty of them walk up from the low blocks, and forty-one were counted outside the doors on the eighth of the Bellweather month, and the two figures are different and both are right.**

Mear counts the forty three times in a book of his own because he did not believe the first figure, and then he goes down the hill road to look at a wall, because a wall is a thing he can look at without asking anybody for it.

---

At Kestrel Yards, at about half past three, Tarin Senn chalks the receipt onto her own board under the five conditions, with the hour and the date on it, in her own hand.

*Fifth line: 2/- received. The condition is about the yard's name and not about payment. Both of those are on this board because they are the same board.*

The two shillings were invoiced on the Friday the twenty-fifth in eleven minutes, posted on the Saturday the twenty-sixth on the ordinary post at twopence, and receipted on the Wednesday the thirtieth in four days without argument, with a refusal to add a line to a schedule of charges on the ground that the firm has not been asked to and does not know what the line would be for.

**A yard that says thank you on a firm's own floor has told the firm that the yard has noticed it needs to, and by the autumn there would be a man in that building once a quarter about a retainer, so she does not go and say thank you.**

Nora Bragg, the yard's clerk of eleven years, squares the receipt under the board and says nothing, because there is nothing to say that the board does not say.

Marek Vale comes down at about four with a docket and a figure, and stands in front of the board, and asks her to repeat the hour on the receipt.

She repeats it. He writes it down. Then he asks her to repeat it a second time, in the same words, and she repeats it again, patiently, the way Ilva Sorn repeats a launch hour, and he says thank you and writes it down again.

**He asked twice and he does not know why, and he does not know he does it, and Tarin watches him write, and notices, the way she noticed on the docket at the Exchange's second floor and did not ask, and says nothing, because a yard has no procedure for a man asking a woman to say a figure twice, and she has a board to keep.**

"What is the board saying this week?" he asks.

"It is saying the yard's name and the hour and the date," says Tarin Senn. "And it is saying two shillings received. And it is saying the condition is about naming and not about payment. That is the whole of what a condition about attribution is, and it took a hundred and forty working days to earn nothing."

"And the district's shilling?"

"The district's shilling is the district's business." She turns back to the board. "A shilling a week for the right to print its own reach hours, with the ward's name on it, said yes in ninety seconds with no vote, and nobody in the room asked either named reader whether the reading might be printed. I have got opinions about that and this board is not the place for them."

They stand there for a while in the yard with the disagreement between them, and it is professional and it is real and it is in public, with the whole morning shift in earshot, and neither of them softens it.

**A district that cannot read its notice cannot buy a window, and a district that cannot buy a window is a district nobody has to compensate, and a ward with a dark public glass is a ward with a private reading twice a day, and the ward has to decide about it.**
