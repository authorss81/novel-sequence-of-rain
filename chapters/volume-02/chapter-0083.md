# Chapter 83 — The Third Month

The basin house corridor smells of wet wool on the first Thursday after Drownmonth, and the press room door is open because the press is cold, and Kell Bray is standing in it with a book under her arm that nobody in Hearthward knows exists.

She has been carrying the nine addresses for a week. They are on the back of a Council form in a fair hand, nine lines, the street and the year the job changed hands, nineteen years of other districts' clerks' names out of the bottom drawer of her own desk, and the second drawer from the top of the technical cell's press still holds them where Skell put them on the Thursday before, because a Council has no schedule for receiving a list of addresses.

**A ward is not a building, and the address of a ward is a person, and the person may be replaced in a year, and there is no envelope with the stub and no address on the cover of the book.**

She does not take them out. She has learned that much in four days in this building: a good minute is a weapon with no handle, and the finding stands, and it is not withdrawn and it is not re-set, and the district it names has not been told that it exists.

Skell comes down the corridor with a stack of direction copies tied in string, and nods to her the way clerks nod to clerks when there is work on.

"Morning," he says. "You're early for the stub."

"I am early for everything in this building," says Kell Bray. "It is the only way to watch it happen."

He smiles without stopping. The copies are the assessor's four paragraphs on the stub, the first thing this Council has sent out of that building in a generation, and they are going into nine district files, and one of them is going to come back with nothing under it, and they all know it.

In the ground-floor room the temporary compensation ledger sits on the table where the assessor left it. It is about two inches thick with a printed head, and the expiry is written on the inside cover in her own hand, and she said it out loud twice on the day it was opened, and every clerk in the building can say it now without looking: at the end of the financial year.

Peascod the clerk turns the cover back for the morning, because the book has to be opened whether there is anything to put in it or not, and reads the one line that is in it, in a low voice, for the room.

It is nineteen words long, and it says the figure is unknown.

**The 2/6 is the mean of 45d and 16d, to the nearest penny downward, and it is not re-derived and it does not change.**

"Is that the whole of it?" asks a licensing clerk from the doorway, who has come with a form and does not know what the book is.

"That is the whole of it," says Peascod. "A ledger and not a fund, and it pays documented loss only, and nobody knows yet what it is for."

Upstairs, in the technical cell, Sabin Orle is writing the hearing notice in her own hand. She writes slowly because the notice has to carry the date and the room and the parties, and because a notice with a place and an hour and a name under it is the one thing nobody in this basin has been able to get for a year.

The notice says the Council will hear the matter of the ninth ward's reach window, and the sixth ward's tide, and the temporary ledger, on the fourteenth of the new month, in the basin house, at two o'clock.

She puts the pen down and looks at the calendar on the wall, which is a Council calendar with the wet quarter marked in three bands, and the third band has no name written on it in her hand because no chapter in this building has ever had to write it.

Skell leans in. "What do I put on the notice for the month, ma'am? The boys will ask."

She looks at him. "Put Miremonth," says Sabin Orle. "The first of Miremonth is today."

**Miremonth has thirty days and begins on a Thursday, and the season's rhythm is rain, clear, rain, clear, and the smallest back-cut of the three is still ahead of it, on the morning of the eleventh.**

That is the whole of the naming, and it is done the way Sleetmonth and Drownmonth were done, for what the month does to low ground.

---

Neris Kade comes up the hill at about eleven with a shilling in a paper twist and a page in a drawer that she has not finished.

The shilling is the second week's shilling, for the right to print the ward's own reach hours with the ward's name on it, and the clerk at the firm's counter takes it without a word and writes her a receipt on the firm's paper and dates it and initials it, and she puts it in her coat pocket with the first one.

She does not go to the counting floor. She goes to Tannery Walk and reads her own district's figure on the firm's wall — NINTH WARD, BELLWEATHER REACH, FORTY MINUTES: 1/4, with the receipt for eleven shillings underneath it — and stands there for a minute with her hands in her pockets, and then goes and checks the school gate shutter, because the children walk up.

The shutter has the trace hours on it in a fair hand: the bend at about half past one on the low ground, eleven minutes past four at the south end, four hours and ten minutes apart on the water, with the nine minutes on it as well, because she is not having anybody in the third block thinking they have got a clean hour.

**The ward was not asked whether the figure might be printed, and was not paid again, and has no purchase in it, and the district's own free reading is on a firm's list at threepence a week, which is thirteen shillings a year and one pound six for two years.**

At about half past two she goes into the west end of the records room with a page for the district return, and the copy clerk at the third desk on the left takes it without looking up.

"Will you copy it fair and put the date and the hour on it, the way you do."

"I will." Tovan Iles reaches for the ruled foolscap. "Are you the ninth ward? Because there's a form for it and it's a different hand."

"Use the return."

"Right." The pen moves. The room is the quietest room in a building that is not quiet, four desks, a window, a press, a counter, and the press takes a minute.

On the bench behind her, squared in the tray, is the Office's true minutes copy of the sixth ward's claim sheet, made at about eleven twenty-six on the thirtieth, in eleven minutes, in her own hand, dated, timed and initialled. The tray goes to the technical cell. That is the whole of the procedure and it is correct.

Neris stands in the doorway for nine seconds longer than she needs to, the way she did on the Tuesday, and then goes back down the corridor, and nobody thanks the copy clerk and nobody ever has.

---

At four o'clock she sits at her own desk in the district office with a pen and a clean sheet, the way she does about twice a month, and she writes nothing.

The page is in the drawer, open, at the seventh line. The heading is unchanged: NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came.

She reads the seven lines. The first three are about the Bellweather bulletins and the fourth names a man and a plate in a coat, and the fifth is about the ward's own public glass and a fortnight, and the sixth and the seventh were written in the Bellweather month and are about a habit she got out of a corridor.

She puts the pen down and picks it up and puts it down.

Because there is an eighth, and she has known there was an eighth for a fortnight, and it is the line about the shilling: it does not say who may have this, and nobody has asked, and I said yes.

**She has a card of nine names in a drawer and the sheet she cannot bring herself to write, because a card of nine names is a list of people she is fond of and a sheet is a document that says she is answerable for them.**

The page is seven lines. There is no eighth line and she cannot write one.

In the drawer under it is the ward's shelter rota card with nine names on it, and it is still unwritten in the form it would need to be managed, and she closes the drawer on both of them and goes down to the pump house, because the pump house is a thing she can manage.
