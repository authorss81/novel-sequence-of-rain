# Chapter 86 — The Third Thing

The west end of the records room is the quietest room in a building that is not quiet, and Tovan Iles has been in it since twenty past two on the third day of the Bellweather month, and she will be in it for as long as the demotion lasts, which is permanently, because her demotion is permanent and it is not a favour.

She is twenty-seven, a copy clerk, with a fair hand and a very good wrist, and nobody in the building has thanked her since the third of the Bellweather month, and there is a key in her pocket that has been there since that day.

Her box is on the ground floor. It holds two things, and she has never counted them in front of anybody and never will.

The two things are these, and they are both true, and they were both made the way a copy clerk is trained to make them, without asking anybody:

One: a true copy of the thirtieth's day sheet as it stood before the classification was changed, made on the third of the Bellweather month, in her own hand, dated, timed and initialled, on her own paper.

Two: a second true copy of the ninth ward's entered claim sheet including the whole of the seventh box, copied at 10:09 on the Wednesday of the second of Drownmonth, unpinned off the rail, in her own hand, dated, timed and initialled, on her own paper. The Office's routine minutes copy of the same sheet was made on the twenty-first at 11:26 and went into the tray and is not in the box.

She has never told anybody, including herself in the way people tell themselves things they have already worked out, that the two things in the box are the same size and cost the same, and that the first one cost her a job. On the thirtieth the classification went onto the day sheet at about four in the afternoon and a day's readings became a return, and a return cannot be produced by a person, and she copied the sheet before the stamp went on because she was the copy clerk and it was on her desk. That is the whole of the reason. There is no other reason and she looked for one for a fortnight.

**They are the only two documents in the basin that say, on the same two months, that a cost can be written down in a fair hand and be worth nothing to anybody.**

On the sixth day of Miremonth, at about eleven o'clock, with the rain doing nothing remarkable outside the window, she squares the Office's true minutes copy of the sixth ward's tide claim sheet in the tray — eleven minutes, her own hand, dated, timed and initialled, made on the thirtieth — and checks the stamp, and the tray goes to the technical cell, and that is the whole of the procedure.

Then she takes a fresh sheet of her own paper, squares it on the third desk on the left, and makes a third thing.

It is a true copy of the sixth ward's entered claim sheet including the whole of the seventh box, in pencil where the original is in pencil and in ink where the original is in ink, the four lines about what the sixth ward can do about its water, the cost of the second high spring, the forty tons, the eleven days of masons, the lighters going down empty, the wall waiting a month, and the refusal of a figure — *if you put a figure on this I will not sign it* — and the charter signed on Tuesday at ten o'clock, and the sentence about a ward being a party to nothing and a lighterman being a party to nine.

She does it in nineteen minutes, which is eight longer than the minutes copy, because a minutes copy is a copy of what is on the sheet and a true copy of a claim sheet is a copy of what is on the sheet including the pencil, and the pencil is the part everybody writes in and nobody reads, and there are two words in the seventh box of that sheet in pencil with somebody's thumbprint over one of them.

**It is something a copy clerk could make without asking, and it is true, and it is one thing, and she makes it without asking anybody, because asking would mean explaining why a minutes copy is not enough, and a minutes copy is enough for everybody but her.**

The glass trade pays a ticket. A ticket is a piece of paper that says a man may cut and bed a plate and set it in a brass surround, and a man with a ticket may be asked to cut a nine-inch plate in a morning, and a woman with a ticket is paid a different rate for the same nine inches and has been since before anybody in this room was born. She sat the examination for it twice, in the spring and the autumn before last, and passed it, and the ticket is a matter of record, and it has been in a drawer in her father's house since the year he died, and on the third of the Bellweather month it went into a file with a line on it, and she was a copy clerk at the end of that week.

She does not think about the ticket at the third desk. She thinks about it at five o'clock when she puts the key in her pocket, and she has not said one word about it in this building, and the examiner is a Deputy, and the examination is in the spring, and it is held by somebody else, and she does not sit it.

The reason is not defiance and it is not loyalty to a warden she has spoken to twice. A minutes copy goes into the tray and the tray goes to the cell, and the cell is a different department's business, and what the cell does with a tray is not recorded in the tray. A true copy in her own hand, on her own paper, with her initials and the hour on it, exists in a form that can be asked for by the person it is about, and that is the only kind of copy in this basin that can be asked for by the person it is about, and she has worked that out on her own, at this desk, over two months, and has told nobody.

She dates it, times it, initials it, folds it once, and puts it in her own box on the ground floor at about half past twelve, with the key that has been in her pocket since the third of the Bellweather month.

Nobody sees her do it. Nobody asks her about it. The box now holds three things.

---

At about three Kell Bray comes into the west end with a page for the Hearthward return and a question she has been carrying for four days.

"Can I ask you something?" she says, in the flat voice of a competent person in a room where nobody important is present. "It's about the work."

Tovan looks up. "Go on."

"When the Office makes a true copy of a claim sheet, how many does it make?"

"One." Tovan does not stop writing. "It's the minutes copy. It's the only one and it goes into the tray and the tray goes wherever the tray goes, and it's stamped and it's timed and it's in the book, and that's the end of it."

Kell Bray stands there for a moment with the answer not helping her, the way Neris Kade stood in the same doorway on the Tuesday with the same answer not helping her.

"What about the original? Is the original kept?"

"The original is on the rail, or in the file, or in a district's drawer with the door locked, and the original is the district's business and not ours, and it is not our copy and we cannot produce it and it is not the same as having a copy." Tovan squares the tray without looking at it. "I can tell you what is in a tray because I put it in a tray. I could not tell you what is in a district's drawer if you asked me, and you have not asked me, and nobody has asked me."

"That's all right," says Kell Bray.

"That's all right." Tovan puts the sheet on the tray and squares it, and goes back to the third copy of a set of minutes she is making for the technical cell, and the room is quiet again.

**It did not help and nobody thanked her. Kell Bray asked, and Neris Kade before her on the Thursday, and neither of them knows what is in the box, and neither does she in the sense that matters, because nobody has ever asked her to count.**

She is a copy clerk in every appearance. Her demotion stands and it is not a favour and nobody in the Office thanks her. The glass-tender's examination is in the spring and is held by somebody else and she does not sit it, and there is no form on which to enter a complaint about that, and she has not looked for one.

At about five she locks the press, counts the trays, puts the key in her pocket with the other key, and walks home in the rain past the ninth ward tower, whose signal glass is dark. In the Weatherglass the head above the ward ledger rail is dark with it. She does not look up at either, because she has looked up at both of them every day for two months on the way home and there is nothing to see.

The walk is nineteen minutes and it is the same nineteen minutes every day, and the wet is in it, and there is a lamp in the tobacconist's that is on at five and off at five and she has never once worked out how he does that. A man selling wicks has a pitch at the second bridge and the pitch has been there for nineteen years and there is a rule about a pitch and a rule about a man who stands on it, and the rule says a man may stand where another man has stood, and nobody in this basin has ever asked the tobacconist what happened to the man who had it before him, and she walks past it twice a day and has never once asked either.

**The bubbled temporary in the frame has been in it since the autumn before last, and those are two objects with two histories, and nobody who has walked past that tower since the autumn has said how long it has been, because the honest answer is two different lengths and only one of them is about a lamp.**

Two months of looking at a dark tower is two months of not saying a number. A number would have to be a number of months or weeks, and the frame's temporary came out in the autumn and the lamp went out in the middle of the Bellweather month, and the two are not the same length, and a person who said one would be wrong about the other, and a copy clerk who is wrong about a date in a room like this one is a copy clerk who is asked about it.
