# Chapter 66 — The Price Of Looking

Edda Tarn had been a counter clerk for nine years and she had the particular calm of a person who is very good and knows it, and on the Sunday morning of the thirteenth day of Drownmonth she came in at a quarter to ten to do a thing that was not on her list, and did it anyway, because she had read a wall.

She had come down to the west end on the Saturday afternoon with the ward's letter — a refusal, one sheet, in a steward's hand, four paragraphs, correct in every line, and it had taken her forty minutes to read and about four seconds to know that it was the best letter she had ever handled and worth, at that moment, exactly nothing.

**Then she went and looked at a shutter.**

It is on the tannery side of Tannery Walk, four doors from her own counter, on premises that have been shut since the autumn, and it is two feet by three and it is painted in three hands, and it is a reader's working.

She stood in front of it for about eleven minutes in the rain with a folded sheet in her bag, and she read it twice, and then she went and got a piece of paper out of the bag and wrote the whole of it down from memory, which she does, and then she read what she had written.

*Two ends. Yard's slip, and the Exchange's own instrument at the south bank. The bend in the Release on the seventeenth, third watch. One hour corrected by the second reader. One movement disputed, both readings standing, neither withdrawn. Both names, the hour, the date, the place. And at the bottom: this is not a notice and nobody must do anything because of it.*

Now: the firm's own product, delivered free to this ward twice a day, is on a counter in a shop, in two columns, on the same stock, with a stamp at the bottom.

And here is the list of what the firm has and this thing does not.

*It is anonymous. There is no name on our sheet. There has never been a name on our sheet and there is a good reason for that and the reason is nineteen years old and it is correct and it is the reason nobody has ever been able to say we published a false number, because there is no author to sue.*

*It is uncorrected on its face. The corrections go in a line of their own, item seven, which is correct, and which every competent reader in this basin has described out loud as *a firm that prints its own errors*, and which is, in fact, a firm printing its own errors in a form that cannot be compared with the line above it, because item seven has no times on it.*

*It is delivered to one counter in one ward.*

And here is the list of what this thing on a shutter has.

*It is dated, timed, and signed by two people who can be named. It is corrected on its face, in public, by a second person, with the hours on both corrections. It is in the district the sheet is delivered to. And it says, at the bottom, in writing, that it is not a notice — which is a thing a market cannot say and has never wanted to say.*

---

**And then Edda Tarn worked out what it actually is commercially, standing in the rain, and it took her about four minutes and it is the most important four minutes of her working life so far.**

A sheet that is corrected in private is a claim. A claim can be refused, and this firm has a rule about refusing things, and refusing a claim is what the ward's letter of Saturday was.

**A sheet that is corrected in public is a record.**

And a record can be compared with a price. That is the whole of it. That is the only thing a record does that a claim cannot do, and this firm has spent nineteen years pricing weather and has never once held a record, because a record has to be made and a record has to stand still while somebody checks it, and the only people who make records are people who are not paid to be right.

Nobody in this basin can check our sheet. A buyer cannot check it, because it is anonymous, and the *one* line in it that is somebody's — the Office's band — is the line we do not own, so the only thing anybody could audit is the Office's.

**This thing on a shutter can be checked. It was checked. It is wrong, in two places, and the corrections are on it in a hand with the hour on them, and anybody with a chain and a staff can go and look at the post.**

---

She got back to the second floor at about half past eleven and told Ivo Slate and then told a partner, in that order, because that is the correct order and because she is very good.

"It is a commercial threat and it is not the board," she said. "It is the corrections."

"The corrections are the best thing we have ever printed."

"Then you will understand me faster than anybody else, because I have been on the quotation floor for nine years and I have watched a market lose to a piece of paper four times, and it was never the paper. It was always the place on the paper where somebody had written *I was wrong, here is the hour, here is my name.*"

She put it on the desk.

"And here is the rest of it, and it is the part that is going to matter, and it is not a threat, it is an opportunity, and I have been in this building nine years and it has taken me until this morning to see it, which should worry all of you."

"Go on."

**"We cannot sell certainty against a record that is cheaper to check than to buy."**

She said it slowly, the way you say a thing you have just thought of and are not yet sure of.

"Our refund rule says a window not opened is refunded at the quoted price within the week, and the price of the withdrawn price is published. **That rule is the mechanism of our own defeat, and it has been since the morning of the twenty-third of Sleetmonth, and none of you have ever once said it out loud, because the rule is the reason we are trusted.** But read it the other way round. A firm that has published a price once cannot unpublish it. It has to keep publishing it. It is committed, in perpetuity, to the arithmetic of its own past, in public, at a rate nobody controls. And on Thursday the seventeenth there will be two of those prices in one quarter, on the same reach, at the same figure, and if the second one is struck then the market will have struck a line through twice in public in one quarter and there is no argument in this basin or out of it that will survive that except *we do not know what the water is worth*."

"Which is the truth."

"**Which is the only sentence a market is permitted to say, and which is a sentence with no product in it at all.**" She squared the sheet. "The firm's whole advantage is that we can name a number. The moment we cannot, we are a shop with a wall."

---

Then she did the thing that the chapter is named for, and she did it because a woman had put a thing on a shutter in a poor ward that the firm could have sold, and because she is a person who, when she cannot compete with a thing, goes and puts a price on it.

**She priced it.**

Not by closing it. Not by buying it. Not by writing to a ward. Not by putting a man on Tannery Walk. She took a fresh sheet of the firm's list stock and she wrote a line on it in her own hand, in the column where the firm puts things it can supply, and she priced the whole of what had appeared on that shutter.

The rate is what it is worth to a buyer, which is not what it cost.

*SUPPLIED — LOCAL CORRECTED READING, per ward. Signed, timed, dated, with public corrections. Free to the district. 3d. per week.*

She wrote it in the margin first and then in the column, and she made her own clerk put it on the printed list, and the printed list goes up on the wall in the quotation hall every Monday at eleven, in public, with the refund rule painted underneath it, and anybody in the city can read it.

**Three pence a week.**

And she did the arithmetic, because that is what she is, standing at the counter at about twenty past twelve on a Sunday with a sheet in her hand and nobody watching.

Three pence a week. Fifty-two weeks, and a coin is a coin. **Thirteen shillings a year.**

And two years of a woman's free, corrected, signed, timed readings — delivered on a shutter, in a plate-glass case, and on a piece of board in a district with four hundred and forty people in it, six times a day, by a woman who has been doing it since the weather turned — come to **two pounds and six shillings.**

She stopped writing for about four seconds.

She had a counter clerk's arithmetic and she had been a counter clerk for nine years and she knew what a year of a thing is worth and what two years of a thing is worth, and what she had in front of her was a figure so small that a man in this building could carry it in a hat, and the figure was not interesting, and the figure was not the point.

**The point is the other sum, and she did that one twice.**

Eleven shillings is what a ward sold this ward's own information for once, in advance, off a councillor, in a coat, in forty seconds, on the second of last month.

Eleven shillings is what a counter-reader charged one man on Saturday, in coin, on a plank, for about four hours and one morning of it, and the man counted it out in sixpences and threepences and a penny and put it in a satchel himself.

**Thirteen shillings a year is what this firm has decided the whole of that is worth, to a buyer, and it did not consult the woman, and it does not know her name, and she is on a shutter four doors up the street with her name on it in a fair hand.**

And a very small number is going on a wall on Monday morning in a fair hand, because that is what a market does with a thing it cannot stop.

She did not enjoy it and she was not proud of it and she wrote it anyway, and the reason she wrote it anyway is the reason she is a counter clerk and not a partner, which is that she was asked a question and she answered it in the form the answer had to take.

---

Then she read the ward's letter again, because it was in her bag and it was the only document in the building that had ever made her want to sit down.

*There is one more thing and then I will sign. Three weeks ago this ward closed a window of its own on purpose, in writing, with a form and a bell, so that a man who had already paid for it could not get at the water, and it cost us a wall and a year, and there is no column in the world that says what it cost, and there is not going to be one. I am not going to write to the firm that sold it.*

She put it back in the bag.

**A firm that has a free corrected signed local reading for a shilling a quarter and cannot obtain one schedule from a district, because the district may refuse, and has refused, and the refusal is worth more than the reading.**

She wrote a note for the Monday, which was read out on the floor at eleven and was about three sentences long, and then she went out at about half past one because she wanted to see the shutter in daylight with other people looking at it and she wanted to be in the middle of that and not at a counter.

The list was already in her head and it had four lines on it and the four lines were not about weather at all:

*1. We have a product we did not have. It is a reading. It can be corrected in public without a claim being made and without a refund being due, and that is the whole of the difference between it and a window, and it took a poor district and a woman with a notebook to show us.*

*2. Nine districts. One shutter. One counter. The Exchange's sheet is free, anonymous, and printed, and it goes to one counter, and it is the best weather product in this basin and it is worth eleven shillings to the ward that furnished the material for it.*

*3. On the seventeenth, whether or not the reach comes down, we will publish two reach prices in one quarter, and every one of them was printed by us, and the district's own figure was furnished to us for money.*

*4. **Ask the districts.** Not for their schedules. Ask them what they would pay for a signed, timed, corrected reading of their own water, delivered to their own door, free to them, and see whether anybody in nine wards says nothing.*

---

It was about half past one on Tannery Walk and it was raining ordinary rain and Mrs Teodora Bain had four people in her shop, which is a good number for a Sunday, and she had told four of them about the shutter and one of them had told nine more, and Edda Tarn stood in the doorway with her coat dripping on the boards and did not buy anything.

"I've come to look at your shutters," she said.

"There's three of them on that one and a fourth coming Tuesday if the parish gets a move on." Mrs Bain came round the counter. "I have had eleven people in here today ask me what it says, and I have told eleven people I cannot read the small writing, and I have told the truth, which is that I cannot read the small writing, and that is a different reason and they have all taken it the same way."

"Do you know what it is?"

"I know what it *says*," said Mrs Teodora Bain. "It says two people got it wrong about the water on Thursday and wrote down that they got it wrong and what the hour was. And I know that whoever wrote it has had it up since seven o'clock last night for nothing, because I asked him, and he said it was nothing, and he said it like a man saying a number in a shop." She put her cloth down on the counter. "And I have been giving this street the weather twice a day for nothing since the weather turned, and before that I gave it to them for nothing for two months because nobody else would, and I would like to know what it is worth."

"You have priced it," said Edda Tarn.

"I have not."

"You have," said Mrs Bain. "You have just told me you have, and I have watched you tell people things all morning. Four and tuppence the paper, and the paper is theirs, and a hand is yours." She picked the cloth up again. "That's the third one of you this month who's come in here and said the word *nothing* to me, and all three of you went away and charged somebody for it."
