# Chapter 90 — The Hearing, First Afternoon

The hearing room in the basin house is a plain room with a trestle in it, and on the trestle is a bound book about two inches thick with a printed head, open at the one line, and beside the book is nothing, because nothing else has been admitted yet.

It is the fourteenth of Miremonth, at two o'clock, raining, with nine people in the room and a clerk with a fair hand, and Sabin Orle at the head of the table with a pencil she puts down about four times a year.

The rain is coming off the basin house steps in sheets and there is a mat by the door that has been there since the Council moved in and has never once been thick enough, and there are four umbrellas against the wall inside where a man who has come to give evidence has nowhere to put one, and nobody has ever printed a word about the mat.

On the wall behind her is the returned ledger with the column headed CLAIM — DISPLACED TURNS, five lines under it now, no sponsor field, no disclaimer: Hearthward terraces week of the second; the ninth ward, the reach and the wall, week of the seventeenth; the Bellweather reach, quoted and not opened, the morning of the thirtieth of the Bellweather month; the sixth ward's tide, morning of the thirtieth of Drownmonth; and the ninth ward's Miremonth window, morning of the tenth.

**The third of those is the Bellweather dry line. The fourth is a different thirtieth. Nobody in this room joins them.**

Neris Kade sits at the side with her seven-line page in her district office's records drawer at home and a copy of it in her hand. Anwen Sarr sits opposite with Circular 14 in her coat, read forty-one times. Maro Hesk sits at the back with his hat in his hand. Tobias Mear sits beside him with a Council form and a pencil. Marek Vale sits at the very back on a censured reader's pass, saying nothing, hearing half of it on the left side and all of it on the right.

"The matter," says Sabin Orle, "is the ninth ward's reach window, the sixth ward's tide, and the temporary compensation ledger. The ledger is provisional, it expires at the end of the financial year, it is a ledger and not a fund, and it pays documented loss only."

She turns to Neris. "Mrs. Kade. Your page."

Neris puts the seven lines on the table. The clerk copies the heading: NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came.

The assessor reads all seven, including the seventh — *the thirtieth's return does not say the reach levels came down, because there is no instrument in the Office that looks at a reach, and therefore nothing that was lost on the reach can be said to have been lost by anybody* — and rules:

"Admitted. Not compellable, still. A district record, accurately kept, stamped and dated in your own hand, locked in your drawer. It joins the two ends of the thirtieth and it is worth nothing to this ledger, and you did it anyway."

**It is admitted and it does not move anything, and that is what admitted means.**

"Miss Iles is not called," says the assessor, without looking up. "The Office's minutes copies are in the tray and the tray is in the cell. Nobody knows what else a copy clerk holds, including her, and nobody is asking."

Tovan Iles, at the third desk on the left in the west end of the records room four hundred feet away, copies a set of minutes for the technical cell and is not asked, and her box holds three things, and her demotion is permanent and is not a favour and nobody thanks her.

---

Then the figures, and they are all fixed and none of them drifts.

The Exchange's published reach price was 7/6, struck through twice, withdrawn for the season. Its refund is 3/9, exactly half, paid three times. The ninth ward's own figure is 1/4, computed by the firm on the seventeenth of Sleetmonth out of an eighteen-page return it bought for eleven shillings on the second. The assessor's interim is 2/6, the mean of 45d and 16d, and it is not re-derived and does not change.

**The Exchange withdrew the reach line with no price on it at all, and the refund rule does not reach that; a line withdrawn with nothing against it has no price, nothing to refund and nothing to publish.**

The firm's paragraph is put on the table with the 1/4 in it and the receipt for eleven shillings under it. The ward's letter of eleven lines is put beside it: six men at the district rate, the gang, nothing for the timber, nothing for the pump, the space between the two figures is a wall, no third figure, will not sign one.

Tobias Mear puts his hand on the Council form, and does not have it open, and then opens it.

"Ma'am. I ask that one and four be received as the ward's figure."

He says it to the table and not to the assessor, which is a thing people do when they want a room to hear them, and the room hears him. Hesk turns his head about an inch. Anwen Sarr's hand stops on her coat.

"It is computed out of the ward's own return," says Mear. "Eighteen pages, the ninth ward's own clearing rota, shelter rota and pump order, furnished by a councillor of the ninth ward out of a book in a coat on a vote of this Council. I wrote down in the Bellweather month that a preparation can only be priced by the party that intended it, and I have not withdrawn it, and the ninth ward is the party that intended this one. It is the ward's figure, because the ward supplied everything the figure was made out of, and if the Council will not take a district's own figure for a district's own morning, then the Council is not measuring loss, it is measuring paperwork, and the paperwork is a Council's own."

That is a long time for a room, and Sabin Orle lets it stand before she answers it, which is her habit, and which is why a man will say a thing like that to her at all.

"Mrs. Kade," says the assessor, and the clerk's pen goes to a fresh line. "Did the ninth ward enter one and four in a claim, at any time, in any form?"

"No, ma'am."

"Was one and four in the claim sheet when it went on the rail?"

"No, ma'am. There was a box for a figure and it was left empty."

"Has the ninth ward published one and four anywhere, as its own figure, on a shutter, a sheet, a minute or a notice?"

"No, ma'am. It has never seen it until it was on a firm's wall in this basin, and I read it there."

"Would you sign one now?"

"No," says Neris Kade. "I would not sign a figure for a morning, and I said so in a box in capitals a week ago, and the box is in this room's file."

"1/4 is tendered," says the assessor, "as the ward's figure, by a party with a Council form in front of him. And it is refused, as the ward's figure, for want of a record. The district declines it, in open session, on the record, and the refusal stands whatever anybody writes on a piece of paper afterwards."

She turns back to the table and speaks to the room rather than to Mear, and it is the same voice she used in the Bellweather month in front of nine people.

"The eighteen-page return is an internal district record — entered in this building on the twenty-fifth of last month, which is to say it is now a document in this building and not in yours — and it cost you the document. The computation out of it is the firm's property, bought for eleven shillings on a vote taken at four o'clock on a Wednesday while the warden was on a pump. The ward never entered 1/4 in its claim and never published it as its own. The Council may not supply a figure and may not invent one. A loss which is a period cannot be entered as a sum."

**Anwen Sarr's clause four, again, defeating the person who wrote it, correctly, in a room, with a clerk: the column names what closed and by no means who closed it, and a figure nobody entered is a figure nobody can be paid.**

Mear closes the Council form. He does not argue it, and there is a short silence in which it is possible to see him deciding not to, and then he says, "Thank you, ma'am. That is the sentence I wrote down, and I have just heard it applied to my own argument, and I would rather it had been applied by somebody else."

"It was applied by the district," says Neris Kade, to her copy of the seven lines and not to him, and there is no temper in it, and the clerk writes the whole of it down.

"The only published price for that window in this basin," says the assessor, "is the Exchange's, at seven and six. Both figures are defensible, and the difference is the whole of what the Exchange knows about its own buyers."

Anwen stands. She demands side-by-side publication — the ward's figure beside the firm's, the district's schedule with the price — in language better than the column's, and she knows it, and the whole room knows it.

The assessor hears her out, and refuses her within the hour by a printed sheet whose fourth paragraph is a good argument: where one party has used another party's published figure, the Office has no duty, no instrument and no opinion.

**She has been refused four times now. She does not lose her temper. The clerk writes the refusal down and she sits back down.**

"The right to refuse," says Sabin Orle, looking down the table at the clerk so it is minuted, "is real, and it is cheap, and it is correct. A district can refuse a claim. And it is insufficient, because a district can refuse a claim and cannot refuse a price, cannot withdraw a return it has already sent, and cannot invoice against a number that describes somebody else's object."

The room sits with it for about four minutes. In the four minutes Hesk turns his hat over in his hands twice, and Mear writes nothing on the form, and Neris Kade looks at the seven lines in front of her and does not touch them, and at the very back a censured man who can hear all of it on one side of his head and half of it on the other does not move at all.

Then the assessor closes the book for the day, because a hearing that is not a fight still has hours, and the ledger still has one line in it, and the fourth party has still not been asked.
