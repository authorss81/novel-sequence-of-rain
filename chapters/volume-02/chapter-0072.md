# Chapter 72 — The Price Of A River

The forward board went up at eleven on the Monday morning of the twenty-first day of Drownmonth, and there was no Bellweather reach line on it, and for about four minutes nobody in the counting floor noticed, and then about thirty people did at once.

It had been on every board since the autumn. It was the reach the firm had been selling for nineteen years and it was the only line on the sheet that anybody in the building could say out loud without looking anything up, and on the Monday it was simply not there, and in the gap where it had been there was a nail and a rectangle of paint a different colour from the paint around it.

---

Edda Tarn got there at ten and she was thirty-eight and she had been a counter clerk for nine years and she was the person on this floor who knew which of the four things they were about to do was going to cost the most, and she had known by the Tuesday of the previous week, and she had told a partner on the Friday, and the partner had said *not until the twenty-first* and had gone home.

She stood in front of the board at about five to eleven with a printed sheet in her hand and a fair hand of her own on the back of it and did the thing she is good at, which is to be legible in a hurry.

"Mr. Lyle."

"Wend."

"Wend, what is the rule on the reach line."

"That a window not opened is refunded at the quoted price, the price is paid back within the week, and the price of the withdrawn window is published." He said it the way he said everything, which is to say entirely accurately and without any performance at all. He was nineteen and he had been on the second floor a year and eleven months and he read the board every morning because it was the only thing in the building that changed in a way you could be blamed for not knowing. "And that the reach line is struck through twice in this quarter already."

"At seven and six, both times, the twenty-third of Sleetmonth and the seventeenth of Drownmonth, and the price was published both times, and the refund rule is what published it, and I did not write the rule."

"No, ma'am."

"So what is the reach line for."

Wend Lyle looked at the nail and the rectangle.

"It's gone, ma'am."

"It's not gone. It's gone from the *board*, and I have been asked to put something in the space, and I want you to understand what I am about to do, because in about four months somebody is going to come and ask me why, and I am going to have to point at the spot where I wrote down what it was for, in my own hand, with the hour on it." She turned the sheet round and put the back of it up against the wall beside the board, and there were already two small hooks there for exactly this purpose, which had been put there by a counter clerk in about 1961. "**A market that has struck a line through twice in public cannot go on selling the thing behind it, and the only lawful thing a market can do about that is admit that a number exists. So we are going to print a number.**"

"That's a decision," said Wend Lyle.

"That is a decision made on the Friday by a man who is not in this building and who will be embarrassed about it, and I have been told I may have the hour on the wall, and I am going to use it." She put her thumb on the sheet. "Now. Say something to me about the instrument."

---

**The instrument is at the south end of the Bellweather freight apron, in a masonry wall, and it is the only measurement of a river in this city.**

A copper float in a stilling well. An iron chain of two hundred and forty feet in three-eighths with links of a closed pattern. A drum with a deliberately helical groove, so that a stretching chain cannot carry the reading and a chain that has been in the water for a season cannot lie. A staff with marks cut in it. And a sealed glass datum cut, ground and seated by hand by a man who is a censured reader and is not in this building either, to a length and not to a sight, checked against the chain's own marked link and cut twice, so that the reading is repeatable to a quarter of an inch at an interval by two different people with two different chains.

The maker is sixty-one and has made tide gauges for forty years for the port, the two breweries, the Old Custom House landing and a grain dock at Nacre that shut in sixty, and this is the first one he has ever put in a river. He was asked, in the autumn, to make a second point on the same reach ninety feet from the first, and he quoted a price and a lead time, and the firm did not take it, and the quotation was still on the desk of the man who had asked for it in pencil.

**And what it does not do is see a turn.**

Wend Lyle wrote that out. He was given it to write out and he wrote it in nine words on the back of a Council form in pencil and it took him about a minute and a half and it is the only sentence anybody in this basin would be able to check in a year, and that is most of what a nineteen-year-old is for.

*A level is not a sequence, and if we print the level and call it a reading, somebody will believe it, and then it is a lie with a date on it.*

---

**And then he did the thing that made the Monday worth putting on a wall, which was that he was not frightened of the other firms.**

Wend Lyle had been the man who noticed a new line in the printed list in the middle of March and had asked who set it, and had been told at his own desk that a thing in a printed column was not provisional, and had said that he would not treat it as one. He had then spent a fortnight not being told what the column was for, which is what happens in a firm that has spent nineteen years not asking anybody anything.

On the Monday he came in at half past seven and found a note on the middle desk in a hand he now recognised, which said: *Ask the districts, not for their schedules. Ask them before the board, not after. One question each, on the telephone, and write the answer down, and if they refuse, write that down too. — E.T.*

He asked nine wards on the telephone on the Monday morning between half past seven and about a quarter to ten.

Nine wards, one question, and the question was not about the weather. It was: *what can this firm put in a public list about what your district is able to do about a river, that you would be willing to have your name on?* And every district was allowed to refuse, and every district was told so on the telephone before the question was asked, and four of them refused, and the refusals are four of the five lines of the notice, and two of them are in a pencil hand and one of them is the fourth ward's, in a clerk's good hand, and the fourth ward's is identical in wording to the refusal it filed in the middle of the month because that ward has one way of writing a no and has had it for forty years.

Two districts answered. The sixth ward said that it had four lighters and a chain barge and that there was a bar at the Nacre end that took her at eight feet and a half and that he would not, and did not say whether he was willing to have his name on it, and the clerk telephoned him back four times about that specific question and never got an answer, and it is in the notice as *unresolved*.

**And one of the four refusals is in the notice in full, and it is the fourth ward's, and it is a clerk's good hand, and it is word for word the refusal the fourth ward filed in the middle of the month, and the fourth ward has one way of writing a no and has had it for forty years:**

*This ward has no return of this kind and has not kept one and declines to invent one. It has answered the Council's letter of the tenth of the month to the same effect and is not required to say so twice. The Council's form is not a return under the Standing Response and the Clerk of this ward has so advised the Council in writing.*

**Which is better than the first one, because the first one did not know it had a second box.**

The ninth ward was not asked. It had said in writing that it was not going to write to the firm that sold it, and there is a line on the record of the Monday that says the district was not telephoned because a district that has refused in writing is not to be asked again on a Monday by a clerk, and a partner read that line and did not strike it.

---

**And the ninth call was a licensing clerk in the firm's own back office, who had been given a list of nine names and a question and a piece of paper, and who is a public servant on the firm's licence, and who has been doing the work of a public body for a private firm for eleven years without ever once being told that this is what he is.**

His name is **Coll Marsden** and he is twenty-five and he has been a licensing clerk for two years and he made the nine calls from a desk in a corridor with a telephone and a list and he had a piece of paper to tick and he did not get a tick for six of the nine, and when Hesk came out of the counting floor at eleven and said *the licensing clerk's office is to stop billing that at all*, Marsden said:

"Bill what, Mr. Hesk?"

"Telephone. Nine calls. It's on the sheet."

"It's on the sheet," said Marsden, "but there was no form. There's no form for asking a district a question. You could put it on the commercial returns schedule and it would be wrong, because it's not a return, and you could put it on the agency schedule and it would be wrong, because there was no agency, and I have got four forms on this desk for asking a firm a question and there is not one of them for asking a district whether it is willing to be described in public, and I have never in two years wanted one."

---

**Hesk came in at about ten past eleven and read the sheet on the wall and then read it again and then said one sentence to Edda Tarn that she had not expected and never repeated to anybody.**

"That is a good notice," he said.

"It is a *notice*, Mr. Hesk. It is not a product. There is no reach line on the board this morning and there is not going to be one this afternoon and I want you to understand that what is on the wall is the reason, and it is not a good notice, it is a *confession with a stamp on it*, and if I have got this wrong I would rather be told now than on Monday."

"No," said Maro Hesk. "It is a good notice. **I have been in this trade ten years and I have never once been able to say the price of a river, and there is now a piece of paper in a public room which says the number and says what it is a number of.** That is not a confession. Nineteen years ago I would have paid a hundred pounds for a notice that said *the depth is not a reading*, and I would have put it in a drawer and used it about twice a year in an argument, and I would never once have printed it, and I would have been wrong about my own product for nineteen years and I would not have known."

He read the fourth line again.

"*A market which cannot sell a reach has to sell the record of the reach, and the record is worth what the reach is worth, and the two are the same number, and this firm does not know which.*"

"That is a good notice," he said again. "It is not a *product* and you are not paid for it and I have told the licensing clerk's office to stop billing it at all. Nine calls. One morning. And it cost the firm nothing and it cost me nineteen years, and the reason I know that is that I did not know it until about eleven o'clock this morning."

---

**And by the Tuesday afternoon the thing had to be printed, because a notice is on a wall and a price is on a list and the difference between them is that the list has to be filled in on a Monday and the wall is the wall.**

There were three places.

The counting floor, on the board, which is where everybody who works in the building looks. The quotation hall, on a plate, where a party comes to be sold something. And **the free district sheet, twice a day, at a counter in a shop on the tannery side of Tannery Walk**, which is where a district that cannot read the Office's own bulletin goes instead, and which is printed in a shop on a press that is not the Council's and which nobody in this building is allowed to inspect.

Edda Tarn wrote the same paragraph in the same words for all three, because a paragraph that says one thing in a counting floor and another thing on a shop counter is a paragraph that has been written twice, and a thing written twice is a thing somebody is managing.

And at the foot of it, in a smaller hand, which is the firm's own hand, which is a thing that has happened about four times in nineteen years, and which cost her something she had not been asked for:

*Published at the request of nine districts and with the refusals of four. The ninth ward was not asked, having refused in writing. The figure printed below is this firm's own and was published under the refund rule. It is not the district's figure, and no district's figure is printed here, and the firm is asked daily and has not been given one.*

**And on the Tuesday, at about four in the afternoon, she put the number on the wall that had been the firm's own number since the autumn, which was the one figure of the Bellweather reach anybody in this basin could see, and she wrote it down in the space where the line used to be, and it was 7/6, and it was the price they had struck through twice.**

She stood in front of it afterwards for a while with a cloth in her hand that she did not use.

"Mr. Lyle."

"Wend, ma'am."

"When the partner asks you why there is a number on a wall under a line that has been struck through twice, what are you going to say?"

Wend Lyle thought about it for longer than she had expected him to, and he said:

"I'm going to say that the refund rule published the price both times and that the rule is the firm's own and that it is the only reason the firm knows the number at all, and that the number is on the wall because we put it there, and not because anybody made us."

"That is a good answer." She wiped the board round the edges where there was no dirt, which is a thing counter clerks do. "**Do not learn to enjoy it.**"
