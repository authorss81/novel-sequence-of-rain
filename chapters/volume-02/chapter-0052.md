# Chapter 52 — The Counting Floor

The Cloud Exchange has four floors over a chandler's shop on the western side of the city, and only one of them is interesting, and it is the second, and it is the floor the firm is named for.

It is a long room with no windows on the wall side, lit from above, with four long tables and about forty people at them on an ordinary day. Every table is double-sided: buyers on one side of the board, sellers on the other, and between the two a clerk. There is a board the length of the room and the board is ruled in rows, and every row is a window: a number, an operation, hours, a price, and beside the price a single letter, and the letter is the standing of the window, which is either **F** for firm, **O** for open, or **W** for withdrawn, and there is no fourth letter and the firm does not have a fourth letter.

The refund rule is painted on the end wall, at the height of a standing man's eye, in white on green, in a hand that has not changed in nineteen years because it was painted by the founder and nobody has ever been allowed to repaint it:

**A WINDOW NOT OPENED IS REFUNDED AT THE QUOTED PRICE. THE PRICE IS PAID BACK WITHIN THE WEEK. THE PRICE OF THE WITHDRAWN WINDOW IS PUBLISHED.**

There is a plate under it, in brass, with a founder's name and a date, and the founder's name is Halvard Reoke and the date is the year the Exchange was licensed, and the brass has been polished so often that the date has gone soft in the middle and reads as 8-.

Maro Hesk was forty-one and he had built the second floor from eleven people to the forty it now held, and he was standing at the end of the room in a coat that had been a good coat eleven years ago, and he was reading a board.

He was not the founder's son. He had come into the trade as an underwriter at thirty-one, which is late, and he had spent his first four years being told by men who had been in it since the licences came out that there was no money in weather, that weather was a cost of doing business and not a business, and that the only honest position was to write the risk down and go and stand somewhere else. He had written the risk down for four years and had got extremely good at it and had understood, at about the age of thirty-five, that the thing everybody actually wanted and would not pay for honestly was *somebody to be answerable*.

What the Weather Office had was instruments. What it did not have, and had never had, and had said out loud in a public hall nine weeks ago in a column of its own devising, was anybody to be answerable for what the instruments had cost.

So he built a firm that is.

He had never once done anything dishonest. That is the part that people who dislike him find hardest, and it is entirely true, and he had checked it against himself very carefully in a hard winter two years ago, and the test he had applied was this: *if a competent hostile reader of the Exchange's records were given all of them, would they be able to write a sentence that was untrue?* And the answer had been no, and he had been proud of it in a way that had cost him a certain kind of friend, and he had understood at the time that this was the actual price of the product.

He had a good eye for a gap. Not a dishonest eye. The opposite: he could see, without wanting to, exactly where a thing in the world had no one standing next to it. He had been in the trade ten years and he had been the founder's man at the counter for four of them and the founder had retired to a coast and stopped answering letters.

---

He had been looking at the board for about four minutes when the whole problem arrived at once, which is how it happens to him, and which is why he keeps a piece of chalk in his hand at all times and does not sit down on that floor before eleven.

There were forty-one rows. Thirty-seven were open or firm. Four were withdrawn, and there are only four withdrawn windows in the history of the Exchange because a withdrawn window is a thing that costs money and the rule is that it costs the firm, and the firm has never once had a table discussion about whether to withdraw one.

He knew all four by heart. He had signed all four.

One was a Ternway lane barge window in the autumn before last, refunded at two and six after a wind shift that put the tide in half an hour wrong. One was a Nacre apron crane window, refunded in full, because a barge came in on somebody else's window and fouled the cradle, and the Exchange paid for that out of its own pocket rather than argue, and Hesk had insisted on paying out of its own pocket and had been right to. One was a Bellweather reach window on the fifth day of the Bellweather month, which the Exchange had quoted and withdrawn the same afternoon because the level came down two hours later than the tide table said it would, refunded at two and six.

And the fourth was the one on the wall.

It had no row number. It had been entered in the days after the weather, in the second week of the Bellweather month, by a young man called Ivo Slate on the authority of a partner who had gone home early, and it read:

**W · BELLWEATHER REACH, apron sill to bottom, 40 min, stated window. Quoted 2/0. REFUND PAID 1/0.**

And it had been struck through in pencil, in Hesk's own hand, on the fourth of Sleetmonth, and beside the strike-through he had written the refund and the date, because the rule says the price of the withdrawn window is published, and he had published it, and the price was a pound.

He had known for four weeks that the pound was wrong. He had known it the way a man knows that a figure in his own ledger is a figure he put there because he had nothing better, and which now has a market around it, and which will be quoted back at him by a man with a barge.

That was the problem and it was not a moral problem. A moral problem he could have had in ten minutes.

The problem was this: **a refund is the price of not selling. It is not the price of what the buyer would have done with the window.**

The Exchange's rule was that it never asks. It never asked what a buyer would have done, because asking would put the Office's answer — *we have no instrument that looks at a reach* — in a room where it was a commercial fact, and the Exchange's whole product is that it does not put the Office's answers in rooms, it replaces them.

But a firm that refunds at a quoted price, when it cannot ask what the window was worth, is a firm that has priced *its own uncertainty* and called it the buyer's loss. It is honest. It is also, and this is the part that had kept Hesk awake for three nights, **exactly as generous as a refund and exactly as inadequate, and the difference between those two things is a pound, and the pound is going to be quoted at him within a month by somebody who has read the rule and understood it.**

He picked up the chalk. He did not write anything on the board. He had a rule about the board.

---

"Mr. Hesk."

"Sit down, Edda, you've been standing there since ten and I saw you at ten."

"I'm not standing, I'm thinking at a volume."

"That's the same as standing in this building and it always has been." He put the chalk in his pocket. "What."

"The Office's bulletin came down at ten minutes past. Weekly, with the column." Edda Tarn was thirty-eight and had been a counter clerk for nine years and had the particular calm of a person who is very good and knows it. "The dry line column. There's a new item in it. Not a claim. A window."

Hesk went and got it. It was the printed weekly return from the Weather Office, the one that goes to the yard boards and the assembly rooms and the ward head offices, and it was two pages, and page two had the column, and at the foot of the column, in a fair hand, in the ordinary course, was one more line.

**DISPLACED TURNS — DRY LINES. (Headings not adopted by this Council.) Window, Bellweather reach, quoted and not opened, day of the claim, thirty days. Basis: window quoted against an exchange instrument. No claim of this office was made. No branch of this office was closed.**

Hesk read it twice.

"Who wrote that?" he said.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously and I'll tell you why in a minute. Read the heading."

The heading was in capitals and it had no attribution and no seal, and it said **DISPLACED TURNS — DRY LINES**, and underneath, in smaller type, in brackets: *Headings not adopted by this Council.*

"Whoever wrote it," said Edda Tarn, "wrote it in a hurry, at the top of a form, in the ordinary course, at the end of a long day, having been handed a column and told to carry it up."

"Yes," said Hesk. "That is exactly how it was written, and I would stake a year of my fee on it, and neither of us will ever know it and it does not matter." He folded the sheet once. "Edda. Put the weekly in the file under 'unattributed, unsolicited, and the reason it is useful.' And do not take it out of the file."

"Why?"

"Because in about four months one of the eight people in this room is going to be asked to explain a price to a man who has been hurt, and the man who has been hurt is going to be holding that sheet, and I want the sheet to be on our side of the table when it happens."

---

He had the afternoon to think about it and he used it, and what he thought about it was the thing he was best at, which was the shape of a gap.

The Exchange sold windows. A window was an undertaking, priced, refunded. To sell a window about the Bellweather reach you had to know two things, and the firm had exactly one of them.

The first was **when the reach would come down.** That was the level, and the level was theirs, and they had a float in a well and a chain over a drum and a glass datum cut by a man on a ridge who had been honest about his own limitations in writing, and the level was theirs, and it was the better of the two by a long way.

The second was **what forty minutes at the bottom of the reach is worth**, and that was not a level and it could not be measured with a chain, and the only person in the basin who had ever tried to put a figure on it was the Office, and the Office had refused in a public hall, in print, in nineteen words it was very proud of, and had then printed a column saying that the thing it could not price had nonetheless been lost.

And the Office was right. That was the part that had taken Hesk the whole afternoon.

The Office was right, and Hesk was grateful, and he was grateful in a way he had never said out loud in ten years of counting-house work and had certainly not said out loud in the last seven weeks, and he said it now to a piece of chalk on a counter, because the floor was nearly empty and nobody was in earshot and he had been in the trade a long time.

If the Office certified a window — if it entered a *claim* on the reach, with a place, an effect, a trigger, an expiry and a concurring seat, and struck a bell for it in a room with a witness in it — then the window would cost a branch. It would be better than theirs. It would be dependable in a way nothing else in this basin is dependable, and it would be unspendable without a Council quorum, and it would be published, and every buyer would have a piece of paper.

And there would be **fewer of them.** Not better ones. Fewer. Because a claim is expensive to enter and expensive to defend and every ward's seat in this basin would have to concur, and two of those seats have refused in living memory and one of them refused in the autumn in a chamber over a clause about a cost.

A market cannot run on a quorum. A market runs on **nobody having to agree**, which is the reason a market is a market and the reason a Council is a Council, and he had spent eleven years watching the two of them fail at each other in a way that was correct on both sides and cost both of them.

He said the whole of that out loud, in an empty room, to nobody, and then said the sentence that is the actual content of his life:

"Right," said Maro Hesk. "So we price the gamble. Where are we short?"

Two places, and he wrote them on the back of the Office's weekly in pencil, because he was that kind of man and because his own paper is not a document.

**One.** A forward board needs a price for a miss. Nobody knows what a missed reach window costs the party who paid for it. The refund rule gives him a number he invented in a hurry in the days after the storm, and a forward board printed at the end of this quarter will be read in the autumn by a man with a barge and forty minutes of a flat and a fortnight's hire, and the man will take the refund and the man will be right to take it, and the man will be owed more than he has been given, and the difference will come to the yard where the two of them are standing.

**Two.** A refund is the price of not selling. To pay anybody anything else, a firm has to know what the buyer was going to *do*, and the Exchange has a rule against asking, and the rule is correct, and the rule is now the reason the Exchange cannot pay the second claimant on a claim of any kind.

He looked at the two lines for a while.

Then he went and got a clerk, and the clerk was nineteen and called Oren Lyle, and he gave him two lines of work to take to the Council's technical cell, and they were:

**First. Ask the technical cell for the standing schedule of what each ward is able to do about its water in a season. Not our water. Their water. A list of works, a list of crews, a list of what a ward can be relied upon to have, and what it can be relied upon to be able to do, in a season, with a date against it.**

**Second. Ask them in the form they answer in, and take the form they send, and do not paraphrase it, and do not summarise it, and if they send us a form with nothing in it, then I want the form with nothing in it, kept, dated, and brought to me.**

Fisk wrote it down and looked up.

"They'll send us the price list first."

"They will send us the price list first," Hesk agreed, "and the price list is what is in front of us and the price list is the first thing anybody ever sends you, and I have a rule about the first thing anybody ever sends you, and I would rather have the second thing."

"And if the second thing's an empty form?"

"Then it is the most valuable piece of paper in this building," said Maro Hesk, "and I want it framed, and I do not want it framed for a moment."

He went back to the board and looked at row four, which was a withdrawn row with no number, and at a pound written in pencil in the margin in his own hand, and at the strike-through, which he had put there himself, correctly, on the correct grounds, in the ordinary course, on the fourth of Sleetmonth.

He did not rub it out. He had been asked to, once, by a partner, and he had said no, and the reason he had given was that the rule said publish it, and the true reason, which he had not given anybody, was that he had a bad feeling about the thirtieth of the month and about the low end of this city and about a reach he had never once measured, and that a man who has a bad feeling about the thirtieth of a month ought to keep the paper.

He turned round and said it to the room, because he said things to the room, that was his gift and it was why the forty people on the floor took his calls.

"Two things and then I'm going up. First: the front page goes up on Monday, in the yard and in the hall and on the third board at the chandler's, and it is the first price list this firm has ever published, and it is not a forecast and it does not contain a forecast, and if anybody asks me whether it is weather I will tell them it is a price."

"And the second?"

"The second is that we have a hole," said Maro Hesk, "and it is a real hole, and I would like anybody on this floor who thinks they can fill it to come and see me before the board goes up, because we would rather print the hole than print a lie, and I have given ten years of my life to not printing a lie, and I would like it noticed that the last seven weeks have been the first ten years of them in which it was even available to me to print one, and I did not notice for six of them."
