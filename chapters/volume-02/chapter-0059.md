# Chapter 59 — The Form With Nothing In It

Wend Lyle was nineteen and he had been in the Cloud Exchange since he was seventeen and he had a satchel he had bought himself out of four weeks of counter wages, and on the Thursday morning of the third day of Drownmonth he went into the Council's basin house on the ground floor with a letter in his hand that had been sitting on a spike in the district research desk for three weeks.

The letter was not his. It was a request, dated the thirteenth day of Sleetmonth, and it had been written out by a licensing clerk and initialled by a partner, and it asked the Council's technical cell for **the standing schedule of what each ward is able to do about its water**.

Underneath the request, in the same hand, in the same ink, at the same desk, on the same afternoon, there were two lines. Lyle had read them a hundred times and could have said them in his sleep, and the reason he could was that he had been told that a thing written down for the fourth time is worth more than a thing written down for the first, and he had copied them out into his own notebook on the fourteenth of Sleetmonth and never once looked at them since.

*Take the form they answer in. Do not paraphrase it.*
*If they send us a form with nothing in it, I want that form with nothing in it, kept, dated, and brought to me.*

The first of those two lines was the whole of his errand. The second of them was why he had not left the basement until twenty past eleven, and why his coat had a stone dust on the shoulder of it, and why he was going to have to explain to a partner why a boy of nineteen had spent four hours of a working morning sitting on a stool in a cold room being told, four separate times, by three separate men, that a form had nothing in it.

---

The technical cell's press is on the ground floor, against the end wall, and it is a press of the kind that was made for bound volumes: two doors of deal, a lock with a hasp, and a shelf under the bottom door where the current season's working had stood until it was bound.

The bound volume in it was a foot and a half thick and it had been in the press since before the building had a name anybody used, and it is the oldest instrument in this city that is still in daily use and the only one that nobody has ever thought of asking about.

It is not a record of anything. It is a **form**, filled in by hand, once a season, for two hundred and forty years, by whichever clerk of the ward's district was sent. A column for every one of the nine wards and a line for every season, and against each line the ward is to set out, in its own words and with a date against it, **its available responses to a season's water** — crews held, what it can put on a reach or a canal or a roof, what it can be relied upon to have and to be able to do.

At the head of each season's line there is a stub. It is detachable along a perforation, it is a third of a page, and it is the whole of the mechanism: a district detaches the stub, fills in the stub, and sends the stub in, and the cell enters it against the ward's line and the season and the book goes back on the shelf.

Which is the thing that took Lyle four hours in a cold room to understand, and which he understood at about twenty past ten and could not afterwards explain to anybody.

**A ward cannot answer a form that has not been sent to it, and nobody had ever sent this one to anybody.**

---

The man who handled the request was called Skell and he was a records officer in the technical cell, and he was not unkind.

He was also not, and Lyle understood this within about a minute and it took him the rest of the morning to stop being angry about it, **a man who treats an unanswerable request as a category.**

"There is no form for this," Skell said, on the first time, and he said it not unhelpfully but in the tone of a man reporting the depth of a river.

"It's a request for a schedule."

"It's a request for a thing that we do not issue."

"Then could you issue one."

"There is no form on which to write it." Skell turned round on his stool. "Son, I will tell you what I have got. I have got a book, and the book has got nine columns and a line for every season since — I don't know — before the survey. And the line is filled in by the district, and the district sends me the stub, and I enter it, and that is the entire process, and I have done it every year since I came here and before me and before him." He gestured at the press without turning round. "And the districts do not send me the stub. Some of them did. And now none of them do."

"Some of them did."

"The second and the seventh and the eighth. They're good. The eighth has never missed a season in my time and I would not let that book be re-laid for anything." He said it with a warmth that was not professional. "The ninth hasn't sent one since before I came and I have asked the man before me and he says the same."

"How long?"

"Forty-five years. Maybe. Long while." Skell put his thumb on the edge of the ledger. "There was a fella wanted it done properly and then he went, and after that nobody's wanted it. I couldn't tell you the year if you asked me. I wasn't here."

Lyle wrote it down. He wrote the whole of it down, in his own small hand, in the back of the notebook, with the date at the head and the time beside it, and he wrote the word *maybe* against forty-five, and he wrote *source: S., recd. off* the thumb* because a thing you have been told by a man who was not there is a thing you can swear to the having-been-told-of and not to the year, and Lyle was nineteen and had been a counter clerk for two years and had worked out that the difference between the two is the whole of the trade.

He did not know then that seven days later an assessor in this same building would write the year down without hedging, and that the year was a particular one, and that the man's name was on the inside face of that season's stub, in a good copperplate ink, in a hand that was not the clerk's, and that she would have it in front of her in ink and not in hearsay on the same morning, and would rule that neither of them could be ruled out.

What he knew at about ten past ten on the Thursday morning of the third of Drownmonth was that there was a bound volume in a press with a heading and nothing under the ninth ward's column, and that a man had told him so, and that the man had been in the building for a long time and had not gone and look.

---

They sent him to the basement for the forms.

The basement of the Council's basin house is two rooms and a coal hatch and it is colder than the ground floor by about four degrees on any day of a wet quarter, and the technical cell's share of it is a press of specimen sheets: every form the cell has ever issued, kept one of each, with a year on the spine, because a form changes slowly and a cell that cannot show you what it sent last time is a cell that gets the form wrong.

Lyle went down with a clerk who was about fifty and who did not come further into the room than the door.

**Page four.**

The spring form went back forty years and he did not have to hunt for it, because it was the only thing on the shelf that anybody had ever bothered to write a word on the outside of. It said, in a hand forty years old and gone brown at the edges: *the ninth sent one of these.* And it was a four-page form and **three of the four pages were for the Council's own use** — return dates, district office, a reference number, a box for a clerk's signature, and a great deal of ruled space that was not for anybody.

Page four had one box on it. One. Across the top of it, in a better hand than the rest of the form, which was the hand of whoever had set the type and not of whoever had written it: **LOCAL FLOOD PRECAUTIONS — LIST.**

That was all. A ruled box, a heading, and a date line, and about four inches of room, and there was no second box and no schedule and no column and nothing whatever that told a district that what it was being asked for was a record of what it could do and could be relied upon to do.

And under the box, on the same page, in a fair hand, in ink, with a stamp over the bottom of it — a stamp that had gone purple and stayed purple for the rest of its life and was the only thing on that page that had been touched by anybody since — there were four lines.

*Third block low side, catch pits, six with the water steward.*
*Clearing rota, Tuesday and Friday, from the second block's grate.*
*Pump house two by hand. Pump house one on a call.*
*Nine can be got to a flat by nine in the morning. Not nine by six.*

That was the ninth ward's answer, in the ninth ward's own hand, in the only box it was ever given, four years ago, in answer to a form that had three pages for the Council.

**Eighteen pages came out of that box.** That was the thing Lyle understood, standing in a cold room in a cellar with a clipboard he had not been given and no authority to write on, and it is the whole of the argument in one document: a district asked a question it did not know it was being asked, in a form with no room in it, and answered it correctly and briefly, and somebody in this building then made eighteen pages out of four lines, and the eighteen pages were the only schedule of the ninth ward in the world, and they were in a market file and not in this press.

"Can I see the return?" he said.

"No."

"It's in the building somewhere. It's stamped. Somebody stamped it and it went where they stamp things."

Skell looked at him for slightly longer than was comfortable.

"Whatever it went to, it did not come to me," he said. "That's the honest answer and it's the whole of the honest answer. The cell gets the stub. The cell has always got the stub. A district's return is not a stub." He shut the press. "Son, I've got a shelf of nine hundred forms in that cellar and not one of them has got a schedule on it, and that's not because anybody's hiding anything from you, because if I was hiding something I'd have to have known where to put it. The forms are like this. That's the whole reason there's nothing in the book. The forms are like this and then nobody comes."

Lyle stood in the doorway of a coal hatch of a basement for about four seconds and then said the thing that was out of scope, and he knew it was out of scope while he was saying it, and he said it because he was nineteen.

"Has the Council ever sent it out?"

Skell stopped.

"Has anybody," Lyle said, "ever sent one of those books to a district. Not the book. A stub. A page. Anything, with a stamp on it and the ward's name on it and somewhere to send it back to."

And the man who had been in the building for a long time and had not gone and look sat on a stool in a cellar and thought about it for about as long as it takes to count nine.

"No," he said.

"Ever."

"No, son. In the time of this building and in the time of the one before it. The stub's for filling in and there's no envelope with it and there's no address on the cover of that book and there's nobody in nine districts that has ever heard of it, and I'll tell you how I know nobody's heard of it." He nodded at the press on the ground floor, through the ceiling. "Somebody'd have to have knocked a hole in nine ward boundaries to get nine answers out of nine clerks in one season, and I have never in my life seen a form in nine districts go out with a stamp on it. I've never seen one *come in* with a stamp on it either, come to that. Not one. Not in thirty-one years. They arrive. They get stamped here and they go back to whoever they came from because there is nothing to be done with them in this room, and the stamping is the part that lasts."

He got up off the stool, which took him a while.

"That's your answer, son. You can write it down. Nobody ever sent it out, and I could not tell you the last one that came in, and the reason your form's empty is not that somebody is keeping the wards off the page. The reason your form's empty is that the page is in a book in a press and the book has never been in the same room as a district, and there is nobody in this building who has ever been paid to put it there."

---

The form went back up the stairs and out of the basement and across the ground floor and into the press on its shelf, and it is worth being exact about what came out of it, because almost everything that follows in this quarter happens to this piece of paper.

It came out with the heading filled in. The Council's technical cell, the standing response, the season, the date. Four lines of good fair hand at the top of a printed sheet, done by a man who had never seen the form before and had asked for a pen.

And underneath the four lines, **nothing.**

Not ruled off. Not stamped *no return*. Not stamped *declined*. Nothing at all — nine columns' worth of ruled lines, going down the sheet, one for each ward, and the season written once across the top of the lot, and the whole of the rest of the page as blank and as correct as the day it was printed.

The ninth ward's line was empty. The fourth's was empty. The sixth's had a run of about nine seasons with a season in against them and nothing under the season.

And that was the discovery, and it was not the Council's, and it was not Lyle's.

Lyle walked it back to the Exchange on the Thursday afternoon, up the hill through the wet quarter, with the flat in the satchel under his coat, and he did not take it straight to a partner, because he was nineteen and he had been four hours in a cellar and he was going to be asked what a form with nothing in it is worth and he wanted to know the answer before he was asked.

So he went to the district research desk on the second floor and he laid the flat out on the counter beside a rack of returns, and it took him about a quarter of an hour, and nobody stopped him, because it was a form and it was a boy's job and the desk had a rule of its own about not interrupting anybody who was being careful.

**The desk had four hundred yards of paper about districts.**

Returns, quarterly returns, spring forms, late returns, a bound set of Hearthward's for eleven years, a Ferrygate file, a file of water office abstracts for the north side, a shelf of the Council's own reclassified local records that came to the firm on a subscription because the Council sold its own old paper. Four wards' worth of what those wards *do*. And nine columns' worth of what the Exchange *knows about* those wards, which is a different book entirely, and is the most valuable thing in the building, and is a book of prices.

And there was not one line in any of it that a district had written about itself in answer to a question the firm had put to it.

Lyle took the flat — the Council's form, heading filled, season filled, nothing under — and he put it on the desk, and he took a pen, and he tried to fill in the ninth ward's line himself, out of his own firm's records, in the ordinary course, the way anybody in a counting house fills in a form.

**He could not do it, and it took him about eleven minutes to find out why, and the reason is the same reason as the cellar.**

He could write: *Reach, low water, nine usable mornings a year. Market instrument at the south bank, see our sheet.* And that was a price and an advertisement and it was not a response, and if it had gone on that form it would have been the Exchange describing a ward's water to itself.

He could write: *Crews, per the ward's return on file, held in season.* And that was a return the firm had bought for eleven shillings off a councillor in a hurry, and it was a true document, and putting it on a Council form in the Exchange's hand would have been the Exchange answering the Council about the ward and calling the ward's own words its own.

And there was one thing he could write that would have gone in and been worth something, and he wrote it out on a scrap and then crossed it out, because he could not get a date against it.

The Exchange knew what the ninth ward's reach had done on every morning for six weeks. It had a float, a chain, a helical groove, a glass datum cut and ground and seated by a maintenance hand on a ridge a hundred and forty miles away, and it read the instrument four times a day and printed the result free, twice a day, at a counter in a shop, and a man with a fifth column in a keeper's book on a tower a hundred and forty miles the other way had compared the two of them for three days and got twelve agreements out of twelve and had told nobody.

The Exchange had more about the ninth ward's water than the ninth ward did, and it had never once asked.

That was the discovery and it did not need a cellar or a press or a man on a stool. It needed a boy and a form and a rack. **The firm that had spent four pounds eleven and three weeks and a licensing clerk's afternoon obtaining a Council schedule of what a ward could do had, on its own second floor, four hundred yards of paper about wards and no question in any of it.**

The reason was not anybody's villainy. The reason was on the wall in the quotation hall, under the eleven lines, in the terms: *The Exchange is not a party to any district's preparations and is not required to price them.* And a form is a party. And a question is a party. And so, on that same second floor, on that same afternoon, the firm that had gone out and bought a district's rain for eleven shillings in advance of asking had a rule under which asking was not permitted, and had been running inside that rule for ten years, and had never once been asked to account for it, because no district had ever been in a position to ask, and a district cannot ask a firm a question it has no standing to ask.

Lyle sat on the edge of that desk for about ten minutes with the flat in front of him and worked out that the Council and the market were the same shape, from opposite ends of the same street, and that both of them had an empty form, and that neither of them was lying, and that both of them had an answer and neither of them had a question.

Then he put the flat in a folder, and wrote on the folder, in his own hand, the firm, the season, and the date, and carried it in to a partner, because he had been told to bring it to a partner even if it was empty, and it was empty, and there was nothing in the state of this basin that made that a reason not to walk in.

He put it on the desk and said, "It's empty, Mr. Hesk. All of it, all nine, and the sixth has got a run of nine and the heading's filled in and the season's filled in and there's nothing under any of it."

Hesk read the four lines at the top of it. He did not turn it over. He read the four lines and then he put his thumb on the blank ruled lines underneath, the way a man puts a thumb on a line of a book he has read before, and he said:

"Who told you the forty-fifth?"

"The records officer. Mr. Skell. He said maybe, and he said he couldn't tell you the year if you asked him, and he wasn't here."

"Was there a year on the spine?"

"There was a year on the spine. I didn't copy it because he hadn't said it and I wasn't going to have a year in my notebook he hadn't given me." Lyle heard how old he sounded and did not care.

"Good," said Maro Hesk.

Then he said, in about the tone of a man making a note about a delivery, "Hand it back, will you. Keep the folder, write today's date on the top corner in your own hand, and bring it to me again in the morning."

---

**It came back to him on the Friday morning of the fourth of Drownmonth, and he dated it, and it went into the satchel he had bought himself out of four weeks of counter wages, and that is where all of this starts.**

*Cloud Exchange · district research · form received from the Council's technical cell, 3rd Drownmonth · season as filled · no return under any ward heading · C. F. Skell, recd. — W. Lyle.*

Underneath that, in the same hand and the same ink, because it was the same instruction and he was the same boy: *Nothing under it. Kept as it came. Brought as ordered.*

That was the whole of the errand. Four pounds eleven, three weeks, and a licensing clerk's afternoon, and a flat sheet of Council paper with four lines of good fair hand at the top of it and nothing whatever underneath, and a firm that had gone to a great deal of trouble to be told that it already knew.

He went down the hill at ten past eight on the Friday morning with the folder under his coat, and it was raining, ordinary, and the season's second back-cut was a fortnight off and nobody in this basin knew it was coming, and the man who had asked for the form was going to look at it for about four minutes and then ask for a piece of paper and go and write a letter to a ward, and **the ninth ward had a clerk who would read that letter, and had not yet been asked for anything, and would answer it, and the answer would be the best document anybody in Veyra produced that month.**
