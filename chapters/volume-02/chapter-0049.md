# Chapter 49 — Private Work

The maintenance shop is on the ground floor of the Weatherglass's north wing, below the records floor and below the chart room, and it has four benches, a diamond scribe, a grindstone that somebody's grandfather bought, and a window that looks out at the eastern ridge and at nothing else at all. It is eight degrees colder in Sleetmonth than it was in the Bellweather month. The bench nearest the door has a bad joint and Marek has been meaning to re-glue it since the autumn before last.

He was in at seven. He had been in at seven every morning for seven weeks, because there was nothing to be late for.

The rules had been read to him in a corridor on the forty-fifth day of the Bellweather month by a Deputy who had said *this is not a suspension* four times, and each time he had said *I understand*, and each time the Deputy had waited a beat longer than a person waits, and at the end of it the Deputy had said, with what Marek has since decided was the best of him, *the benches are not a privilege and they are not a punishment. They are what is left.*

So: the benches.

A censured reader may work a bench. He may be on the ridge. He may take private commissions at his own hours, in his own time, on his own terms, and charge what the work is worth, because the Office's own rate for private glasswork is a third of a private glassmaker's and the Office has always used that difference to keep its hands busy and has never once said so out loud.

He may not take a watch. He may not be put on a roster. He may not sign a reading for this office. He may not attest the order of anything. He may not enter a claim, and he may not be named in the sponsor's line of one, and if a claim is entered on a bench in a building he happens to be standing in, he is a man in a room and nothing else.

There is no form for any of that. There is one line in a record, dated, signed by a Deputy, every clause of which is true.

What a censured reader loses, in money, is the field differential. A rostered reader on a named watch draws a shilling and fourpence the hour and a wet-watch supplement, and the whole of it, on a Bellweather watch, is most of what a maintenance hand earns in a week. Marek had done two hundred and six field hours in the Bellweather month. He had not done one since.

The arithmetic was not interesting. It had been done, and it did not improve.

---

Ilse Doff came in at about half past nine with the shop's second-best apron over a good one and a manila envelope under her arm, and she put the envelope on the bench nearest the door, which was the one with the bad joint, and looked at it.

"That'll be the credit," she said. "Nineteen years I've had the last of the credit. Take the lot."

Marek looked at the envelope.

"That is not mine."

"It's the shop's."

"I work in the shop."

"You're not in the shop this morning, are you." She had her hands in the apron pockets and she was not enjoying this and had decided to get through it in the fewest possible words, which was her way of being kind. "Mr. Vale. You're a reader. You're a *censured* reader, and the paper says there's no work coming down from the Office for you, and the paper's right, because the Deputy has told Field three times and Field has stopped asking. That's not me. I can't put you on the ridge instruments, I can't put you on a watch, and if I put you on a repair call out of town I'd be doing something I've no authority to do and somebody would find it in about a year and it would be my name on it and I'd be sixty-one in a shed with no bench."

"I know what the paper says."

"Then what's the paper."

Marek turned the envelope over. It was a job docket from the Cloud Exchange, addressed to the Weatherglass maintenance shop, care of the maintenance foreman, and it had a number on it in a fair hand and a date and a box that wanted a description of the work and a box that wanted a price.

"They've written to us," he said.

"They have written to us." Ilse Doff took her hands out of the apron. "Three times this week. The first one I put in the wrong drawer and I want that on the record before anybody says I didn't. The second one I put in the right drawer and I brought it to you this morning because you are the only person in this building who can do it and because the last time I brought you something that was going to be difficult you made me look stupid in front of the shop, and I would rather be stupid in front of the shop than unemployed, and I have nineteen years."

Marek read the docket.

It wanted a sounding float, an iron chain of a stated length and stated pitch, a drum, and a sealed glass cylinder to be cut, ground, annealed, and seated as a datum. It wanted a datum very much indeed. It wanted the datum in a brass seat with three adjusting screws, and it wanted the whole thing to be repeatable to a quarter of an inch, twice, at an interval, by two different people with two different chains.

"It's a gauge," he said.

"It's a gauge," said Ilse Doff.

"It's a tide gauge."

Ilse Doff sat down on the stool by the grindstone, which she was not supposed to do, and put her elbows on her knees.

"Mr. Vale," she said. "I have been in this building nineteen years and I have never in my life seen a piece of glasswork come in from outside that I couldn't tell what it was for inside about a minute. I could tell you what that is from the door. It is a *datum*, it is a thing you grind to a length so that a chain pays out true, and it is going into a *well*, and the well is a hole cut in a wall with a copper float in it, and the wall is in a shed at the south end of the freight apron, and I have driven past that apron in my life perhaps four times and I could not tell you what was on it then and I could not tell you now."

"You could say it out loud, though."

"I could say it out loud," she agreed, "and I have said it out loud, and that is the entire extent of what I know about it, and I would be obliged if you would now do the work and charge them the money and let me go back upstairs."

---

The float was on the bench by ten and it was better work than Marek had expected, which was the first thing that made him stop and think.

It was a drum of drawn copper, about fourteen inches over and nine deep, with a riveted seam that had been dressed flat and then polished until you could not find the seam with a thumbnail, and it was ballasted with three pounds of fine gravel set in pitch at the bottom, and the pitch had been poured level and had been *the pitch that decides whether a float is any use at all*, and this one had been poured by a man who knew it.

A chain of two hundred and forty feet, three-eighths of an inch, with links of a proper closed pattern and a swivel at each end and a shackle that was new and would be the first thing to fail.

A drum, turned, with a helical groove cut in it that was not quite square and not quite helical, and the not-quite was deliberate and was the whole of the trick: a helical groove pays the chain out at a constant rate per turn whatever the chain's pitch, and a square groove pays it out at the chain's pitch, and a chain stretches.

And the glass.

The glass was a cylinder about nine inches long and an inch and a quarter through, sealed, and it was not optical glass and it was not plate glass. It was a soda-lime tube that had come out of a stock bin of instrument stock, drawn, and it was slightly green at the edge, and it had a ground flat at one end and it had been *annealed*, and the annealing was the part that mattered and it had been done properly, in a lehr, over nine hours, because somebody had paid for nine hours.

Marek held it up to the window and turned it.

"You can seat this," he said.

"That's what they say in the letter."

"They say a great deal in the letter." He put it down. "It's not Office stock and it was not ordered by this office and there's no mark on it. Who cut it?"

"Ivo Slate," said Ilse Doff, from the stool. "The agent. He came and looked at the benches on the Monday and he asked three questions, one of which was about the scribe and one of which was about the lehr and one of which was about how much a sealed tube costs, and he wrote all three down in a little book, and I have watched men in coats do that for nineteen years and it is the only honest thing a man in a coat has ever done in this building."

---

The agent came at half past two.

He was courteous in a way that was clearly not a performance, which is a rarer thing than courtesy that is. He was about forty, in a good coat, with a satchel and a little book and a printed card, and he gave the card to Marek and not to Ilse Doff, and he said his own name without being asked and said what he was licensed to do and what he was not licensed to do, and the not list was longer and he read it out.

"Ivo Slate," he said. "Licensed agent, Cloud Exchange, western district and the yards. I am not licensed to give advice on weather, and I am not licensed to make a forecast, and if anybody tells you I have, they have mistaken an agent for something else. What I am licensed to do is quote a product and take a booking."

"For a gauge."

"For a *datum*."

"There's no difference."

"There is a difference of four pounds," said Ivo Slate, "and the difference is the jar, and the jar is a service and the service is what you are being asked to make repeatable. In three months, when the well has been cut and the float is in it and there is water in it most of the day, somebody is going to take a reading off that chain and write it in a book, and if the chain has stretched the reading will not be the reading, and if the reading is not the reading then nobody can price anything with it, and the Exchange does not price anything it cannot measure twice."

Marek looked at him.

"That's the whole job, is it," he said. "You want it repeatable."

"I want it repeatable," said Slate, "and I want the person who made it to be a person I can name, and I have your name off a work order that came down here on Monday and I would have preferred to have asked you for it."

He said the last part looking at the floor, and it was not a performance either.

Marek took the tube to the bench and set it in the jaws and started the scribe on a piece of waste, cutting a seat mark, and Ivo Slate stood where he was invited to stand and did not talk, and for about forty minutes the shop was a shop.

Then, at about ten past three, Slate said:

"May I ask you something you don't have to answer."

"You may ask."

"Where did you read it?" He nodded at the chain, the drum, the datum, the whole arrangement on the bench. "Not which building. Which tower. Because there are nine glass benches in this basin and the Office has eight of them and you are not working in this one today as far as I can see, and I am asking because a datum has to be seated against something that is already there, and if whatever it is seated against is in a building I cannot get into, then I have bought a very good jar for a well nobody can read."

Marek kept his hand where it was.

"There is no tower," he said. "It's a river wall. The well is cut in a masonry wall and the wall has a brass plug in it from when it was built, and the plug has a number on it, and the number is the only fixed thing on that apron that has not moved since the year it was put in."

Ivo Slate wrote that down.

"Thank you," he said. "That is a complete answer and it is better than the one I expected to have to work around."

Then, in the same tone, entirely without weight, the way a man asks a second question he has decided to ask:

"And the reading that goes in the book — the one off the chain — whose is it?"

"It'll be whoever is standing there."

"Somebody has to be paid to stand there."

"Somebody does."

"Yes," said Slate. "That is the part of this business I have never got used to, and I have been in it eleven years."

Marek wrote the price on the docket and Slate wrote it too, in his little book, and they agreed on it in about ninety seconds because it was not a difficult piece of work and both of them knew what it was worth, which was the first honest negotiation Marek had had in seven weeks.

---

He worked it on the Wednesday and the Thursday.

The seating took most of a day because the flat has to be ground to a length and not to a *sight*, and a length is a thing you can put a caliper on and a sight is a thing you can only be argued out of. He ground it twice, checked it against the chain's own marked link, and ground it a third time on Thursday morning because on Thursday morning he was not satisfied, and he was not satisfied because his left ear had a high thin sound in it that had been there for nine weeks and that he had not told anybody about and that had nothing whatever to do with glass, and which had put his hand half an inch off the scribe twice on Wednesday afternoon.

He did not mention it. He had not mentioned it in nine weeks and he was not going to start on a Thursday in front of a docket.

He was, however, extremely careful, in a way that had a shape to it that Ilse Doff noticed and did not comment on, and she noticed because she had watched him work for six years and had seen him get that shape twice before, both times in the week after the ninth of the Bellweather month.

The anneal he did not do. There was no lehr in this building. The tube came annealed from a man in Kestrel Yards who had charged for nine hours and had done it properly, and Marek signed the docket for a piece of work he had checked and had not done, which is a thing a glassmaker does perhaps twice in a career and always declares, and he declared it in the box on the docket where the description of the work went.

At about four on Thursday he took his coat off the hook, and the coat had a tube in the inside pocket, and the tube had a nine-inch square of sequence glass in it that had been cut out of the crown of a pane a poor ward had paid for two years ago and had never been sent, and there was nothing in the pocket that belonged to the Cloud Exchange and nothing in the tube that belonged to anybody, and he put the coat on and went out to the yard to see Slate's van had gone.

Then he came back in, because the cuff needed doing.

The inside of the left cuff of that coat had three blocks of pencil in it and had had three blocks of pencil in it since the eighth day of the Bellweather month, which was the last time there was room. Two readings and a filing mark, and the third block had gone at about two in the afternoon on the thirtieth with a half sentence on it: *reach mark off post 07:20, flat 40 ft, 3 in by 08* — and the rest of the line was *reach came back*, which is Petrie's phrase and not his, and he had stopped in the middle of it, because there was nowhere to go, and because he had been standing in mud at the time.

He turned the cuff up and looked at it for a while.

There is no fourth block. That is not a thing a cuff does.

He looked at the Cloud Exchange job docket on the bench, which was lying there because Slate had left it, and which had a wide blank margin down its right-hand side, because job dockets are printed with a margin for corrections and for the person who does the work to initial things, and because the form was designed by a firm that expects people to be wrong on it.

He took the docket, and he wrote in the margin, in pencil, in his own small neat hand, the first block of the next place:

*Thu, Sleet 12. Reach gauge datum seated. 240 ft chain, 3/8, closed pattern. Float drum 14x9, gravel ballast, pitch poured level — not mine, Sallow's. Groove helical, not square; chain pitch will not carry. Datum ground to length, checked on the marked link, cut twice. Ear: wrong side. Twice.*

And underneath it, smaller, and he hesitated about that one for a while and then wrote it:

*This docket is the Exchange's paper and goes back to a counting floor. There is no cuff space. From here the paper is the record and the paper is not mine.*

Ivo Slate's agency report on this commission is dated the sixteenth of Sleetmonth and is one line long and reads: *Datum made and certified repeatable to a quarter of an inch at an interval by two persons. Maker named on the docket and willing to be named again. No further particulars required at this time.*

He filed it, because he files everything, and he never in his life opened that file again.

---

The address on the docket was a shed on the Bellweather freight apron, at the south end, past the chain, on the upstream side, and there were four sheds on that side and only one of them had a new brick course under the sill that had not been there in the autumn.

Marek knew the apron. He had been on it at about half past three in the morning on the thirtieth of the Bellweather month in nine inches of moving water, and he had stood on it at about eight o'clock the same morning in the mud in a work coat, and he had said *it was just the water* to a foreman who had kept a book for thirty years and had a column in it that had never once been filled.

He had not been back down the hill since. There had been no reason on earth, and he had made sure, every week for seven weeks, that there was no reason on earth.

The van tracks in the yard were old and had gone soft in the frost, and there was a lantern burning at the door of the third shed, and the door was open, and from inside it came a sound Marek stopped in the yard to listen to properly, because he did not know the sound and it took him a second to place it.

It was a chain, being paid out slowly over a drum by somebody who was being careful.

It was a hundred and forty yards from the gate of a yard, and it was not going to be nearer to anything in this city than the yard.

Marek stood in the yard for about as long as it takes to decide something, and then went to see whether the jar was the right length, because it was, and because he had signed for it.
