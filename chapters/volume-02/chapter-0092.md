# Chapter 92 — The Held Trace

They hold the trace on the evening of the fifteenth of Miremonth because the hours the ward worked on the tenth have to be checked, and a reading published at the moment of seeing is a reading he has not yet checked, and it is her term, and he has stopped arguing with it.

The bench is in the outer room of the maintenance shop under the north window, the only bench on the ridge a censured reader is allowed to work at. It is a plank on two trestles with a felt strip along the front edge and a clock on the wall above it that is four seconds fast and has been four seconds fast since the spring. The strip comes out of the press at the back end at seven o'clock, cool, smelling of the shop, and the working copy is laid on the plank with the tower end to the left because she is four miles off in a tower and the left is the side of the room she is in.

She is up the ladder he has still not gone up, four miles off in the ninth ward tower, at the local end, on the tower's own feed with the plate in its seat and the spare boxed on the shelf under it and neither of them looking at the substrate of it and neither of them mentioning it.

"Do the middle first," she says down the wire. "You always do the ends first and then you have nothing to compare."

"I do the ends first because the ends are what the office has."

"The office has had the ends since the autumn. I would like the middle while there is a middle."

He does the middle first. It holds from about seven until about ten, ridge end to tower end, working copy to strip, two channels with somebody else's competence at one of them, and the strain comes on at about eight and it is the fourth time in two months and it is not the worst: the metallic taste, and the pressure half a tone up on the left.

A held trace is three hours of a room with a lit strip in it and a wire going four miles to a tower, and the two of them are on their own with it, and the room does not fill up at night the way a room does in a shop. There is a lamp over the bench and a clock four seconds fast and the rain on the north window, and after the first hour neither of them says anything that is not about the strip.

At about eight he eats a piece of bread that Ilse Doff has left under the bench glass with the lehr log, and he does not take his eyes off the strip to do it, and at about half past eight he asks her to repeat an hour.

She repeats it. He writes it down. Then he asks her to repeat it a second time, and she repeats it again, patiently, down a wire from four miles away, the way a foreman repeats a rack count, and he says thank you and writes it down again.

**Nobody in the room remarks on it, because there is nobody in the room. The wire does not remark. He has asked a question twice, and he does not know he does it.**

Between about eight and about nine they talk about the tenth, which is what the evening is for, and about the water steward, whose name is Beddoe, and who wrote the morning in a book in his own hand.

"Off at half past one," says Sable Orin. "Was it."

"Nine minutes at both ends and the steward had the flat for forty and he had it off the flat before the water came back up the south end, because he has done it in his head for twenty years and nobody ever let him."

"And he wrote it down."

"He wrote it down. In a book. His own hand, with the hour and the gang on it and what the gang did, and the pump gang's gear, and the number of the gang, which is six and a steward, and he wrote it the same way he does everything, which is as though the book were going to be asked for."

**The ward's window is the only one in this basin that anybody wrote down at the time, and it is written in a book in a pump house, and nobody has asked for it, and it will be there in a year for whoever does.**

"He will be disappointed," says Sable Orin, "when nobody asks for it."

"Yes," says Marek Vale.

"That is not your problem. It is the problem of everybody in this basin who has ever kept anything. You will write yours up and put it in a drawer and hope the drawer is deep, and I will tell a warden a thing in a tower and there will be a book with the date in it, and neither of those is the same as being asked."

"That is the second time you have asked me that."

"It is the second time you have not answered it."

And then, at about nine, in the middle of the held trace, he sees it.

A movement in the middle, in neither sequence, about a fifth of a second long. Arriving before the movement it belongs to and going when the movement goes. In neither end. Not his. Not hers. In the middle.

The strip has a small vertical jump in it and then nothing, and before the jump there is a fifth of a second of a line he can hold in his eye, and the line is not in the tower's end and it is not in the working copy, and it is not his hand, because his hand is nine inches away from the strip doing nothing at all.

He looks at it for a while. He does not put his pencil on it.

He lifts the glass weight off the end of the strip without breaking the hold, and looks at the section under the lamp, and puts the weight back, and it is a thing he could measure if there were an instrument in this building that measured it, and there is not one, and the Office has never had one, will not build one, and does not consider the absence a defect in its work, and he has read that in a circular and thought at the time that the circular was about a river and was wrong about a strip of paper.

**He sees it and he does not report it and nobody remarks on it. It is a physical fact, and it is the only time it is named.**

She is talking about the fee and the board and the reading going up free to a warden before any claim, and he hears all of that, and he looks at the strip, and the jump is five hundredths of a millimetre and it is there, and it went when the movement it belongs to went, and it came before it, and it is a fifth of a second long, and it is in the middle of a held trace on a plank on a ridge in the rain, four miles from the only other person in it.

He does not tell her. He writes the hour and the two numbers and the nine minutes on the sheet, and he says, "You were saying about the board," and she says, "I was saying the reading goes on a board a warden is welcome to read, free, before the claim, and that is the term, and it is not an argument," and he writes that down too, because he writes everything down, which is the whole of what he is.

They let the trace go at about ten, sideways, both feeling it at the same instant, which they agree afterwards is the correct sign.

"What did you get?" she asks down the wire.

"The hours," says Marek Vale. "Half past one and eleven past four. The nine minutes stands."

"Write it up," says Sable Orin. "And I will put a fee on it. And the reading goes on a board a warden is welcome to read, free, before the claim."

He writes it up on the back of his own copy of Circular 15, in pencil, in his own small hand, four hundred words, the longest thing he has written in his working life, with the line in the middle about stopping arguing and not stopping believing, and the cost at the foot: metallic, third day running, left pressure moved wrong way again, left side quieter afterwards, not tested, not told.

**The third block of the cuff is full since the thirtieth of the Bellweather month. There is no fourth block on a cuff to put it on.**

He signs it. He does not date the fourth line and does not notice.

He puts the sheet in the drawer with the two unfiled tags, and closes the drawer, and stands at the north window for a minute with the rain on it, looking at nothing on the ridge, at a shed roof and a fence and the black shape of the hill the tower is on, four miles off and invisible.

The shop shuts at ten, the same as it has shut every night since the autumn, and the key of it is his and the key of the tower is a different key in another pocket, and the strip is in the drawer, and the shop's day book is under glass with the lehr log and will be opened in the morning by a foreman who does not know that anything was held tonight.

On the way out he opens his mouth to tell her a thing, at about half past ten, with the tower light on and her voice coming down the wire, and he does not.

**He still has not told anybody and he still intends to.**
