# Chapter 92 — The Held Trace

They hold the trace on the evening of the fifteenth of Miremonth because the Miremonth window has to be checked and a reading published at the moment of seeing is a reading he has not yet checked, and it is her term, and he has stopped arguing with it.

The bench is in the outer room of the maintenance shop under the north window, the only bench on the ridge a censured reader is allowed to work at. She is up the ladder he has still not gone up, four miles off in the ninth ward tower, at the local end, on the tower's own feed with the plate in its seat and the spare boxed on the shelf under it and neither of them looking at the substrate of it and neither of them mentioning it.

It holds from about seven until about ten, ridge end to tower end, working copy to strip, two channels with somebody else's competence at one of them, and the strain comes on at about eight and it is the fourth time in two months and it is not the worst: the metallic taste, and the pressure half a tone up on the left.

At about half past eight he asks her to repeat an hour. She repeats it. He writes it down. Then he asks her to repeat it a second time, and she repeats it again, patiently, down a wire from four miles away, the way a foreman repeats a rack count, and he says thank you and writes it down again.

**Nobody in the room remarks on it, because there is nobody in the room. The wire does not remark. He has asked a question twice, and he does not know he does it.**

And then, at about nine, in the middle of the held trace, he sees it.

A movement in the middle, in neither sequence, about a fifth of a second long. Arriving before the movement it belongs to and going when the movement goes. In neither end. Not his. Not hers. In the middle.

**He sees it and he does not report it and nobody remarks on it. It is a physical fact, and it is the only time it is named.**

They let the trace go at about ten, sideways, both feeling it at the same instant, which they agree afterwards is the correct sign.

"What did you get?" she asks down the wire.

"The hours," says Marek Vale. "Half past one and eleven past four. The nine minutes stands."

"Write it up," says Sable Orin. "And I will put a fee on it. And the reading goes on the board a warden is welcome to read, free, before the claim."

He writes it up on the back of his own copy of Circular 15, in pencil, in his own small hand, four hundred words, the longest thing he has written in his working life, with the line in the middle about stopping arguing and not stopping believing, and the cost at the foot: metallic, third day running, left pressure moved wrong way again, left side quieter afterwards, not tested, not told.

**The third block of the cuff is full since the thirtieth of the Bellweather month. There is no fourth block on a cuff to put it on.**

He signs it. He does not date the fourth line and does not notice.

On the way out he opens his mouth to tell her a thing, at about half past ten, with the tower light on and her voice coming down the wire, and he does not.

**He still has not told anybody and he still intends to.**
