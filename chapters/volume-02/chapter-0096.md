# Chapter 96 — Lit for Money

The hearing room in the basin house is empty on the nineteenth of Miremonth except for the clerk, and the ledger is open on the trestle, and the rain is coming down the hill outside.

It has rained like this for nine days and there is no back-cut in it and nobody is waiting for one. The season's band is still over the basin and the small clear morning was the eleventh and there is nothing after it on any calendar anybody in this building keeps.

On the trestle there are three things. One is the bound book about two inches thick with a printed head, open at the payment page, with the date against it. The second, beside it, is the Standing Response Register itself, open at a page headed *available responses* with a date against it and nothing under the date. The third, beside that, is a salvage man's schedule on two sheets of ordinary paper, in a fair round hand, priced: forty-one entries to the penny, a hundred and sixty yards on a good forty minutes and a hundred and twenty on a short one, the clearing at 3/-, 3/6 and 4/1, the crew of four and a foreman, the tackle, the two carts, the N end first.

**The temporary compensation ledger expires at the end of the financial year. It is a ledger and not a fund. It pays documented loss only. It has paid one claimant and refused another in the same afternoon.**

Peascod works the payment page with the flat of his hand, because a ruled book that has had a wet thumb on it in the autumn has a curl in it and the entries are in a fair hand and he is not going to have the curl put a smudge through the seventh and six.

The page reads as it reads. The ninth ward, seventh and six, the window of the morning of the seventeenth of Drownmonth given up in writing to stop a second sale, the Exchange's published price, the one and four refused in a column beside it for want of a record, and under that the salvage man refused in the same afternoon because a claimant must be a district. Four entries in the book altogether, and the first of them is nineteen words long and says the figure is unknown, and it is still the line everybody can say without looking.

Nobody has entered anything since the sixteenth. That is not a matter of a book waiting for entries. It is a matter of a form: to enter a loss here a district has to have said what it could have done, in a form, and the nine districts in this basin have said it in nine different ways, and the ward's seventh box is a thing nobody reads out loud, and the interim figure of two and six stands for the whole season and does not change for an event.

He squares the Register against the edge of the trestle so the two books are flush, and looks at the page he has been opening every morning since the first.

*Available responses* is a heading a Deputy had in a file for a year and nobody ever wrote under it, and now it is a printed page in a book the technical cell maintains, and the printed page wants a district's answer in a form, and there is no form, so the answer to nine directions is a date and nothing under the date, and the Register is exactly as useful as the drawer the addresses came out of, which is to say it is useful to nobody today and available to anybody who comes.

**A register of what a district can do is not a list of things. It is a form, and there is no form, and the nine answers that do exist are in nine hands in nine buildings and the Register cannot see one of them.**

A boy from the press room puts his head in, about eleven, with a request for a copy of the page, because his foreman wants a copy of the page.

"There is nothing on the page," says Peascod.

"Then I will tell him that," says the boy, and goes, and does not wait to be told anything else, and Peascod goes back to the trestle, because that answer has been the answer to about four requests a week since the first of the month and it is the correct one.

The Register is maintained by the technical cell and not by this office, and it is kept on a shelf in the corridor where a person may take it down without asking, and a person may take it down and read the heading and see the date and see that there is nothing under it, and may put it back, and that is the whole of what a Register of available responses has achieved in a month in which nine districts were asked what they could do and nine of them answered on paper.

---

On the wall is the returned ledger with the column, and beneath the column, in a clerk's fair hand, a new line for the season that is the first line anybody in this basin has ever entered about a window:

*Window, ninth ward, the reach off at half past one and eleven past four, morning of the tenth of Miremonth, gang on the flat forty minutes. Basis: a claim entered by a district. No branch of this office was closed.*

It names what closed and does not name who closed it.

It is not the window the ledger paid for on Tuesday, and the clerk has had to hold the two of them side by side to get the column to take them, and there is no column in the book that will hold either of them properly, and there is no page anywhere that says how to tell a window a district kept from a window a district gave up.

**The line on the wall is the tenth of Miremonth, and the money on the trestle is the seventeenth of Drownmonth, and the two of them are a morning a ward got and a morning a ward sold, and the book has one column and puts them in it, and a man who comes in here in ten years will not be able to tell which was which.**

**And the word at the head of the column has been changed by a clerk completing a form and nobody costed it.** The returned ledger's ruled form carries two heads, CLAIM and LOSS, and the clerk, finding no claim number for a window because a window is not a claim, completes the form on the LOSS head, striking CLAIM and writing LOSS in a fair hand with the date. The column now reads LOSS — DISPLACED TURNS. Nobody costed the change. Nobody struck it out.

The clerk dates the page, initials it, and leaves the room, because there is nothing else to enter, and the ledger sits open on the trestle with the date and nothing under it and the priced schedule beside it.

---

Down the hill, in the rain, the ninth ward tower's signal glass is lit.

It is lit for the first time since the middle of the Bellweather month. The head above the ward ledger rail is lit with it. The light is a plain lamp light behind a bubbled temporary in a hand-bedded brass surround, and it carries no legible state, and it is not a restoration.

**No pane has been fitted and nothing has been repaired.**

It is lit because a ward paid the keeper's evening hours and the oil, seven and six entered and countersigned and carried down in the ordinary post, out of the payment for the window it gave up at somebody else's price. A pane nobody ever collected sits forty miles away with a piece of its crown cut out of it and in a coat.

The rain is coming down the hill in the way it has come down for nine days and there is no back-cut in it. From four streets away you can see the light in it and you cannot tell from four streets away what it is, because it carries no state, and a man who has never seen it lit before would say a lamp is up, and a man who has seen it every week of his working life would say nothing, and both of those are correct and neither of them is on a shutter.

It was dark for money yesterday. It is lit for money today.

**Nobody learns why it took so long. Nobody puts a hand in anything.**

Gant Ollis trims the lamp and writes nothing in the tower's book about it, because a lamp is not a record. He has the fifth column open in front of him and the twelve agreements in it and the two-line note to an instrument maker folded into the back, and he has not been asked for any of it and is not going to be. Marek Vale has still not been up the ladder. Sable Orin counts a shilling on the top step in the morning and does not say thank you. Tovan Iles copies minutes in the west end and is not thanked. Neris Kade stands at the school gate with her hands in her pockets and watches the light from four streets away, and does not go and say so, because the children walk up and the hours are on the shutter and that is enough for today.

The tower burns in the rain with its one colour.
