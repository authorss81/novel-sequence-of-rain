# Chapter 61 — Six Catch Pits

The line came into the ninth ward on the Saturday morning of the fifth day of Drownmonth, on a piece of paper that was not nailed to anything.

There is a chandler on the corner of Sump Lane who sells coal oil, lamp glass, tar, and a weekly run of twine, and who has kept a copy of the Cloud Exchange's district sheet pinned behind his counter since the weather turned, because his customers ask him what the river is going to do and he does not have an instrument. He does not read it. He unpins the old one, pins the new one, and the old one goes in the drawer, and there are about sixty of them in the drawer and he has never once looked at the drawer.

Neris Kade came in for a quarter of a hundredweight of coal oil at about ten on the Saturday morning and read the sheet, because it is on the level of the counter and she has read about forty of them, and it is item six on a two-column printed page and it is the only item on the page that is a price with a date on it.

*BELLWEATHER REACH, low water, morning of the 17th. 7/6, refund 3/9. One sold. Sales closed the 5th at four o'clock. No further entry this window.*

The rest of the sheet was about the Office's band and a level in inches and a line of corrections and the ordinary commercial furniture of a market that is not a liar.

She read the line three times standing at the counter with a funnel in her hand.

*One sold. Sales closed the 5th at four o'clock. No further entry this window.*

Which is not a refusal. The Exchange has never refused the ninth ward anything. It is an ordinary commercial event that happens about nine days a year and that has no name and no column and no place in any published table: the sales for that window closed at four o'clock on the Saturday, and this ward had not bought one, and a thing that has not been bought before its sales close does not exist afterwards.

**And the reach was coming down anyway, because weather does not consult a price list.**

---

The Sunday of the sixth was a clearing day.

It had rained through the Saturday night and hard in the first two hours of the Sunday morning and then it had stopped dead, and the stop was the sort that gets into a ward's bones, because in this basin the stop is when the water starts going somewhere it is not supposed to go. The upper reservoir had dropped seven inches in eight hours. The draw came round southerly and laid a long band over the whole basin and held it, and a long band is a fixed pressure gradient with no bend in it, and it was going to hold for about a week if it was that sort of band, and every person who had lived in Low Spindle for more than a year knew what a week of that sort was worth.

There were two wardens and a barrow and six catch pits, and the water steward, and about four hours.

Neris Kade had been doing this for six years and had a hand up the low lane with a boot scraper on it and no pride in the business. **A district's only asset in a reach is the amount of rubbish a district is willing to take out of a grate by hand for nothing**, and the ninth ward's was four hours and two women and a barrow, and the fourth's was a contractor's gang on a standing contract and it was twice our size, and the third's was a permanent works crew, and the difference between those three numbers is the difference between the ninth ward and the rest of the basin and it is not anybody's fault.

The third block's low side ran for about an inch and went out again by the middle of the afternoon. No ground floor was wet anywhere. The second grate at the Sump Lane corner was full to the bar and she got it out with a hooked pole and a bad back, and the fourth was clear.

There is a column in Sergeant Ampow's wet notebook that says, in his own hand, *weather, and what it did*. He has kept that notebook for twenty-two years and it is the only document in the ninth ward that anybody has ever asked to see, and it has been asked for twice, both times by the water office, both times about a different matter.

Ampow wrote one line on the Sunday, which was a thing he did on roughly one Sunday in three.

*6th, clear by eight, wind southerly light, band all day. Cleared 4 hrs. 2nd grate full, 4th clear. Nothing over the step anywhere in the ward. Nor.*

That last word is his and it has meant one thing in twenty-two years, which is *and nothing else happened*.

**There is no column anywhere in the world for a good Sunday.**

---

She did the free thing on the Sunday afternoon, and it is the whole of the argument in one afternoon's work, and it cost the ninth ward nothing and was credited to the ninth ward by nobody.

The ward's clearing rota, its shelter entry and its pump order had lived, for as long as anybody could remember, in three places: the water steward's figures, a card, and a drawer.

The figures were Marda Loon's and they were the best-kept record in the ward and they were on page four of a return that the Exchange bought for eleven shillings. The card was the warden's council's own and it was a card. The drawer had a sheet in it that nobody had opened in about a year and that was, in every respect anybody could prove, a **record of what the water steward had done in the season of Sleetmonth and of how the low side had behaved in it**, in a fair hand, with dates, in about two hundred words.

There was no form in the ward for it. There was no form in the Council for it either. Nobody had ever asked for it.

She got a sheet of the Council's ordinary return stock out of the district office press, which is the same stock as the spring form and the quarterly return and has the district's own reference line printed at the top of it, and she wrote on it in her own hand, in one pen, without lifting it, over about forty minutes on the Sunday afternoon, in a room over a locker's yard with a stove in it:

*Ninth Ward — clearing rota, shelter entry and pump order. Dated this day. Season of Drownmonth.*

*Clearing rota. Second block grate, Tuesday and Friday, from eight. Third block low side, the four grates, on any day after two days of rain, from seven, and the third block before the fourth. Low lane channel, cut on the district's own call, twice in the wet.*

*Shelter entry. Low-ground shelter rota maintained by the district; occupants as the district's own list; no public schedule is maintained.*

*Pump order. Pump house two, by hand, at any marked depth at the corner of the fourth block. Pump house one, on a call, and the call is the warden's, and there is no one else who may make it.*

*Crews. Six with the water steward. Nine can be got to a flat by nine in the morning. Not nine by six.*

*Sent to the warden's council. Sent to nobody else. This is not a public notice and nobody may read it out as one.*

---

The difference between that sheet and the sheet she cannot write took her the whole afternoon and about four seconds of the whole afternoon.

The one she has just written is a **clearing** rota. It says what a district will do with water that is on the ground.

The one she has never written, in about four years, and has a card for, and will not write, is a **shelter** rota. Nine names on a card in a drawer. And a card of nine names is a list of people she is fond of, and a sheet is a document that says she is answerable for them, and she has had four years and has not managed it once, and she is not going to manage it this year, and the only reason that is a fact worth a paragraph is that she wrote the other one on the Sunday, correctly, in one pen, and then went and stood in front of the drawer with the other one in it, and shut it.

**A district has now produced, for the first time in two years, a document about its own water that nobody asked for, and it is about the rain, and it is not about the people.**

---

**Hesk's letter came on the Sunday morning at about half past ten, on the district's own delivery, and the man who brought it up the outside stair put it on the counter and did not come further in and did not ask for a signature, because a letter is not a rate and a rate is the only thing a district ever signs for.**

Neris Kade read it standing up.

She read it twice, and then she sat down on the edge of the desk in a cold room and read it a third time, and then she wrote on the back of the district's own delivery docket, in her own hand, the date and the sender, and put it in the drawer with the pump orders, and then she took it out again and put it in the other drawer, and then she left both drawers shut and stood at the window for a while.

It was eleven pence of ordinary post and four paragraphs and it was the first thing that anybody had ever sent this district that asked it for something it could refuse.

**The ninth ward has been asked for its rain by a firm, in a printed form, at a counter, and told the price, and that was in April. It has been offered a form for its own water and been handed one. It has been sent a public sheet twice a day for six weeks, free, and had the reason for the free explained to it by a tobacconist. It has been bound by nothing and has been asked for nothing that it could give, and the only thing anybody has sent it that was not a bill, a form or a price is a letter from a man which says, on its first page, that the ward may refuse.**

She did not answer it on the Sunday.

She went down to the south bank instead, at about two in the afternoon, because there was a thing she had to do there and she had been putting it off for four days, and because the reach was coming down and would be coming down again in a fortnight, and because the thing she had to do there was the only piece of work in this basin that cost nothing at all and that she had standing to do.

---

The south-bank shed is a hundred yards from the freight apron and about a mile and a half from the low ground, and it is a corrugated shed with a roller door and a chain on the door, and it has been Petrie's for about thirty years.

**Nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands. A four-hundred-and-ten-pound dewatering pump on a pallet with a Union guarantee on the drum and a cover that has been on since the eleventh of the Bellweather month. Nine crew on the district's books since the sixth of the Bellweather month. The water office's order 114. A painted board on the door with no name on it and the sixth of the Bellweather month on it, and a front in two years and about eleven months, which is a Friday, and which is a table on a wall at the Weatherglass and not a threat.**

The load has been there since the sixth of the Bellweather month and it was paid for, and the pump is on a guarantee, and nobody has cancelled the repair and nobody has stood the crew down and no decision has been made not to repair it.

**The load has now been there once already through a reach morning, and the reach did not come down, and about nine hundred pounds of good timber stayed where it was, and the wall at the Lower Sill went into its second consecutive season of not being worked on, and the crew went home and were paid for a fortnight.**

"Petrie."

He was about sixty-eight and he was at a bench with a plane in his hand and he had been in this shed about thirty years, and he put the plane down, which he did not do for people.

"Warden."

"I want you to have the flatbed's ground inspected."

He looked at her. He had a rule, and the rule was that a shed keeps a load and a load is not a document and a document is not a shed, and he had applied it four times in a week to a water-office clerk, a district crew, a man in a good coat and a man with a camera, and it had been correct four times.

"Inspected," he said.

"Before Thursday. Not Thursday morning. Before Thursday."

"I'll have Farrow's boy do it with a chain and a staff off the mark and I'll have the reading put in the book in the right column, which is the column it goes in and not one of his own." He waited. "And that's all that's in it?"

"That's all that's in it."

"Then I'll have it done," said Petrie, "and I'll have it done the same as I'd have done it if you'd not come, and I'll tell you the same thing I'd tell anybody, which is that there's a mark cut round that post at knee height and the mud's over it and the mud's not going anywhere while there's a band on it. And I'll write the reading in when there's a reading to write."

"Right."

"Which is Thursday week," he said. "If it isn't, the reading's on the Friday."

"Which is Thursday week," said Neris Kade.

And then she said the words, which were not for him and were about the weather and were the only sentences anybody in the basin can say out loud in a shed and mean:

"**If the mark comes off the post we do not go.**"

Petrie looked at her for about four seconds.

He did not ask why. There were perhaps nine reasons available to a man of sixty-eight in a shed in about four seconds and every one of them was a reason to leave it alone, and one of them was that he had been a shed foreman since before the wards had these walls and had spent a career being told by men in coats what a thing was worth, and had watched about forty of them say *it was just the water* and had believed every one of them at the time and had been wrong roughly half the time, and had worked out over about thirty years that the safest thing a shed foreman can do with a sentence he does not understand is not to ask what it means.

**He did not ask why, and she did not say, and he went back to his plane.**

And what he did, afterwards, alone, in a shed, is worth setting down because it costs nobody anything at all: he went to the shelf, and took down the level book, and opened it at the right week, and read his own column.

It is a four-column book and it has been kept for about thirty years. The hour, the level at the post, the state of the crew, and **what the weather was doing**, in his own words, badly.

On the line for the twenty-third of Sleetmonth, in the fourth column, there are two words. *Reach came back.*

It is not a reason. It is a fact and a fact is not a reason, and the column that would have taken a reason has been empty for about thirty years, and nobody has ever filled it, and there is no form in the world in which anybody could be made to.

He looked at it for a while. Then he shut the book and put it back on the shelf, and picked the plane up, and did not write anything down, because he had not got anything to write, and because a man who has nothing to write and writes something has got himself a document, and he had been keeping a shed for thirty years and he was not going to start keeping anything else because a woman from a district office had said three words in his doorway.

**Since the thirtieth of the Bellweather month something has happened on that morning that has been in this building's records and out of them, and the only man in this basin who has never once asked anybody about it is standing at a bench in a shed with a plane in his hand, and in eight days somebody from a district office is going to make him ask.**
