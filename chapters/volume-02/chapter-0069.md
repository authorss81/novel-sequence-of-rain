# Chapter 69 — What Is Written In A Hand

The Council's letter came to the Hearthward district office on the Friday of the eleventh of Drownmonth, four paragraphs and a postscript, and the postscript was the whole of it: *A district may refuse. A refusal will be minuted in the register as a refusal, with its date and its words.*

Kell Bray read it twice, and then she did the thing she has done for twenty years, which is to take a book down off a shelf and make a list.

She is thirty-one and she has been the Hearthward assembly's clerk since she was eleven, which is not a figure anybody in Hearthward finds remarkable, because there are four hundred and forty people in the lowest four blocks of the ninth ward and there are rather more than that in Hearthward, and a ward that has kept a clerk for twenty years is a ward whose business has been on paper long enough to need one.

A request had come in that asked a ward what it could do about its water.

She wrote four lines on the cover of the incoming book.

*Hearthward. Rain-garden crew: four men and a foreman. Low-side channels and the two public gardens and about eleven hundred yards of the low lane. Two hours, Tuesday and Thursday, wet season, standing order nine years, and it was the water board's before that.*
*Water steward, since three years and a coming spring. She tells the foreman when to come.*
*Upper, middle and lowest row. The lowest row is the one that floods. We have four catch pits at the foot of it under iron grates the size of a cartwheel, and the outfall runs under the market and comes out at a stone throat at the foot of the row, and there is no second outfall and there has not been one since the terrace was built.*
*Six ground-floor rooms on the lowest row sit below the surcharge line. That is a fact about our ground and not a forecast and it is the reason we clear on a Tuesday.*

That is the Hearthward answer and it is a good one and it went out on the Monday of the fourteenth in two pages, in her hand, and Anwen Sarr read it and made one correction to a comma and signed for it as chair.

**Hearthward had answered in a day and a half and had lost nothing and had been asked for nothing, and it had a column, and it was the only one of the three districts that was ever going to be comfortable.**

---

The first of the three answers to arrive at the technical cell was not from the fourth and it was not from the ninth, and it came on the Sunday morning of the thirteenth in a hand that had been taught to make a rope's turn by somebody older.

The sixth ward is on the water and has been for two hundred years and its district clerk's office is a room over a chandler's with a stove in it and about nine feet of shelf. What came back was four lines on the back of a Council form, in pencil, in a hand that had been taught to make a rope's turn by somebody older:

*The sixth ward holds four flat-bottomed lighters and a chain barge and a lighterman and two men.*
*We can have 40 tons of stone on the low side of the sixth ward's channel in about six hours at a spring tide, and we can do it twice a day, and we can do it in three days' bad weather at half.*
*The chain barge needs nine feet and she draws six and there is a bar at the Nacre end that takes her at eight feet and a half and I would not.*
*This is a lighter's answer. I have no idea what your form wants. — Warrick, lighter, sixth ward.*

It is not a schedule of available responses to a season's water. It is a schedule of available responses to a *tide*, in the only units a lighterman has, and it is the most complete, most honest, most useful set of figures anybody had sent that office in about eleven years, and it arrived on a Sunday in pencil on the back of a form, and **it is the reason nobody in the basin can price a reach**: because the thing that actually moves forty tons of stone is a tide, and a tide is an ebb, and an ebb is a sequence fact, and there is no instrument in this city that will name one for money, and the only party that has ever claimed to is a firm that sells windows.

The fourth ward answered on the Wednesday, in one line, and it is the correctest answer anybody gave:

*This ward has no return of this kind and has not kept one and declines to invent one. — for the chair, fourth ward.*

**Which Sabin Orle minuted in the register as a refusal, with its date and its words, because she had said on the face of her letter that she would, and a word given on the face of a letter is a debt and this Council has never in two hundred and forty years broken one it gave itself.**

---

**And then Kell Bray went to Ferrygate, on the Thursday of the seventeenth, because the third answer had not come and the third answer was the one that mattered, and because Nell Cray had sent her four lines on the Monday by the cart that goes up the low lane and had written at the bottom of them, in a good round hand, *You will want to see the book, and it is the big one and it is behind the counter, and mind the step.***

It is about four miles. She went in a cart with a man called Ossick who has driven the low lane for twenty-six years and who said, without being asked, that the crossing keeper had been in on the Thursday and the lights were on the water at eight o'clock, which is a thing nobody asked him for.

Ferrygate is a market district on the northern water with a ferry crossing and the Nacre Gate above it, and it has a rain-garden rota with six names on it and a gang of generally five and about two hours on a Tuesday and a Thursday in the wet season, and it is a town that has been running the same three documents for six years and has never once put any of them in a weather notice.

**Nell Cray is the clerk of the Ferrygate district assembly and has been for nineteen years and her stated ambition, which she says out loud and often, is to get through a week in which nothing interesting happens.**

She met Bray at the door with the book already off the shelf.

She had filled in the Council's form. It was two pages and it was not pencil.

"A rota for the low-side channels, the two public gardens, and the low lane. A column of days. Two hours, Tuesday and Thursday, wet. Six names and a gang of five. It is not a forecast, it makes no claim, no bell rings for it, and it has never been in a weather notice, and I do not put it in one." She turned the page. "The ferry fallback, which is the crossing keeper's extended night hours on the board at the landing in case the lane goes. And the low-ground shelter entry, which is two lines and has been in this book since the winter before last and I move it on myself, in boots, in December, and in January if it was hard, and twice in the wet."

"Six years?"

"Six. Nobody has ever asked for it and it is not in a notice and I have never sent it to anybody, and I will tell you the whole of it in one sentence because you have come four miles." Cray shut the book. "**I sent it once. To the Council. Six years ago, on a spring form, in the ordinary course, because their box asked. And their man stamped it, and entered it, and filed it, and I have never had it back and it is not in any book of theirs that I have ever seen, and it is in one somewhere, because a thing that is stamped is not a thing that is lost.**"

Kell Bray opened the book again.

"Where does it say it went."

"In their own hand, in the margin, in ink." Cray found it. "*Local record. Not a return under the Standing Response. Entered against the district file.* And it is correct. It is exactly correct. It is the most correct thing anybody has ever done to one of my documents, and he was right, and it was not his book."

---

Here is the thing that Kell Bray has never in twenty years written down, and it came to her standing at a counter in Ferrygate at about eleven in the morning of the seventeenth of Drownmonth with a book open in front of her, and it is the reason nobody is going to find out.

**The same sentence is true of three districts and of one firm.**

The ninth ward answered a form with one box on it and the answer came out as eighteen pages and the form is a press downstairs and the thing in the press is not wrong. The sixth ward answered a form with four lines of pencil on the back and there is nothing on the form to put a pencil line on. The fourth ward has nothing and says so and is right. **Ferrygate has had a schedule of its available responses to a season's water in its own book, in its own hand, in a proper column, for six years, and it is the only one of the nine wards in this basin that has ever written one, and it sent it once, to a book, and a competent man entered it correctly against the wrong heading, and neither of them ever knew, and the job — the sending of it — stopped, and nobody wrote down that it had stopped.**

And the Cloud Exchange, four miles away in the west end of the city, has never asked a district anything in nineteen years, and a boy of nineteen brought a form back out of a cellar with nothing on it, and a partner wrote a letter on the back of a counter desk because he had run out of documents at four o'clock.

**It is a job that stopped, and it is a job that stopped in four places at once, and every one of the four was a person doing their part correctly, and every one of the four was paid, and not one of the four was asked whether the job was still wanted.**

Kell Bray has been in a ledger for twenty years. She has watched three of them stop. She has written the minutes of two hundred and eleven council meetings and she has signed off a thousand returns and she has never, in twenty years, written one line about a *ceasing*.

She wrote it down that afternoon. It is four words on the back of a Council form.

*Nobody records the stopping.*

---

**And then she did the thing, and it is the only remarkable act of her life and she did it on the Thursday morning in a week in which she also had a council meeting and an audit.**

The ninth ward did not answer Orle's letter. It is the busiest ward in this basin and its warden had spent a reach in a room over a locker's yard four days earlier and had not written a line since and its chair was fifty-eight and had said in a room that she would support whatever came to the table and that nobody was going to tell her anything.

So Bray wrote the ninth ward's line herself, on the Council's own form, in a fair hand, with the date on it, and she did not pretend to be anybody.

*The crews the ninth ward can hold, and what it can be relied upon to have and to be able to do, in a season, dated. Six catch pits, a barrow, two wardens, a water steward. A crew of nine can be got to a flat by nine in the morning and not by six. A pump house with a hand on it and a second on a call, and the call is the warden's, and there is nobody else who may make it. Clearing rota on the district's own call, the second block grate before the third, the third block before the fourth, twice in the wet.*

*And it is to be recorded on the same line, because the line is the whole of the book and the book is no use with half of it: the ninth ward's reach. The reach comes down about nine days a year and it was closed twice in one month by the district on purpose, in writing, and the second of those was the largest of the season, and the crew of eleven is stood down and the wall is in its second season, and the water office's table says two years and about nine months.*

*Nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands and a four hundred and ten pound pump are in a shed on the south bank under a painted board with no name on it. They have been there since the sixth of the Bellweather month. The load is paid for. The order is the water office's one hundred and fourteen.*

*Signed: N. Kade, Storm Warden, ninth ward. — S. Trask, chair, ninth ward assembly.*
*Written in at the request of the ward by K. Bray, Hearthward assembly clerk, who is not an officer of this Council and has no office in it and was not asked.*

She read it back to both of them in a room over a locker's yard and neither of them asked a single question about it, and that is not because they are careless. It is because a warden who has closed a reach in writing, twice, in public, with a bell on a wall, knows exactly what a line in a book is for, and a chair of twenty-two years who has taken two votes in a month on things nobody has ever voted on knows it too.

Neris Kade said: "That is the first thing anybody has written down about this ward in four years that is not a bill."

"It is a line in a column," said Kell Bray. "That is all it is. Anybody can fill in a column. **That is the whole of what a column is, and the only time anybody in this basin will let you write one is before you need it.**"

"You cannot be compensated for a thing you have not got written down," said Dessie Trask. "I have said that in a room and been laughed at."

"You cannot be compensated for a thing you have not got written down, and you cannot get it written down afterwards," said Kell Bray, "and the only time anybody will let you write it down is before you need it. And I have been in a ledger twenty years and I have watched three of them stop and I have never written down one, and I have written four words on the back of a form and I am going to have them written into the minute of this council so that there is a piece of paper somewhere in this basin with the word *stopping* on it and my name against it."

---

**She carried it herself, on the Friday morning of the eighteenth, to the ground floor of the Council's basin house, because the letter went to the Council and to nobody else, and because a thing carried by hand is a thing that can be given to a person rather than left on a table.**

There was nobody in the corridor. The press was on the end wall with two doors of deal and a lock with a hasp, and the book was on the shelf under the bottom door with the current season's working.

Mr. Skell was in the room. He was the records officer who had told a boy of nineteen about the four-and-fiftieth in a cellar a fortnight ago, and he looked up, and he did not ask her who she was, because there is a rule about a member of the public bringing a form into a records room, which is that a member of the public may bring a form into a records room.

He read the first line.

"This is a form we did not send," he said.

"Yes."

"It's the Standing Response. Season of Drownmonth. Ninth ward." He turned it round and round. "There's nothing wrong with it."

"That's what I was told in a cellar a fortnight ago by you."

"I said there was nothing wrong with the forms."

"You did," said Kell Bray. "I have written down in a minute of my own council tonight that you said it, and you may check it, and I should be obliged if you would."

He looked at the signatures at the bottom for some time.

"There's no district clerk's stamp on it," he said. "That's not a requirement. There's no requirement."

"I know there isn't."

"There's no form number and there's no date of receipt, because it has not been received." He got a pen and then he put the pen down and picked it up again. "It's a good one, though. It's a good one. There's a crew of nine to a flat by nine and not by six, and there's a pump house on a call, and there's nine hundred pound of timber in a shed with a painted board on it, and the district's told the Council about the board on the board, on its own line, which nobody has ever—" He stopped. "Who taught you the shape of it."

"Nobody," said Kell Bray. "I have been in a ledger for twenty years, Mr. Skell, and there is one line on the earth and I have wanted to see it filled in for about nineteen of them."

He entered it. It took him about four minutes. He entered it against the ninth ward's column, for the season of Drownmonth, in the register, in the book, in the press, on the ground floor, in the hand of a records officer of the technical cell; he wrote the date against it; and then he shut the book and put it back on the shelf under the bottom door with the current season's working, where it is now.

**On the flyleaf over the section, three inches above the season of three hundred and fifty-five, in the same fair hand and the same ink, there were nineteen words that an assessor had written on the Thursday of the tenth of Drownmonth about the whole of it, and a copperplate hand in a good ink said *issued to the ninth ward's district office. J. Marchant to follow*, and J. Marchant was not in the duty book after the season of three hundred and fifty-five, and the second-writings box under the press was empty, and it had a card on it, and there was no rule requiring anybody to report an empty box to the Board.**

Nobody in that building had asked her to do it. Nobody had known it was going to be done. Nobody could have stopped it, and nobody in the Council of this city has any power whatever to stop a clerk in another ward from walking a form into a records room and asking a man in a press to enter it.

And there was a shop at Ferrygate with a counter in it, and against the wall of the district office next door there was a boat hook, put up in the autumn against the plaster by a man who crosses water for a living, and nobody had ever asked for it, and it was the only object in the four miles between that counter and that press that no person in this basin could price and everybody could see.
