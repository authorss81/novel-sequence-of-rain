# Chapter 95 — Dark for Money

The ninth ward tower's signal glass is dark on the eighteenth of Miremonth, and the head above the ward ledger rail in the Weatherglass is dark with it, and the reason is the district's glasswork line and not a hand.

**The glass has not lit since the middle of the Bellweather month. The bubbled temporary in the frame has been in it since the autumn before last. Two objects with two histories, never merged, never swapped, and no one in this basin counts the dark in months, because nobody in this basin can say what the count would be counting.**

Gant Ollis sits in the tower with his book open at the fifth column — *Exchange sheet, four times a day, compared with this column* — and twelve agreements in it, and a two-line note to an instrument maker a hundred and forty miles down the hill road with the answer *it is the chain* folded into the back.

The lamp is trimmed, which is not the same as lit, and the oil is short, which is a sentence about a can and not about a night, and both of those facts are written nowhere, because a keeper writes the water and the wind and the hours in his book and does not write what he has or has not got.

He has never looked at the substrate of the plate in the seat and does not look at it. He showed the run sheets and the fifth column to a counter-reader and did not show her the holder, and she did not ask, and neither of them went near the plate. The spare is still boxed on the shelf under the seat, dated *spare*, not fitted, because a spare plate that is fitted has been spent.

**He has never once been asked for anything out of that chair in eleven years, and if it was a record he would have to have somebody come and take it out one day, and then they would be here every season, and then he would be a public officer instead of a man in a tower.**

The book has five columns and eleven years in it and four of the columns are his own invention, which is the most anybody has ever said about them. One is the water, one is the wind, one is the light, and the fifth is the Office's sheet compared four times a day against what the Office's sheet says, written to the penny of the difference, and it has been written four times a day for eleven years by a man who cannot be asked for it, and it has been right about forty times and it is not in anybody's hands but his, and he has shown it to a counter-reader once and to nobody else, and the showing was a mistake he has thought about every day since.

He writes the keeper's evening hours in the hours column when they are paid and leaves them out when they are not, which is a practice and not a policy, and the column is a column he invented, and nobody has ever asked him which of his columns is which.

About ten the district's own man comes up the ladder with a tin in both hands, which is the way anybody in this basin carries money up a ladder, and puts it on the sill.

"Keeper's hours for the week," he says. "And the oil. There's a shilling short on the oil and the warden said to say she'd send it."

"Say thank you," says Gant Ollis.

"There's nothing to say thank you for yet," the man says. "The money's the money."

"Then don't say thank you," says Gant Ollis, "and don't write anything on the sill either, and go down before you're under this ladder on the way, because I've watched two men do it."

He puts the lamp out at the half hour, the way he has put it out at the half hour every evening since the middle of the Bellweather month, and stands in the dark tower in the rain and does the thing that has no record in it at all, which is to stand where the light would be for the length of a smoke.

**He did not darken the frame and there is no hand in it anywhere. The paid-for pane sits uncollected in a shop forty miles away with a piece of its crown cut out of it and in a coat, and nobody knows. He still has not been up the ninth ward ladder, and neither has the man who came up it with a tin, and the two of those facts are in the same tower and neither of them is in a book.**

Down the hill the district tin is short. The ledger has paid the ward seven and six for the window it gave up, at the Exchange's price, with the ward's one and four refused beside it, and the money has not been released, because a ledger is not a fund and a payment has to be entered and countersigned and carried down in the ordinary post, and the ordinary post takes days.

Neris counts the tin twice on the table in the district office with the district clerk standing at the other side of it not helping, because a man standing on the other side of a table helps. The shelter fund is in a different tin and the different tin has more in it than this one, and that is correct, because the shelter fund has been in for four years and this one is a week old.

The district book has no column for a shilling a week and no column for seven and six, and she writes nothing in it, because there is nowhere to write it. She writes the pump hours and the gang on Tuesday, in the columns that have been there since her father's time, and the gang is six and a steward, and Beddoe's book at the pump house has the tenth of the month in it in his own hand.

"There is a form for a claim," says the district clerk. "There's a form for the window. You put a schedule on it."

"That is the ward's claim," says Neris Kade, "and it is in the seventh box, and the seventh box does not go in the district book, because the district book is a book about the district and the seventh box is a box about the claim, and I have watched two clerks in a year put one into the other because it was easier, and both of them were dismissed, and one of them was not dismissed for that."

"Yes, ma'am," says the district clerk, and stops.

She counts the tin a third time and gets the same number, and puts the ledger receipt in the drawer with the shilling receipts, and the drawer shuts.

---

Marek works the bench in the outer room with the lamp Ilse Doff moved onto it in the autumn, and gets on with two commissions he has had since the middle of the month, neither of them for the Office, one of them for a miller at Ferrygate who wants a level read off a wall he has had repainted.

The shop's day book holds Circular 15 entire, copied on the Sunday after it was pinned, the twenty-seventh, with the seal and the date, under glass with the lehr log. The bench sheet of the fifteenth, the maintenance report of four hundred and eleven words, the two unfiled tags — all in the drawers, none of them touched, none of them fileable.

**The three uncompellable books — the shed foreman's level book, the tower keeper's tower book, the maintenance foreman's day book — have never been asked for, and nobody asks for any of them today.**

Tovan locks the press at five, counts the trays, puts the key in her pocket with the other key, and walks home past the dark tower in the rain, and the tower is dark the way a thing is dark when it is dark, and not the way a thing is dark when a person has done something to it. Her box holds three things: the thirtieth's day sheet before the classification, the ninth ward's claim with the seventh box at 10:09, the sixth ward's claim with the seventh box in pencil. Nobody knows how many are in there, including her. The first person in the building to ask her a question about her work was Kell Bray, and it was about minutes procedure, and the answer was one and it did not help, and nobody thanked her for it.

She stops under the tower on the way home, in the rain, for about the length of a cigarette she does not smoke, and looks up at a thing that is not lit and has not been for weeks, and there is a ladder up the inside of it that she has been up eleven times in two months and that nobody in this basin has ever asked her about either, and she looks at the outside of it from the ground, which is a thing a person does once, and then goes home the long way because the short way is past it.

Neris sits at her desk with the seven-line page locked in the drawer and the shelter card with nine names under it, unwritten, unmanageable, and closes the drawer on both, because managing it would mean writing it down, and writing it down would mean being asked about it.

She has one of them open on her knee for a minute before she puts it back, because nine names is a rota she could do in her head on a Tuesday and could not do in her head on the night a rain comes, and a rota is a list of who is standing in a doorway for four hours, and the ninth ward has nine people in it who can stand in a doorway, and a form would say what to do with the tenth person, and there is no tenth person and the form does not know that, and so it stays in a drawer.

The tower stays dark. It is dark for money, and the money is coming, and it is not here yet, and the ordinary post takes days.
