# Chapter 94 — Partnership

Kestrel Yards on the seventeenth of Miremonth smells of tar and wet rope, and the boards are up with all five conditions on them in chalk, and the receipt for two shillings under them with the hour and the date on it, and the whole morning shift is in earshot, because a yard is not a room and there is no door to close.

The launch on the slip is the Kestrel Lady and she came up at ten with a ding in her bottom plank and four men on the windlass and Ilva Sorn calling the hour off the crane post, and nobody stopped when the two of them started talking, because the haul has to go on either way and the crane does not care.

The cradle is under her, greased, and the chock is in and the shore is on, and the man on the windlass is counting in sevens because he was taught in sevens, and the count is wrong by one every six pulls and has been wrong by one every six pulls for nineteen years, and Ilva Sorn writes the count in the crane log as the man gives it, and the crane log is a record and the record is wrong every six pulls and the man is not going to be told.

Tarin Senn and Marek Vale stand in front of the boards with the disagreement between them, and neither of them lowers her voice for it.

The disagreement is about the shilling. Not the two shillings — those are settled, invoiced on the Friday the twenty-fifth, posted on the Saturday the twenty-sixth, receipted on the Wednesday the thirtieth, chalked and dated and done. The disagreement is about the shilling a week the firm pays the ninth ward for the right to print its own reach hours, and about the threepence a week on the firm's list for the shutter with both readers' names on it, which is thirteen shillings a year and one pound six for two years.

"You sold them the hours," says Marek. "Not you. The ward sold them. Ninety seconds, no vote, no council, and nobody asked either named reader whether the reading might be printed. The board carries my name and hers and the words this board is not a notice, and the ward is not a party to the arrangement at all."

"I know," says Tarin. "I have got opinions about that and this board is not the place for them, and that is not the disagreement."

"What is the disagreement?"

"The disagreement is that you want the yard to refuse the firm's money on the ward's behalf, and I will not." She puts her hand flat on the board, the way she puts it on a hull she is arguing about, in front of everybody. "Two shillings for the use of published figures, at a rate the yard named itself, with the board extract and the hour and the date and a time sheet enclosed. A party may name its own figure. The condition is about the yard's name and not about payment. Both of those are on this board because they are the same board."

"It is the same money," says Marek. "A district is asked for a form with one box on it and answers it in a fair hand and eleven shillings buys the answer twice. The exchange of a good reading for a good price is the easiest transaction in the basin."

"It is," says Tarin Senn. "And a yard which sells dependability sells its crew the right to be unlucky, and I refused a contract that would have fixed a boom cable and a wrist and two carriers' discount, and paid four hundred and six pounds for that, and took the firm's instrument anyway and installed it on my own slip. I know what money does. That is not the disagreement either."

A carrier comes up the slip road with a handcart and stops at the boards, because boards are what a yard has, and reads the five conditions, and looks for a name, and finds the line that says this board is not a notice.

"Which one of you is the firm, then," he says.

"Neither," says Tarin Senn. "The firm is a building up the hill. That is the yard's name and the yard's conditions and the yard's receipt, and a man is not obliged to believe any of it and is not going to be told otherwise at this gate."

The carrier looks at the receipt with the hour and the date on it, and at the tide clock chalked on the end post, and decides that a thing with a time on it is worth more than a thing with a name on it, which is the whole commercial argument of this basin in one man at a slip road, and takes his cart through.

"He will be back on Thursday with a firm invoice and no tide in it," says Tarin, "and we will take it, because he has a load to move. Write that down somewhere, Marek, if you are writing things down."

"I have written it down," says Marek Vale. "It is the third time somebody has said it to me this month and it is the only one that has come with a name attached."

**A yard board is the only public document in this basin that a working person can read standing up, and a condition on it is enforced by a gate, and a gate is the cheapest kind of court there is.**

Marek turns back to the board.

"Read me the fifth one again."

"You have read it four times." Tarin does not look at him. "Fifth: two shillings received, the condition is about the yard's name and not about payment, both of those on this board because they are the same board. It is one line, it is dated, and the yard's clerk of eleven years squared it under the others, and neither of us has any business being clever about a line that is already true."

"And the district's shilling?"

"The district's shilling is the district's business. A shilling a week for the right to print its own reach hours, with the ward's name on it, said yes in ninety seconds with no vote, and nobody in the room asked either named reader whether the reading might be printed. I have got opinions about that and this board is not the place for them, and I have now said them twice in one morning, which is one more than this board is for."

Nora Bragg, the yard's clerk of eleven years, comes out of the office with the slate under her arm and does not interrupt, and puts the time sheet in a groove cut in the board's foot, where it has gone every morning for eleven years, and goes back in.

**She did not thank the firm either, and neither of them did, and the yard has never invoiced anybody in its life until the twenty-fifth of Drownmonth, and a yard that says thank you on a firm's floor has told the firm that the yard has noticed it needs to.**

She looks at him, and at the board, and at the yard, and says the thing she has not said to him, once, late, in a yard, describing an arrangement between two working people:

"If we keep doing this — the yard bearing its errors in public, a reader putting both readings up before the claim with both names on it — then it is a partnership. Yours and mine. Not the firm's. Not the Office's. A yard and a reader, publishing before anybody asks."

**The word has not been said in this basin. It is said once, in a yard, and eleven people hear it, and not one of them writes it down.**

**He does not answer it. He stands there with his hands in his pockets and about one pound twelve in his coat and a left ear that is quieter than it was, and looks at the board with the five conditions and the receipt, and does not answer, and the yard works around them.**

Ilva Sorn comes past with the crane log under her arm and says the launch's hour out loud to nobody in particular, because she says launch hours out loud, and it goes into the shift the way all his repeated questions have not gone into anything.

Nora Bragg squares the time sheet under the board and says nothing. Ilva Sorn signs a proviso on the slate about the yard bearing its own errors in public. The morning shift goes back to work.

The proviso is the whole of what the yard has said publicly about itself this morning, and it is in a slate hand because a slate is washed, and what it says is that the yard will publish its own count wrong every six pulls rather than have the count altered by somebody who is good at figures, and a man on the windlass has read it over Ilva Sorn's shoulder and says nothing at all, and that is the correct response and he knows it.

They stand there for a while afterwards, either side of the board, not touching it, with everything said that can be said in a yard, and the rain coming down, and the disagreement standing, and both readings standing, and neither withdrawn.

The Kestrel Lady is chocked and shored by one o'clock, and a boy is on a cart at the N end of the apron at twenty past one, and the water is four feet and falling, and somewhere above the shed roof the clock on the crane post is being called by a woman who has called it every working day for nineteen years, and the two of them are still there when she calls it, one on each side of a board with five conditions and a receipt on it, and neither of them has said the word again, and neither of them is going to say it before the week is out, and the week is not going to ask.
