# Chapter 68 — The Second Back-Cut

The photograph went up on the forward board at about ten past ten on the Tuesday morning of the fifteenth day of Drownmonth, and it was a good photograph, and it was taken from a public apron wall a hundred and forty yards from a post, and there is not one thing wrong with it.

It is the bottom of the Bellweather reach with the water off it, on a clear morning, with the gauge in the wall at the left of frame and a barge tied up at the apron, and a man standing in the edge of it with his arm out to give the scale, and the low mud of the flat showing about a foot of the stonework. The mark post is in it at the right, small and a bit soft. And under the photograph, in a fair hand, on the printed list:

*BELLWEATHER REACH, low water, morning of the 17th. 7/6. Refund 3/9.*
*Sales closed 5th, 16:00. Window sold twice on the 4th.*
*Sales on this window: 4th, 11:40 — salvage interest, released 12th, 07:20, advance refunded.*
*Sales on this window: 4th, 11:52 — a firm, its own onward sale, disclosed.*
*Sales on this window: 15th, 10:20 — barge interest, this photograph, ten days after these sales closed. Advance lodged, received.*

---

Wend Lyle was nineteen and he had been on the second floor for a year and eleven months and he had a rule about the forward board, and the rule was that you read it every morning because it is the only thing in the building that changes in a way you can be blamed for not knowing.

He read the five lines under the photograph, and then he read the price, and then he read the price again.

"Mr. Tarn."

"Mm."

"The list has got a new line on it."

"It has."

"SUPPLIED — LOCAL CORRECTED READING, per ward. Signed, timed, dated, with public corrections. Free to the district. **Threepence a week.**"

"Threepence a week," said Edda Tarn, without looking up.

"Who set that line."

"I did."

"On whose authority."

"On mine, and the partner's, on the Monday, and it is in the printed list and it goes up on the wall in the quotation hall at eleven and has been in the column since, and it is not a notice, Mr. Lyle, and it is not provisional, and I would be obliged if you did not treat a thing in a printed column as a thing somebody has not thought about."

"Then I won't." He looked at the board again for about eleven seconds. "Who is it, though. On the shutter. The two that are named."

"I don't know," said Edda Tarn, "and it does not matter, and that is going to be a line in a report by the end of the week and I shall be the one who has to defend it."

---

Here is the whole of what happened on the morning of the fifteenth of Drownmonth, and it took about four minutes and it did not require anybody to be dishonest.

**The window of the morning of the seventeenth was sold three times.** Twice on the fourth, twelve minutes apart, to a salvage interest and to a firm that disclosed, correctly, that its use was its own onward sale, and both of those were lawful and both of them were on the board and both of them were printed with the price. And on the fifteenth, at twenty past ten, to a barge interest, and the instrument of that sale was a photograph taken on Monday off a public apron wall, and the man who took it had been refused at a shed door and had then gone and asked a barge operator's mate a question and written the answer down, and the answer was true.

There is no mechanism in the Exchange's form, or in its licence, or in the world, that stops any of it, and everybody involved knew that, and nobody lied.

**And on the Saturday the twelfth, at twenty past seven in the morning, the salvage interest who had paid seven and six on the fourth returned his window, and was refunded in full, and the refund and the release were published, and the price was published, and the whole of it took about nine minutes.**

That last one is the reason the morning matters, and it is not anybody's villainy, and it is the single most expensive thing anybody did in this basin this quarter.

---

Garrick Dellow came up to the west end of the city on the Saturday morning at about a quarter to seven, in a good coat, and he paid the refund and he did not argue about it and he would not have the last half-crown of the clerk's time.

"I am giving it back," he said, "because I have found out something and I would rather not have it than argue about it."

"You have not found out anything about your water. Nobody's told you anything about your water. That is the whole of our position and I said so in an assembly room a month ago and I was not lying when I said it."

"Then I have found out something about the price."

"Say it, then."

"I paid seven and six for a window on a reach I have been standing on for twenty years and I have never once been wrong about that reach, and I have never once been able to tell you what it was going to do, and I have accepted that for twenty years and it has never once cost me a night's sleep." Dellow put his hat on the counter. "**I have found out that a district can close the water up. I have found out that it can pay for it. I have found out that the ward spent a whole repair year doing it on purpose so that a man like me could not get at the ground, and it is written down, in a fair hand, in a form, with a bell on a wall.** And I have thought about it since Friday, on and off, and I have got to the bottom of it and I do not like the bottom of it."

"Mr. Dellow—"

"So you may have it back and go and sell it to somebody, and I should like to be recorded as having said in this room that I would rather have it, and I would rather have had it in March, and I would rather have had it in any of the eleven years I have been buying this morning, and I did not ask for it in any of the eleven because nobody ever came to the door."

He got as far as the door and then stopped, which is a thing that happened to him at about this time every week.

"One more thing and then I am gone. That photograph. There is a man on this apron this morning with a camera and a barge tied up, and if that reaches your board with a price on it, then there will be a person in a flat I've used the name of all my life, who has never seen my reach, come in a barge on Thursday morning to take the last nine days of a season off it, and he will not be stealing it. He will have paid for it. He will have a receipt." He turned round. "**And I could not tell that man's grandchildren that nobody came to the door. That is all this is. I am not a good man, I am a man who does not want to have that conversation.**"

Then he was gone, and the clerk wrote *released, Sat., 07:20* on the board, and the price of the released window was published, and that is the entire record and it is four lines of it.

---

**The hearing was at half past six that evening in the room over the warden's locker's yard, and it was not a hearing, and the only reason it is called one is that the minute of it is headed *before the entry bell*.**

There were eleven people in the room and the stove was going and the chair was in her usual place at the end of the two tables pushed together, and Edda Tarn had come out to the west end of the city at half past two in the afternoon with a printed list and a fair hand and had not come to argue with anybody about anything.

She set it out in about nine minutes, standing up, and she was not the agent who had come in the autumn and she was not the man from the good coat and she was a counter clerk of nine years' standing and she is a person who is very good and knows it.

"Two holders at ten o'clock this morning, and two holders two days ago, and one of the two on Saturday was a salvage interest with twenty years on that ground and a gang of four and a barge of his own, and he is a man anybody in this room would have voted for, and he gave it back at twenty past seven on Saturday morning, and he gave a reason, and the reason is in the margin of the board, and I have read it four times and I have not found a lie in it and I have not found a way to argue with it either."

"What does the firm want?" said Dessie Trask.

"The firm wants the ward to do nothing, because the ward doing nothing is a Tuesday's work and a Tuesday's work is what this firm is for. The firm would prefer the morning to open. **The firm would prefer the morning to open because a morning that opens is a morning the firm has been right about**, and a morning that closes is a second line struck through in the same quarter at the same price, and I am going to come to that in a minute and I have not got anybody's permission to say it and I am going to say it anyway."

"Get on with it," said Bram Slee, from the back, in a voice nobody in the room had heard since the twenty-first of Sleetmonth.

"The firm will also tell you, and it is true, and I would rather say it than have it said for us: **a rule that excludes a district excludes everybody with the same shape, and there are a great many of them and we have sold to all of them and we do not know it and neither does the Council.**

"Our refusal rule is that a window is sold to a party that can show a use for the hours. This ward showed a use in the autumn — a crew on a flat with nine hundred pounds of timber and a pump and nine men — and the agent said so, in this room, and said it was the best use he had heard that week, and he was right. And this ward did not get the window, not because of the use, but because the sales for that morning closed at four o'clock on the Saturday and this ward had not bought, and a thing that has not been bought before its sales close does not exist afterwards.

"Now count them. A barge operator. A salvage interest. A dredging contractor. A pump gang. A water office with a shoring crew and a cracked headwall. A yard that wants forty minutes for a lighter. A grain factor with a loading berth. A mill. A ferry. A drain company. **Every one of them is a party whose whole product is an hour of somebody else's water and not one of them can show a use that costs seven and six.** Not one. And every one of them is a ward, and every one of them pays the same as this ward would have paid, and not one of them has ever been asked what it could do with the hour it bought, because asking costs a relationship and not asking costs a client, and we have been running on that for nineteen years and nobody has ever read it out loud in a public room."

The room did that thing a room does when the person speaking has just said the number of people in it.

"You have said something very clever," said Neris Kade, "and I want to know whether any of it is going to happen on Thursday."

"No," said Edda Tarn. "None of it is going to happen on Thursday. I have said it out loud because the memorandum I wrote on Monday said the day we stopped asking we stopped knowing, and I have been in this building nine years and I have never said that in a room, and somebody from a firm wrote it down in an envelope with nothing on it and sent it to a Council, and he is twenty-nine and I have never met him, and he was right, and I am not having it on my conscience that the only time a counter clerk in this firm says a true thing out loud is in a document somebody else wrote."

---

**And then the woman who had held a window closed for a fortnight in a room with a bell on the wall said the only thing in it that was going to cost anybody anything, and she had not prepared it and she had not intended to say it.**

"What it costs," said Neris Kade, "is the reach."

"Warden—"

"**Not a window. The reach.** The window was the fourth of the month and I have been without it since the twenty-third of Sleetmonth and I am used to that and it is a piece of paper. This is the last of them. Nine days a year and the seventeenth is the largest and the word largest means what it says, and the water office's table says the front is back in two years and about eleven months and there is a front table with a Friday on it, and the wall is in its second season and a third is coming, and there is a crew of eleven on a contractor's books who have been on the books since the sixth of the Bellweather month and are to be stood down on Thursday morning, and they will be paid for the day and a half because I am not going to have a district's crew stood down for nothing, and there are nine hundred pounds of timber in four bands in a shed forty yards off this wall, and after Thursday the table will say two years and about nine months, and it will not say why, because the table is a table and not a column."

"You are spending the reach," said Dessie Trask, "to keep a barge out of it."

"Yes."

"That is the third time in a month this ward has decided to make itself poorer and safer, in a room, in writing, with the price read out before the vote." The chair had not raised her voice once. "And I have supported the first two. And I am going to support this one. And I would like the room to notice that nobody has asked me and nobody is going to, and that I would like that noticed, and written in the minute if anybody's got a pen."

Nobody had asked her and nobody was going to. That was the ward's condition and it was Neris Kade's condition and it came out of it in one sentence, and it is the single most important administrative fact anybody in this basin has produced this year.

**The ward would do it in writing. The ward would not be asked. And nobody in a shop was to be told why.**

---

Sabin Orle came at about eight and read the whole of the sponsor's cost out loud in plain words before she would draft a line of it, which is what she did on the twenty-first of Sleetmonth, and which is now apparently a thing she does.

"First. This is a true claim. The reach does not come down on the morning of the seventeenth, and I will certify that. There are two instruments in this basin that can establish it and I have checked both. One is a float in a wall at the south end of the freight apron, a mile and a half from this room, with a glass datum in it that a maintenance hand cut and did not anneal and declared on the docket. The other is a mark cut round a post at knee height by men who are dead, read with a chain and a staff, and the district's crew clerk reads that post every month of the year and has done for nine years and I have his book in front of me on the seventeenth of last month and on the one before. Neither of those two has anything to do with the weather's intentions and neither of them can be argued with by anybody.

"Second. The turn that closes is the turn that would have put the water off the reach on the morning of the seventeenth, and with it the last of the season's reach and the last chance this season to work the Lower Sill headwall, and the standing of a crew of eleven for a day and a half, and about two months of a table on a wall at my office. I am required to record that the sponsor has named it and that the naming is the ward's informed choice and was taken in a room in its presence, and I have said it out loud in this room because on the last occasion I said it out loud in a room, and I would like it recorded that I said it out loud again.

"Third, and this is the instrument, and I have had it in my head for two days and I have not slept properly for either of them." She put her page down. "**The listed alternative is available to the firm and it is a real alternative and I am going to write it into the seventh box in words the district uses.** If the Exchange withdraws the quotation for the morning of the seventeenth before the entry bell, no claim binds. The reach behaves as the weather decides. The advances are refunded and the price is published. And the district is answerable to the people it did not move."

"And who takes that?" said Bram Slee.

**"The firm takes that, and the firm knows it takes that, and that is why the ward's cost is a reach and not money. The Exchange can withdraw, and withdrawal is published, and this quarter already has one reach line struck through on that wall at seven and six on the twenty-third of Sleetmonth, and the sales for that morning closed at four o'clock on the Saturday, and this morning this firm sold it again. **If there is a second line struck through before the end of the month then a market that has struck a line through twice in public in one quarter cannot go on selling the thing behind it, and the firm will not be able to put a price on the bottom of the Bellweather reach for a year.** That is the whole of the trade this ward has and the ward is spending it, and the firm is being asked to spend its own future, and I am not going to pretend to anybody in this room that I do not know which of us is being asked to do the more."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That is a lever," said Neris Kade.

"That is a **price**," said Sabin Orle. "There is no difference any more and that is what has happened."

---

**The claim went on the rail at four minutes to ten on the Wednesday morning of the sixteenth day of Drownmonth, in one hand, without lifting the pen, and the sheet has a seventh box on it that is the third time that box has ever been filled in by anybody in the world.**

*Reaches come down about nine days a year and the seventeenth is the largest of them and it is the last. The Exchange's sales for that morning closed on the fifth and this ward did not buy and cannot now be sold to, and this ward's own use is a wall and a pump and nine hundred pounds of timber in four bands, and the cost of doing that work is one and four against a price of seven and six, and the Exchange's own man said in this room in the autumn that it was the best use he had heard that week, and he was right. The crew of eleven is stood down at dawn and is paid for the day and a half. The wall is shored a third time out of other timber or it is not shored. The water office's table goes from two years and about eleven months to two years and about nine months, and the table will not say why, because the table is a table. THE WARD IS CLOSING THE LAST REACH IT WILL GET THIS SEASON TO STOP A THIRD SALE OF IT. That is the cost and it is not money and there is not any money in it at all.*

*No person in any shop, counter, yard or public room is to be told why this claim was entered. That is a condition of the sponsor and the assessor has minuted it.*

It came in the middle of the sixth box, in the fair hand of a district clerk who has now seen one twice and did not write either, and it came to a sheet of foolscap pinned to a rail on the end wall of a records room, and there were nine people in the room and a reader in it and a second reader at the back of it who had been paid eleven shillings, in coin, on the Saturday, to hold the far end of a read she had already done once, and a copy clerk at a desk in the west end two hundred feet away.

**Nobody knew what it was made of except the two people in the room who had seen one before, and both of those people did the same thing, and it is worth being exact about it, because it is the difference between the twenty-first of Sleetmonth and the sixteenth of Drownmonth.**

On the twenty-first, Marek Vale had been standing on the same side of the rail and had taken a slip of bench paper out of his pocket and written it out in his own hand and put it back in his coat, and it was a piece of paper with no chain behind it and it was the only account in the world of what a claim had cost anybody, and he had kept it.

On the Wednesday he took it out. It was in the coat pocket, folded twice, four weeks old, in his own small hand, and it said the same two words it had said on the twenty-first, and there was no difference between the two pieces of paper except that on the sixteenth the same two words were standing in the middle of the sixth box of a public form in a room with nine people in it.

**He put the slip back in his pocket. He did not need it any more, and that was the whole of the lesson and he got it in a corridor at about eleven and sat down on a bench for a minute, and it was the first time since the twenty-first of Sleetmonth that anything in this basin had cost him less than it was worth.**

The clerk read the two lines out loud, because a thing that is not read out loud in a room is a thing that four people remember differently, and the ninth ward's head on the wall above the rail did not light, and the claim bound anyway, because a ward cannot be bound by a thing it is told.

At eleven twenty-two in the west end of the records room, a copy clerk of twenty-seven made the Office's true copy of the same sheet in eleven minutes, in her own hand, dated, timed and initialled, and put it in the tray, and the tray went to the technical cell, and that is the whole of the procedure and the procedure is correct and always has been. **She did not make a second one. Nobody asked her to make a second one.**

---

**The Exchange struck the line through at eleven o'clock on the Wednesday, on its own wall, in the middle of the day, in public, with the price in the margin in a fair hand, and paid two refunds in full at three and nine, and Edda Tarn published the withdrawn price at 7/6.**

By a quarter past twelve there were about thirty people in front of it, and a barge operator from the north side of the city was saying, cheerfully, that a window is a window.

And Edda Tarn stood up on the step in the rain and said the sentence out loud, and it took her eleven seconds, and she had not cleared it with anybody and there was nobody she could have cleared it with.

"**There are two reach lines struck through on this wall in one quarter. They are the same reach. They are the same price. One of them is three weeks old and the other one is this morning, and both of them were seven and six, and there is no person in this city who can tell you the difference between them by the number.** And in the days after the storm that reach was quoted at two shillings by a partner of this firm in a hurry, and there is a line further up this wall with two shillings in the margin, and nobody has ever defended those two shillings, and that is not a matter of anybody defending anything. It is that this firm has now published four numbers for the same forty minutes in six weeks and every one of them was printed by us, and the district's own figure for that morning was furnished to us for money, and it has never once been printed, and it is the only one of the four that anybody who lives there can check."

A man at the front said, "Are you saying the price is wrong?"

"No," said Edda Tarn. "**I am saying that a market cannot go on selling the thing behind a line it has struck through twice in public, and that everyone in this room now knows it, and that I have been trying to say it since the twenty-third of Sleetmonth in about nine different rooms and I was not listened to once, and the only reason anybody is standing here is that a district spent a river on it.**"

Nobody thanked her. A clerk told her that the next board was on the Monday and that she would want to be early. A partner read the four lines of her minute over her shoulder at about two o'clock and said that she was not to put it on the wall in those words and that he would put it in the firm's own hand instead, and he did, and it was shorter, and hers was the one that was true.

---

**On the Thursday morning, the seventeenth, the back-cut came, and it was the largest of the season, and the reach did not come down.**

It came in hard from the west at about five and the upper reservoir dropped fourteen inches in eleven hours and a long band came round southerly and laid itself over the whole basin and *held*, and a band with no bend in it is a fixed pressure gradient, and it is the reason a reach is not scarce and nobody who is told about it believes that.

At about half past six in the morning, a man of about sixty-eight read a post.

He came down the hill road on his own feet at about half past five, with a chain and a staff, out of a shed, because the district's own water office clerk was on a pump and could not be spared and nobody had thought to send anybody else, and the mark cut round that post at knee height by men who are dead was under six inches of mud, and the mud was not going anywhere.

He wrote it in his own level book in the right column, which is a column for the level, and then in the fourth column, which is for what the sky was doing, and he wrote *band, all day so far, mud over the mark* and he shut the book and carried it back up the hill.

"Neris," he said, at the gate of the south-bank shed, at about ten past seven, with the chain over his shoulder.

"Mr. Petrie."

"You'll not be working it."

"No."

"Aye." He looked at the door and the chain on the door and the painted board with no name on it and the sixth of the Bellweather month on it. "Well, that's that, then."

"That is that, then," said Neris Kade, and neither of them said anything else about it, and they stood in a gateway for about a minute and a half, and neither of them ever repeated it.

**Dellow's barge was hired and tied and not discharged. The barge interest's light was never let go. The gang of eleven was stood down at dawn and was paid for the day and a half. The ward's own crew of six with the water steward did not go out, and the second pump house ran, and about an inch came into the third block's low side and went out again by the Friday, and no ground floor was wet anywhere in the ward.**

And about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands, and a four-hundred-and-ten-pound dewatering pump on a pallet with its cover on, stayed where they have been since the sixth of the Bellweather month under a painted board with no name on it, and the wall at the Lower Sill went into its **third consecutive season** of not being worked on, and the water office's table on the wall went from two years and about eleven months to **two years and about nine months**, and the clerk who keeps that table moved the peg without being asked and wrote nothing in the line between, and nobody could produce the line between, and a reach is not a season.

**Nothing is refunded. Nothing reopens. Nobody is paid twice for the same morning, and the Exchange paid two refunds in one morning at three and nine on the sixteenth, on printed receipts, without argument, in the ordinary way, and had paid a third on the twelfth, when a man gave a window back with a reason nobody in that building could argue with.**

---

**The second read was on the Wednesday afternoon, at about half past three, on a bench at Kestrel Yards, and it cost eleven shillings, and Marek Vale paid it himself**, in coin, out of about three pounds that was left, and nobody in the ninth ward was told and nobody in the Cloud Exchange was told, and the only other person in the room was a counter-reader with a satchel and a fourth notebook.

It was a bend in a Release. A Release is a sequence fact and a level instrument cannot see one, and a claim of this kind is certified on six conditions and one of the six is a reader's attestation of the order of the movements and of the order only, and there are two ends to that, and he has one channel and has told a route officer so in a yard and has not put it in a file.

"Ninety minutes," she said, when she had it. "You have paid me for two days this week and you did not get a trace, and you got a second opinion, and those are not the same thing, and I want it on the board that they are not the same thing. In about a year somebody is going to read a document with your name on it and mine on it and conclude that one reader can hold a reach. Nobody can hold a reach."

"No," said Marek Vale. "Two people can hold a reach and it still comes apart, and we have written both halves of that on a shutter in your district, and the shutter is the reason your name is on this."

---

**And on the Friday, at about a quarter past four in the afternoon, a man of twenty-nine came down the hill road with two figures on a piece of paper and found a shed foreman sitting on an upturned crate in the sun with a chain and a staff across his knees.

"Mr. Petrie."

"Mr. Mear."

"I was in a room this morning with a market that has now struck a line through two reach windows in one quarter at the same price, and a district that spent a river, and an assessor who read out a cost before she would draft a line of it, and I have been sitting on a wall by the canal for about two hours trying to work out what the number is now." He held up the paper. "Seven and six to a barge interest, one and four to a district holding the load already bought and paid, five and a half times, and I sent it to a Council in an envelope with nothing on it and got a hearing on Thursday, and there is a woman in that building who has written nineteen words in a book that is a foot and a half thick and called it a finding of her own Council's own record, and I would like to know what you think of the reach."

Petrie looked at him.

"I think," he said, "that you're the third man in a fortnight to ask me what a reach is worth, and the other two asked me what it was going to *do*, and there is a difference, and the difference is the price. And I have a chain and a staff and a post and six inches of mud over the mark this morning, and if you want my figure it is that the mark was under at ten past six on the seventeenth and I have written it in my own book in the column it goes in, and nobody has asked me for that book in thirty years and it is not a record and it is not compellable and it is worth nothing, and it is the only figure in this basin that anybody who lives here can check."

Tobias Mear stood in a gateway on a freight apron in the sun with a piece of paper in his hand.

"Say that again," he said.

"No," said Petrie. "You'll not get it in words, son. You'll have to come and read the book."
