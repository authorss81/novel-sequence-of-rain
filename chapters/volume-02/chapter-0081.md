# Chapter 81 — A Lighter's Answer

The room over the chandler's in the sixth ward is nine feet by eleven and has a stove in it and about nine feet of shelf and a window that looks at the water, and it is the district clerk's office and it is open from six until the bell, and on the afternoon of the twenty-ninth day of Drownmonth eleven people were in it and about half of them were there because a lighter had told them on Saturday that he was going to do something with a tide.

Warrick is fifty-eight and has been a lighter on the sixth ward's water for thirty-one years and he was taught to make a rope's turn by somebody older who is dead, and he has never in his life signed anything that was not a charter.

---

"**You will not get it out of me.**"

"You are not going to get a figure out of anybody, Mr. Warrick, and I have not asked you for one, and I have written on the top of my page in pencil that I am not going to, and it is the first thing on the page."

"Then what is it you want."

"I want a cost." Sabin Orle put her pencil down, which is a thing she does about four times a year. "In your words. In the form. I have to read it out in this room before I will draft a line of it, and I did it in the Bellweather month in front of nine people and I have apparently got a habit, and there is a clerk in the basin house about four miles from here who will minute whatever I say and I would rather be corrected here than there."

"Then you'll have it in pencil, because I haven't a pen and I'm not going down for one." He put a stub of pencil on the table. "And you'll have it in the four lines, because there's four lines and I wrote them on the back of your form three weeks ago in a room with a stove in it and gave them to a boy from your office and I never heard a word since, and they're the truest thing I've ever put on paper."

**The four lines, in a hand taught to make a rope's turn:**

*The sixth ward holds four flat-bottomed lighters and a chain barge and a lighterman and two men.*
*We can have 40 tons of stone on the low side of the sixth ward's channel in about six hours at a spring tide, and we can do it twice a day, and we can do it in three days' bad weather at half.*
*The chain barge needs nine feet and she draws six and there is a bar at the Nacre end that takes her at eight and a half and I would not.*
*This is a lighter's answer. I have no idea what your form wants.*

---

**Here is the thing about a tide that nobody in this room had thought of, and it is the reason the chapter is called what it is called, and it is about four words long.**

**A tide is arithmetic.**

It does not need a reader. It does not need an instrument, a claim, a window, a barometer or a bell. It happens at a fixed hour with a fixed height because the water is being pushed by the moon, and every man on that water has known it since he was a boy, and Warrick had said the height out loud on the Saturday — *nine foot three, and the second of them is the one I've been waiting on since Michaelmas* — before anybody in this room had thought about making anything out of it.

"That is why you can do this," said Sabin Orle. "**A claim about a reach needs a reader and we have got one censured and he is in a chair at the far end of the ridge and he can attest the order of the movements and nothing else, and the hour on your claim is a figure out of a table and neither you nor I nor he can alter it by a minute. That is why this is the first claim in this basin anybody has been able to certify on an evening's notice.**"

"And the other reason?"

"The other reason is that a tide is not a possibility. It is a schedule." She wrote something down. "**And a district that spends a schedule is spending the only thing it had, because a schedule comes round again and a possibility does not.**"

---

"Do you understand what you have just told me, Mr. Warrick?"

"About the tide?"

"About the tide." She turned a sheet round so he could see it, and it was the whole of a claim form with six boxes on it and the sixth of them empty, and she had written nothing on it yet. "I have refused a claim in this basin this month for a warden who cost her own ward two reaches on purpose, in a room, with a clerk, because there is no box anywhere in the Charter of Public Notice, or in that building, or in this city, for a district that has cost itself something on purpose. **The ninth ward has cost itself two reaches. And you are about to cost yourself a tide, and there is still no box for that, and I am going to have to write my own box and I am telling you that I am doing it before I do it, which is not how this is supposed to work and which I have done exactly twice in eleven years.**"

"Write it," said Warrick. "I've thirty-one years' practice in writing things down that nobody asked for."

---

**And there is one other thing that happened in that room between three and four in the afternoon, and it is in nobody's minute, and it is the whole of a reader's condition and none of the room knew it.**

The assessor read the tide hour off a table and put the table down. Marek Vale, at the back, on a censured reader's pass and an attestation that is not an act of that office and is not on anybody's list, asked her to repeat it.

She repeated it. He wrote it down. Then he asked her to repeat it a second time, in the same words and the same voice, and she repeated it again, and he said thank you, and wrote it down again.

Nobody remarked on it. Perdie Alvay was looking at her book. Warrick was looking at the seventh box. The assessor was looking at the paper in front of her and **she did notice, because she notices everything that happens in a room she is running, and she looked up for about a second, and then she looked back down, because a man asking a woman to say a figure twice in a room of eleven people is not a thing a Council assessor has any procedure for and she had a claim to get through.**

**He has asked a question twice, in a room, in front of eleven people, four times in nine weeks. He does not know he does it. There is nobody in this basin who could tell him, and the one person who would have told him is in a drying room above the Ninth Baths with a fourth notebook and a condition of her own, and he has still not told her, and he did not tell her at about a quarter to midnight on the Monday night either.**

---

**The claim went on the rail at ten past four on the afternoon of the twenty-ninth day of Drownmonth, in a fair round hand, on a sheet of foolscap, and the sixth box was the sixth box and the seventh box is the fourth time in this city that it has been filled in by anybody, and it was the first time one of them was in pencil.***Place: the low side of the sixth ward's channel, between the Nacre bar and the stone berth, and the sixth ward's quay wall from the berth to the second bollard.*
*Trigger: the sixth ward's masons released from the morning's booking and the lighters not loaded.*
*Effect: the spring tide on the morning of the thirtieth does not come above six feet nine at the low side, and forty tons of stone do not go on the low side that morning.*
*Expiry: the end of that morning, on the same line as the effect, at the assessor's instruction.*
*Sponsor: W. Warrick, lighter, sixth ward.*
*Concurring seat: the sixth ward, at four o'clock, in the room over the chandler's, eleven present, in about ninety seconds.*
*Attestation: a reader's attestation of the order of the movements only — M. Vale, reader, not on field duty, not in this office's service.*

**And the seventh box, which is on the back of a Council form, in pencil, and which is a schedule of a lighter's responses to a season's water written by the only man in this basin who has ever written one:**

*The four lines above are what the sixth ward can do about its water. They are the answer. I have written them three weeks ago and a boy took them away and I have never heard anything and I am not expecting to.*
*This claim is not a refusal. I want forty tons on that wall in the six hours on the morning of the thirtieth and I have been waiting since Michaelmas for the second of them because the first is not big enough for the chain barge and the second is, and it is nine foot three, and it is the last one before the end of the month.*
*What it costs: the second high spring of the thirtieth at the low side. The chain barge cannot lie in. Forty tons of stone do not go on. The masons are eleven days booked and they are stood down at seven and they are paid for the morning. The lighters are hired and they are not loaded, and they go back down empty at the turn, and I pay the hire and nothing else comes back. The wall waits a month and it will get a month because there is one more tide after this and it is the small one and it takes the lighters at half, and if it is a bad month I get three days at half and I will take that as it comes.*
*I cannot tell you what a tide is worth. I have never been asked and I have never thought about it, and if you put a figure on this I will not sign it, and I will not sign it in pencil either.*

**And then, at the foot of it, in the same pencil, four more lines, and they are the reason nobody who was in that room is going to be able to argue with a lighterman for a month:**

*A tide is not a weather. It is a clock with water on it, and I have a charter signed on Tuesday for a stone barge at ten o'clock on the morning of the thirtieth, and it says the stone goes on. If the stone does not go on I have broken a charter I signed myself, in my own name, to a stone merchant who has nothing to do with your office and never has.*
*The ninth ward closed its own reach and broke nobody's contract, because a ward is a party to nothing and that is the whole of its protection. I am a party to nine and that is the whole of my trouble and I do not want a lecture about it.*
*You can certify it. I know you can. A warden on the ninth closed her own reach twice in a fortnight and told the whole low side what it was going to cost before one word of the claim was written down, and I have a charter signed for a stone barge at ten o'clock on the morning of the thirtieth, and I would rather be told now.*

---

**The concurrence took ninety seconds, and a concurrence is the only part of a claim anybody in this basin gets to watch being bought.**

The sixth ward's chair is a woman of sixty-four called **Perdie Alvay** and she has been in that seat for nineteen years and she has a gavel she has never used and a book she brings to every meeting. There were eleven people in the room and about six of them had come for the meeting that was not happening.

"The ward's concurring?"

"The ward's concurring."

"On what?"

"On the tide." Warrick put the pencil down, which is a thing he does when he is going to be exact. "**The tide's the only thing I've got and I know exactly what it's worth and I've just written it down for the first time in my life and it comes to a second high spring and forty tons of stone and eleven days of masons, and I am asking to spend it.**"

"Then I want to know what we get."

"We get the wall fed in the spring instead of in the summer. And a month of it on our own money. And the seventh ward's lighters don't get the stone and they get the reach, which is what they actually want, and they have wanted it since I was a boy and they've never once had to ask anybody for it because it was in my yard and it wasn't in theirs." He turned round. "**And I will say to the room that the ninth ward closed two reaches in a month and cost a year off a table on a wall, and this is a smaller thing than that and it is going to be on a page for ever, and I am not asking for anybody to treat me like the ninth ward, because I broke a charter when I do it and they broke nobody's.**"

Perdie Alvay wrote it in the book herself, in about twenty seconds, and nobody asked her a second question, and the vote, when it came, was seven to two with two abstentions, and one of the two was a man who said he had a barge on the reach and one of the two was a man who said he did not know what a spring tide was, and both of those are on the minute.

**And then the bell was struck at ten past four for the sixth ward, once, and the sixth ward's head on the wall above the rail lit for the length of the strike and then went out.**

**Nobody looked up. Warrick was looking at the seventh box and the assessor was looking at the paper and the rest of them were looking at the ward clerk's hand, and the glass on that wall did the only thing it has ever done and every one of the eleven of them missed it, and it is the whole of the ward-mismatch argument, and it took about four seconds.**

**No ward's glass has lit for a claim since the Council began printing them. The ninth ward's has not lit since the middle of the Bellweather month and lit not at all for either of its claims. The sixth ward has a pane and the ninth ward has a bubbled temporary in a hand-bedded brass surround, and the difference between those two states of glass is a district's glasswork line, and it is the whole of what a ward is worth in this city.**

The clerk read the sixth box out loud, because a thing that is not read out loud in a room is a thing that four people remember differently, and the seventh box was not read out loud, because the seventh box is not part of the claim and is the sponsor's own, and **that is the whole point of the seventh box, and every person who has ever filled one in has understood that and no person has ever been told to.**

And at eleven twenty-six in the west end of the records room a copy clerk of twenty-seven made the Office's true minutes copy of the same sheet in eleven minutes, in her own hand, dated, timed and initialled, and put it in the tray, and the tray went to the technical cell, and that is the whole of the procedure and the procedure is correct and always has been. **She did not make a second one. Nobody asked her to make a second one.**

It came in the middle of the sixth box, in the fair hand of a district clerk who has seen one three times now and did not write any of them, and it came to a sheet of foolscap pinned to a rail on the end wall of a records room, and there were eleven people in the room and a reader in it and a lighter at the front with his cap still on, and **nobody in that room knew what it was made of except the two people who had seen one before.**

---

**And at about six o'clock the assessor wrote a line into that column in the Office's weekly sheet, in the cold version, in the ordinary hand, for the Monday's printing — the fourth this volume and the fifth the column has ever had, and the column is still headed CLAIM — DISPLACED TURNS and the column still has no sponsor field and the heading still carries no disclaimer:**

*Tide, sixth ward, low side, lower than quoted, forty tons of stone not carried, morning of the thirtieth. Basis: a claim entered by a district. No branch of this office was closed.*

**It is the first line in a hundred and forty years of that column that is not about weather at all.**

She read it in the proof at about half past six and she did not strike it, and she did not put a note on it, and she said out loud to an empty room: *"That is going to be on a printed sheet on Monday and it is a tide, and there is no column in the world for a tide, and the column is called dry lines."*
