# Chapter 74 — Nine Addresses

The Board wrote back on the Monday and it was four lines on a printed sheet with a number on it, and the number was a minute number, and the four lines were courteous, and one of them was a question.

*Kell Bray, Hearthward assembly clerk.*

*The minute of the sixth day of the month records that a line has been entered in the Standing Response Register for the ninth ward. The minute of the tenth of the month records a finding that the ninth ward's line has been unfilled since the forty-fifth, and the finding is minuted in the technical cell's own hand on the flyleaf of a public book.*

*The Board asks on what authority a minute of this Council comes to name, in a public register, the failure of a district to answer for forty-five years, and whether the finding is proposed to be re-set.*

*No reply is required within the month.*

Kell Bray read it at half past eight on the Monday morning of the twentieth, in the Hearthward district office, with a council meeting at ten.

---

**The council meeting at ten that morning was about a boundary hedge and it went on for an hour and ten minutes and it is the only meeting of that council in eleven years that Kell Bray has had to sit through and say nothing at all in, and she has never since been able to work out why she let the letter sit on the desk until the twelfth bell.**

She did not answer the Board on the Monday, and she did not answer it on the Tuesday, and on the Tuesday afternoon she did the thing that took the most out of her, which was to go and look at the flyleaf.

She had not been into the technical cell's press room since the Friday morning. There is a rule about a member of the public bringing a form into a records room, which is that a member of the public may bring a form into a records room, and there is no other rule about the public in that building and everybody knows it, and the only obstacle to going and standing in front of an open book in a Council basement is that you have to admit to yourself that you want to.

She went at about ten past two on the Tuesday. The book was on the shelf under the bottom door with the current season's working. It is about a foot and a half thick and it is divided into sections by years and each section has a flyleaf giving the seasons it covers, and there are the nine columns, and the ninth ward's line had something under it for the first time since the season of three hundred and fifty-five, and it was in a fair hand and it had a warden's signature and a chair's and a line saying the writer was a clerk of another district and was not asked.

And three inches above the season of three hundred and fifty-five, in the assessor's own hand and the assessor's own ink, it said:

*The ninth ward's line has been unfilled since the forty-fifth. This is a finding of this Council's own record.*

**And underneath that, in a good copperplate ink that has not gone brown, on the cell's own routing side of the stub that was never detached:**

*Issued to the ninth ward's district office. J. Marchant to follow.*

Kell Bray stood and looked at the two lines together for about a minute and a quarter, and there was nobody in the room, and then she went and wrote a minute at the counter in the corridor, in her own hand, and put it in the district file, and it is the only thing she has done in three weeks that anybody in this building has ever formally asked her about, and nobody has thanked her for it.

**And here is what she wrote, in the corridor, in a minute that is in the district file and is not in a Council book, and it is the whole of the chapter's problem in eleven words:**

*The finding stands. It is not withdrawn and it is not re-set, and the district it names has not been told that it exists.*

**She did not soften it. She made it worse.** And the reason is that the ninth ward has a warden who has been a Storm Warden for nine years and a chair of twenty-two years and a clearing gang of six with a water steward, and it is the poorest ward in the city, and in about eleven weeks somebody is going to use a line in a Council's own book to tell that district it has been in the wrong for forty-five years, **and there is nobody in this basin who is going to have to stand in that room and answer for it, and a clerk of another district put the line in.**

---

**Here is what the Board could not see, and what she could, and it is what her week has been and most of her year.**

A finding on a flyleaf is a needle. It is a way of saying *look here* to anybody who opens that book for any reason at all, which is a great many people, because the book is the standing book and it is kept in a press on the ground floor and people go to it. **The ninth ward's line has been unfilled since the forty-fifth. This is a finding of this Council's own record.** It is three inches above a copperplate note in a good ink that says *issued to the ninth ward's district office. J. Marchant to follow*, and a duty book that ends *discharged, end of season 355*.

And a needle is a needle whichever way it points.

*It names a district.* It names the ninth ward, and the ninth ward is the poorest ward in the city, and it has had a dark public glass since the autumn, and it sold its only schedule for eleven shillings to a firm in a vote taken at four o'clock on a Wednesday while the warden was on a pump, and the finding now says, in a book anybody may open, that that district did not answer a letter for forty-five years. **The Board is right to ask the question. That is the difficulty, and it is the whole difficulty. The Board is right to ask it, and Bray knows it is right, and there is nobody in this basin she can complain to about it.**

And underneath the needle, in the same book, in the same room, on the same Friday, there is a form with a district clerk's hand on it in which nobody put a stamp, because there is no requirement, and a records officer said so out loud, and entered it in four minutes.

**The same register. The same fortnight. One line makes a district's silence a matter of public record and the other line puts its capacity in there, and the second one has no authority whatever and no power behind it, and the only difference between the two is that one of them carries a Council's name and the other does not.**

She wrote that down on the back of a form at about a quarter to nine in the morning and then read it and thought it was too clever for a minute and put it in the cell's own minute book instead, which is where the things that are too clever go.

---

**And on the Wednesday the twenty-third the assessor answered the Board's question without answering it. Sabin Orle, assessor:** *That the stub of each season shall be issued to each of the nine wards, in an envelope, addressed, with the season stated; that where a district does not return the stub within the term, the district shall be written to twice; and that where a stub is not returned after the second writing it shall be reported to the Board. That the second-writings box be labelled and dated, and that its emptiness be reported to the Board at the end of each term. That a copy of this direction be placed in the district file of each ward.*

Four paragraphs. It is the first thing this Council has sent out of that building in a generation. It is signed by an assessor who has been eleven years in the technical cell and who has been right about the duty and wrong about a sheet in the same month.

**And it will not work, and Kell Bray worked out in about four minutes why, standing at her own counter on the Wednesday morning, and it took her the rest of the week to do anything about it, and what she did about it was not enough.**

*A ward is not a building. A ward is a district office, a chair, a clerk, and a council, and the address of a ward is not on any list, because the address of a ward is a man or a woman who is in a room and may be replaced in a year. There is no envelope with the stub. There is no address on the cover of the book. Skell said that in a cellar three weeks ago and he was right, and it took her three days to get to where he was standing, and she got there by herself, which is the whole of her method and the whole of her life.*

*A district clerk may not answer for a district without a vote. She had written four hundred returns in twenty years and had never once been asked for a minute about any of them, and a vote takes a fortnight in the ninth ward and a month in Hearthward, and the stub is issued once a season, and there are nine days of it left.*

*And the rule that requires a report to the Board has no book behind it — that is now in the minute in her own hand, which is the second thing she has put in a Council's minute in a fortnight, and the first one nobody has thanked her for, and nobody is going to thank her for this one either.*

**So the same silence is about to happen again. Faster. With an excellent reason. With a nineteen-word finding three inches above it.**

---

**What she did about it was write nine addresses out in a fair hand, over about forty minutes, on the back of a Council form, from a book she keeps in the bottom drawer of her own desk and which nobody in Hearthward knows exists.**

It is a book of other people's clerks' names. It is nineteen years of being the person who signs the return and posts it, and it has got the name of every district clerk she has ever posted a return to and the name of the one who came after them, and the year it changed hands, and the street, in a small neat hand, because she is a woman who has been the only clerk in a room for a very long time and has consequently written things down.

**Nine addresses, in a fair hand, on the back of a Council form. That is the whole of what one clerk did in the whole of the worst fortnight the ninth ward has had since the Bellweather month.**

She put it in an envelope and addressed it to a records officer in the technical cell and wrote *not urgent* on it, and then she did not post it, and she carried it down to the basin house herself at about four o'clock on the Thursday because a thing carried by hand is a thing that can be given to a person rather than left on a table.

Skell took it and turned it over and looked at the back of it for a while.

"These are right," he said.

"They came out of a book in Hearthward."

"Aye. I know where they came from." He did not ask her name, because there was a form in front of him with a name on it, and he read it, and then he said: "**You'll not get it entered. There's no district clerk's stamp on this and it isn't a return, it's a list of addresses, and this Council has no schedule for receiving a list of addresses.**"

"That is all right. I am not asking you to enter it. I am asking you to have it, because I have got no way of getting it in front of anybody else and you are the only person in this city I have heard of who has ever said no to a Council in the right words."

Skell put it in the second drawer from the top, which is where a records officer puts a thing he has not decided about, and it is still in that drawer.

---

**And then on the Thursday she went four miles up the low lane to Ferrygate in a cart, because four of the names in the book are up that lane or in the north of the city, and one of them is a refusal she wanted to see the face of, and because Nell Cray had sent four lines on the Monday by the cart that goes up the low lane with a line at the bottom of them that said she would come if anybody ever asked her anything again.**

Ferrygate is a market district on the northern water with a ferry crossing and the Nacre Gate above it, and its clerk has been in the job nineteen years and her stated ambition, which she says out loud and often, is to get through a week in which nothing interesting happens.

Cray had the book off the shelf before Bray was through the door.

"Two," she said. "That's the second time anybody's wanted a bit. Same three documents. The rota's the same, the fallback's the same, the two-line low-ground entry is the same and I moved it on the twelfth and there was nothing to move it to, so I have left it."

"Six years of it," said Bray. "Nell, six years of the same three documents and nobody has ever asked you for one. Not once. Not even the Council, and they asked me on the ninth and I told you what happened to what I sent them six years ago."

"You did. It was a fair copy and he stamped it and it went in a press under the wrong head and he was right to do it, and I have never minded that, and I have minded everything else for six years." Cray turned the page. "There's a fourth ward refusal in the paper this week. Same sentence as theirs in the middle of the month. They have one way of writing a no and they have had it for forty years and I would not change a word of it and I am not going to say a word about that to anybody either."

"You will say a word about it. You said a word about it on Monday in four lines and it is in a firm's notice in a printing house in the west end of the city this afternoon."

"I did," said Nell Cray, comfortably, and shut the book, and offered Bray tea, and for about a minute and a half nothing interesting happened at all, and it was the best minute and a half of Kell Bray's four days and it is the only thing in those four days she would have written down if anybody had asked her to.
