# Chapter 55 — The Forward Board

The Cloud Exchange's quotation hall is on the ground floor, and it has a counter, a bench for waiting, a stove, and a wall.

The wall is nine feet high, runs the length of the room, and is painted. It has been painted for the firm to put prices on and it has been painted about four times and the undercoat shows in one place near the door where somebody has had a ladder up too enthusiastically. It was bare and distempered at ten o'clock on a Wednesday morning in Sleetmonth and at eleven it was not, and the difference between those two facts is about four gallons of a green that the paint trade calls a district green and is not.

Anwen Sarr came in at about eleven, with Kell Bray, and stood about six feet back from it, and did not go closer for about a minute.

The heading was in black and it was large:

**CLOUD EXCHANGE · WINDOW LIST · FORWARD · WESTERN DISTRICT AND THE YARDS**

**A window is not a forecast.** A window is an undertaking that an operation may be depended upon in the hours named, at the level or the tide or the hour stated. The Exchange takes the risk. If the operation cannot be performed, the price is returned under the refund rule within the week. **The price of a withdrawn window is published.**

Then, in columns, thirty-one lines.

They were not weather. That was the first thing Anwen Sarr established, standing six feet back, and she established it the way she establishes everything, by looking for the thing that is not there. There is not one word on that wall about what the sky is going to do. There is no band, no probability, no arrival, no watch. There is a barge movement and an hour and a state of the tide and a figure in shillings, and that is all there is, and it is thirty-one lines of it, and it is the first legal thing about weather that has been printed in this basin in her lifetime.

And the second thing, which she established in about a further twenty seconds, is that **it is also the first thing anybody has ever been able to sell.**

"A claim says what will happen and costs a branch," she said, to Kell Bray, quietly, in the ordinary voice she used in a market, and not turning round. "A window says what you may rely on and costs nothing. So it can be sold to anybody, and there is no quorum, and there is no ward's seat, and there is no bell."

"No bell at all?"

"Listen to it. There is no bell in it anywhere." She was still looking at the wall. "Read me the ninth line."

Kell Bray, who was thirty-one and had been in a ledger twenty years and four of them here, and who had spent the whole of the previous afternoon in a records office turning pages, read the ninth line out.

**W 9 · Bellweather reach, apron sill to bottom, 40 min, stated ebb. 7/6. Refund at 3/9 if not opened.**

"That is forty minutes of water at the bottom of the Bellweather reach," said Anwen Sarr. "Forty minutes, priced at seven and six, and the whole of it on a wall in a room anybody can walk into. Kell, I have been in this city thirty-one years. There has never been a price on a reach. There has never been a price on anything at all. The Office does not price claims because it cannot price claims. I sat in a chamber in the autumn with a woman who said — do you remember what she said?"

"'It is not accidental that a claim's cost cannot be documented. It is the design.'"

"That is what she said, and she was right, and I have thought about it every day since, and I concluded that the reason she was right was that a cost nobody can document cannot be argued about, and a cost nobody can argue about is a cost that can be kept on being inflicted." Anwen Sarr put her hands behind her back, which is what she did. "Kell. Here is a firm that has documented a cost, in one column, in shillings, in a public room, and it did it by selling something else."

---

They spent until about two in the afternoon failing, and they failed correctly, and Kell Bray kept a list, because Kell Bray always keeps a list.

The first attempt was a request to the Exchange that it return or destroy the copy of the ninth ward's quarterly return. Refused in four minutes by a young man with a good coat who was extremely polite, on the ground that the Exchange had paid for it, had published it, and was not under a licence to stop publishing it. Correct.

The second was a request to the warden's council of the ninth ward to instruct that no further copies be furnished. Refused in four minutes by the council itself, which had already tried, and which produced a minute — its own, not hers — recording that a council may cease sending and has no purchase in a copy it has sent. Correct.

The third was an application, under the Charter of Public Notice, for a district to be heard before a schedule of its published practice is used by any other party in the making of a commercial product. That was the one Anwen Sarr had written on the train and she had believed in on the train.

The clerk in the records office was about sixty and had been in it for a long time and he was not unkind, and he found the answer in about four minutes as well, and he read it out to her, and then he said, "That's the whole of it, and I'm sorry, and I'll say the other thing as well because you've been in and out of this building all autumn and I know what you want it for."

"Go on."

"The Charter lets a district refuse a claim. A district can refuse, if it publishes a workable alternative and stands behind the people who stay. That's written down, that's real, and there's no form for it because it isn't a form, it's a duty." He put a finger on the page. "And a district cannot refuse a price. There's no duty anywhere in this building about a price, and there's no hearing, and there's no notice, and there has never been any, and if a body is willing to publish a copy of your rain for eleven shillings there is nobody in this basin you can complain to and I have looked for about an hour and a half and I have found you a paragraph in a form."

"What is the form."

"It's a request for a public document at the ordinary price, and the district gets the copy, and the district pays the price, and the copy comes by hand within the week." He said it as a man who has been the fourth person to say something like it today. "That's the paragraph. That's the entire sum total of the machinery in this city on the question you've asked me."

Kell Bray wrote it down.

Anwen Sarr did not look at it for a while.

"Nine weeks ago," she said, "we were given a column. You remember what I said about it."

"You said it was more than you asked for and less than you asked for, and that the difference was about three hundred words."

"I did. And what I said about the column was that it printed what a claim closed and refused to print who closed it, and I defended that in front of two hundred people and I said I would defend it for the rest of my life and I have not withdrawn one word of it and I am not going to." She turned round and looked at the wall again, all thirty-one lines of it. "It is on that wall. It is in a different room in a different building, and the wall has got a price on it and the column hasn't got a price on it, and the price is the *whole* of the forty minutes, and the column has got the shape of it. There is a line on that wall struck through in pencil with a figure in the margin and that figure is the morning of the thirtieth of the Bellweather month, and the Office itself put the lost thing on a public table in a public hall nine weeks ago and said plainly that it could not say what it was worth, and a firm in this city has said what it was worth, and it is seven and six, and it is *wrong*, and it is wrong in the direction of the man who is going to charge for it."

"How do you know it's wrong?"

"Because I know two things about that window that nobody on the floor knows and that nobody in the Office knows either, and neither of them is worth anything." She said it flatly, and Kell Bray had a pencil out, and Anwen Sarr looked at the pencil and said, "No," and Kell Bray put it down again, which is the single most useful thing a clerk can do. "No. Not yet. Not in this basin."

---

The Office's own position came in the same post.

It is one printed sheet, four paragraphs, and it is dated in the Bellweather month, and it has a Director's name at the foot of it, and Anwen Sarr has read it perhaps forty times since and has not been able to get round it.

**CIRCULAR 14. WINDOWS AND THE AUTHORITY OF THIS OFFICE.** *A window is an undertaking offered in the market that a named operation may be depended upon in named hours. A window is not a claim. A claim has a place, an effect, a trigger, an expiry, a witness, and an entry on a ward ledger, and only this Office may enter one. A window has none of these and is therefore outside the authority of this Office. This Office will not certify, endorse, price, regulate, or advise upon a window. This Office has no instrument which looks at a river or a reach, and has never had one, and will not build one, and does not consider the absence of such an instrument a defect in this Office's work. Any claim for the value of a reach is a claim against a party that sold a window, and not against this Office. C. ROOK, Director.*

"It is the best document I have ever read," Anwen Sarr said, to Kell Bray, on the pavement, in the rain, holding it folded. "That is what I have got. I have got a Director of this Office who has said out loud, in print, on a sheet anybody can buy, that he is not going to do this, and that the absence of an instrument which looks at a river is not a defect in his work, and that anybody who wants a river looked at must go and ask the man who sold it to him.

"And it is *true*, Kell. Every clause of it is true. I could not get a person in this basin to say one word against that sheet, and I have wanted to for a week, and I have not been able to, and the reason I have not been able to is that I have been looking for a lie in it."

"You are saying he has said it well."

"I am saying he has said it *correctly*," said Anwen Sarr. "And I am saying that a man who is right gets to be right in public, once, in print, for ever, and that we built a whole column last month so that a man who spent a window could not be found by his own name, and that neither of those two facts is a lie and that both of them are working."

They ate at a counter on the western side because it was raining and because Anwen Sarr does not eat a meal standing up.

And at about half past four, on the way back to the hall, she said the thing she had come to say, and she said it in the passage with the stove in it, and Kell Bray took it down afterwards from memory and then destroyed the page, which is not what a clerk does, and which she did anyway.

"The column prints what closed. The wall prints what it costs. **They are the same fact and they are in two buildings, and only one of them has ever been in a room where a person who lost something was in it.**"

"That is a sentence for the requirement."

"It is the preamble to the requirement. And the requirement is the fourth clause again." Anwen Sarr said this without heat, the way a mechanic says a part number. "It is the same four words. A standing requirement, tabled by the Hearthward rain-garden assembly, and it goes to the assembly's own standing order, which means it is not a motion and it is not a resolution and it is a thing the assembly now does. Where a public body's published practice, or a public schedule of its practices, is used by any other party in the making of a product offered for sale, **the public body's own figure is published beside that party's figure, in the same hand, on the same sheet.**"

"That is the clause you gave away the name for."

"The name was for the column. This is a different claim and it is a worse one, because a column is a record of a thing that has happened and this is a demand about who has to be legible in the future, and I have got less for it and I expect less and I am going to ask for it in a worse room than the last one, which is a market, which is not obliged to answer me at all and has not to answer me and will not."

Kell Bray wrote it out that night, in her own hand, without lifting the pen, because that is the practice of that room, and it took her nine minutes and she did it in one line.

It was refused within the hour.

The Exchange's answer is a printed sheet with a date on it and a manager's name at the bottom, and Anwen Sarr has it still, and it is four paragraphs, and the fourth paragraph is the one:

*The Exchange is a licensed counterparty. It publishes its own figures, in full, with its standing and its refund rule. It does not adopt, compare, or reconcile the figures of a public body, and it does not undertake to do so. A public body whose figure differs from the Exchange's is invited to publish it, at the ordinary price, in its own hand, in its own distribution, on any day it chooses. **The Exchange cannot compare two prices for two different objects, and will not.***

They were right, of course. Two prices for two objects cannot be compared. Neris Kade's district's forty minutes was nine men and a flatbed and a timber stack that was already paid for, and a barge operator's forty minutes was a hull and a day's hire, and they are not the same object, and a market which pretended they were would be doing the thing that markets do when they pretend, which is lying.

Which is the trick, and it is a good one, and Anwen Sarr had spent thirty-one years learning to say true things in rooms and had not in her life learned a way to say the true thing that a room could not turn.

---

The Hearthward market had been shut for a month after the water and it was open again, and it was busier than it had been for two years, because the season's stock had been bought and paid for within a fortnight of the water going down, at prices nobody had haggled over, and the buyers had all said the same thing in the same way, which was that they were glad to find seed on the shelf at all.

The lower six drawers of the vaults were ruined. The vaults hold ninety drawers. Everybody in Hearthward knows which six and nobody says it in a market, and Anwen Sarr walked the length of it at about six in the evening on the Wednesday and did not stop, and Kell Bray walked with her and did not ask, and that is the whole of what the Hearthward market is this month.

The rain stopped. The trams ran. And in a hall on the west side of the city, on a wall nine feet high, between the ninth line and the tenth, in a fair hand, there was a figure in shillings with a line struck through it in pencil, and a note saying that withdrawn prices are published, and about four feet to the right of it, on the same wall, in the same paint, a printed sheet about a refund that did not apply to anybody in particular.

Two documents, one building apart. One of them says what a claim cost and cannot say who paid. The other says what a cost was worth and cannot say what it was for.

Anwen Sarr had spent six years getting the first one into existence and it had cost her the only thing she had ever wanted in a room, and she stood in a street on the east side at half past six in the rain and understood, with total clarity, in about the time it took to get on a tram, that she had spent her career perfecting a sentence that could be printed on a wall in a room she was not in.
