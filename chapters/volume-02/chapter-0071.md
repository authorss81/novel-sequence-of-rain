# Chapter 71 — A Year Is Not a Duration

On the Sunday morning of the twentieth day of Drownmonth the temporary compensation ledger sat on a table in a room on the ground floor of the Council's basin house with a printed head and a ruled page and one line in it, and Sabin Orle read the one line four times and then put her hands flat on the table on either side of it, the way a woman does with a book she has been arguing with.

*The first claimant's figure is unknown. The Council pays two shillings and sixpence as an interim figure, because two shillings and sixpence is the only figure in the room, and the reason it is the only figure in the room is that no party in this basin has ever asked a district what it can do about its water.*

That is nineteen words. She had written them on the Friday afternoon with a pen and a clerk and she had said the expiry out loud twice, and on the inside cover of the book, in ink, before anything else went in, she had written **TEMPORARY. PROVISIONAL. EXPIRES AT THE END OF THE FINANCIAL YEAR.**

Two days. That was all it had been open. Two days and three entries and a book that was going to be shut in somebody's hands in about four months and put in a drawer, and there was nothing in it but a statement of ignorance, a ward's receipt, and a copy.

The room had a window that looked out of the eastern ridge and at nothing else whatsoever, a desk, two chairs, and a press against the end wall with two doors of deal and a lock with a hasp. It was Sunday and the building was shut and she had come in on a Saturday key she has had for eleven years, which is not a thing anybody has ever asked her about and which is the reason she is the assessor.

---

"Mr. Peascod."

"Ma'am." He was at the other table with a pen and a tray and the particular stillness of a man who has worked in a records room for nine years and has learned to be furniture.

"I want to be quite clear about the position before I write anything else in this book, because in about nine weeks somebody is going to hold it up and read it in a room."

"Go on, ma'am."

"**A claim is not a claim here. There is no claimant.** I said so on the Friday and I meant it and I have not changed my mind over the weekend, and the reason I have not changed my mind is that I looked for one and there is not one. The ninth ward sponsored two claims. Both were taken. Both were discharged. A claim that has been discharged is not a claim that can be entered again for the same thing, and there is no box anywhere in the Charter of Public Notice, or in this building, or in this basin, for a district that has cost itself something on purpose."

"Yes, ma'am."

"So the Council pays a sum to nobody, at a figure the assessor adopts, and the assessor's power is to adopt an interim figure where a claimant cannot be identified. I have used it once. If I use it again for a second event in the same season, I am not adopting an interim figure, I am inventing one, and I will not do it, and I have thought about it for a day and a half and I have decided that the reason I could do it so easily is not that it is unreasonable. It is that nobody would notice for about two years and I would have gone on doing it."

Peascod put his pen down, which he did perhaps four times a year.

"Minuted," he said.

"Minuted exactly like that."

---

**The letter went out on the Monday morning in an envelope with the Council's postmark on it and it was four lines long and it was the fourth thing she had written to a district in eleven weeks.**

*To the Storm Warden, ninth ward. The Council has opened a temporary compensation ledger and one figure is entered in it. The Council requires a figure from a party before it can enter a second, and the ward is the only party in this basin that has cost itself anything on purpose. The Council therefore asks the ward for a figure for what it lost on the twenty-first of Sleetmonth and on the sixteenth of Drownmonth, stated in the ward's own words and in the ward's own money, with the date on it. A district may refuse, and a refusal will be minuted as a refusal. This is not a claim and it is not a demand and the Council will not enter a figure of its own invention. — S. Orle, assessor, water scheduling.*

She read it four times and posted it herself, at the counter on the ground floor, at about half past ten on the Monday, because there is a woman at that counter who sorts the post and knows which envelope a district will open.

---

The answer came on the Wednesday morning, and it is eleven lines and it is in a hand used every working day for twenty-two years, and it is the best thing anybody wrote to Sabin Orle in the year she was going to remember.

*The ward can give you six men at the district rate for a day, and a gang for the pump and its gear, and nothing for the timber and nothing for the pump, and the timber and the pump are bought and paid and their cost of being there is the cost of turning up. That is one and four. You have it already, off a man with a pencil.*

*The Exchange's figure for the same forty minutes is seven and six and it is a fair price for a hull and a barge hire and a chain and a gang of four and a foreman. You have that too. I am told you paid it out twice as a refund, at three and nine, which is the price of a morning the market did not sell, and I am told you paid a mean of the two, which is two and six, and that you called it the only number in the room.*

*Here is what is between them, and I have thought about whether to write it or not for two days and I am writing it.*

*The ward lost the reach. The reach comes down about nine days a year. Between the twenty-first of Sleetmonth and the seventeenth of Drownmonth there were two of them left and we had neither, and there is not a third before the water office's table says the front is back, which is two years and about nine months and is a Friday. That is not four hundred and thirty mornings of nothing. I do not know how many mornings it is. I have not counted them and I am not going to count them here, because the day I count them is the day this ward has turned a wall into a bill.*

*What I can tell you is what a year is. It is a number of days on which nine men did not go out to a flat because there was no water off it, and the timber stood in a shed under a board with no name on it, and the wall was not shored, and the masons were not called, and the table on the wall moved a peg nobody asked it to move. A day of that is worth nothing and I can prove that, because it is worth nothing all the time and nobody has ever been asked to pay for it.*

*So: you have two figures and you have a space between them and the space is a wall, and the wall is a wall, and if you build a column for the space I will fill it in and I will want it read out in a room with the ward's name on it, and I will want somebody in the room to have to answer.*

*You asked me for a figure. I have given you the two figures there are and I have told you what is between them. There is no third figure and I cannot make one and I will not sign one if you put it in front of me.*

*N. Kade, Storm Warden, ninth ward.*

---

Orle read it at her desk at about nine on the Wednesday morning with the door shut, and then she read it again, and then she got the minute book out and wrote for about forty minutes, and this is what she wrote.

**RULING, in the matter of a temporary compensation ledger, third day of the ledger.**

*1. A loss which is a period cannot be entered as a sum. The Council's only instrument for a sum is a figure supplied by a claimant, and the Council may not supply one itself and may not adopt an interim figure twice for the same season, that being an invention and not an adoption.*

*2. The interim figure of two shillings and sixpence stands. It stands for the season and not for each event. The ward is paid for a morning and not for a wall, and I record that the ward has been paid for a morning and not for a wall, at its request and in its own words, and that it asked to be asked and that it has been.*

*3. I have read the ward's answer and I record that it is a refusal to supply a third figure and a reason, and that the reason is true, and that the refusal is minuted as a refusal because this Council gave its word on the face of a letter in the Bellweather month and has not broken one it gave itself.*

*4. The ledger is not grown by this ruling. It has one line in it and it is a statement of ignorance, and it will expire at the end of the financial year, and if it stands to the end of the financial year with one line in it then this Council will have conducted an investigation into a market and produced a ledger and paid a district a morning's work, and that is the whole of it, and I am not going to write anything else in this book to make it look otherwise.*

She signed it, and she read it over, and then she did the second thing, which took her another ten minutes and which she did not minute, because minuting it would have been a way of not doing it.

She opened the minute book back to the tenth of Drownmonth and copied her own nineteen words onto a fresh page in the same hand and the same ink, with the date of the original at the top, and the words underneath:

*The ninth ward's line has been unfilled since the forty-fifth. This is a finding of this Council's own record.*

**She copied it because a finding that lives on a flyleaf in a book in a press, three inches above a copperplate note, is a finding that can be removed by whoever removes the page, and she has watched a page come out of a book in her own building and she is not going to be the one who let a page decide anything.**

Then she sat for a while with her hands in her lap, which she has not done in a room with a clerk in it in eleven years.

---

**"Ma'am. There is a sentence in paragraph two that is not doing any work."**

"There is, Mr. Peascod, and I have been looking at it for twenty minutes and it has gone from being the best thing in the minute to the worst thing in it, and I would like you to tell me what it is, because you have been in this room nine years and I have been in it eleven and I have got used to my own handwriting."

Peascod read it off the page.

"*The ward is paid for a morning and not for a wall, and I record that the ward has been paid for a morning and not for a wall, at its request and in its own words, and that it asked to be asked, and that it has been.*"

"Read the last part again."

"*And that it asked to be asked, and that it has been.*"

"**That is not a ruling. That is a woman being pleased with herself in a public record, and it is the only sentence in there that is there because I wanted the ward to know that I had not invented the number, and a public record is not a place for a reason the assessor has got.**"

She struck it out with one line, in the ordinary way, and initialled the strike, and left it legible, which is the whole of the practice and the whole of the point.

"The minute now reads: *The ward has supplied two figures and a reason, and has declined to supply a third, and the reason is minuted as given.* That is five lines shorter and it is true and it is worse, and the ward will read it, and a woman who has spent her ward's reach twice in one month and has been paid a morning's work is entitled to know what the assessor actually wrote instead of how the assessor felt about writing it."

"Shall I initial the strike, ma'am?"

"**Yes**," said Sabin Orle. "And put the hour on it. And that is the whole of what I have done this weekend that I would defend to anybody, and there is not a great deal of it, and about a third of it is a sentence I have taken out."

---

**There was one more thing on the Wednesday and it was small and it was the reason the week did not go the way she had planned it.**

She had spent Monday and Tuesday in a room with a number in it, and by the Wednesday afternoon she had put the number down and picked up something else, because there was one other thing on her desk that had come in on the Friday and that she had not opened in front of anybody, and it was a printed list from a firm in the west end of the city, and it was the free district sheet, and it goes out twice a day at a counter in a shop.

She did not have a district to give it to. That was the whole difficulty and it was the difficulty of her entire week, and it was a sentence she had written on a flyleaf nine days earlier and had not thought about since.

*A district that cannot read its notice cannot buy a window, and a district that cannot buy a window is a district nobody has to compensate.*

The ninth ward could read it. The ninth ward had a reader's working painted on a shutter four doors from a tobacconist's counter, with two names on it and an hour and a date and a line at the bottom saying that nobody must do anything because of it. **The ninth ward was the only district in the basin that could read a private weather product better than it could read the Council's own, and she had built the condition for that herself, on the twenty-first of Sleetmonth, in front of nine people, and she had never once considered what it would be like for the thing to be useful.**

She put the list down on the corner of the desk, squared to the edge, and did not open the second one, and said, to a clerk:

"Mr. Peascod, is there anything at all in this building that has a duty to a district's *comprehension*?"

"No, ma'am."

"That is what I thought. Put that in the minute of today and I will initial it."
