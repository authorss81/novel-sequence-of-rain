# Chapter 80 — An Ordinary Hand

The tally book is not a business book. It is about nine inches by five and it is a school exercise book with a lined page and a print of a sailing ship on the inside of the front cover, and it was bought in a shop in the west end of the city for a shilling in 1952 by a man of thirty-three, and he has not written in the front of it since the second of March of that year.

Garrick Dellow took it down on the Tuesday evening of the twenty-ninth day of Drownmonth and put it on the kitchen table and looked at it for a while, and then he wrote on the second of March, at the top of a page, in a fair round hand that is the hand of a man who was taught a good deal by people who were not in school:

*A tally of ground, 1952 to date. What I have taken off and what it has cost me after. — G.D., 33.*

---

**Eleven years of it, and it is the only schedule of a preparation that has ever existed in this basin, and it is in a school exercise book on a table in the west end, and nobody has ever asked for it and it is not compellable and it is worth nothing.**

It is not a forecast and it makes no claim. It has no place, no trigger, no expiry and no entry, and there is no bell for it, and the reason there is no bell for it is that it is a book in a kitchen about a man's own ground.

The first two pages are the only part anybody would call a book.

*19/4/52. 61 yds, N end, kerb and copper and a foot of gravel. Spoil to the third block's drain, carted. Cleared the drains after: 3/- to the ward's man and a cart. Father's man came with him the first two years and then he stopped coming and that is the whole of the arrangement.*
*11/8/52. 74 yds, middle. Same. Cleared after: 3/6. Rain on it two days before I got there, which is what made it worth doing.*
*2/3/53. 88 yds, whole flat, low side. The good gravel under the silt. Cleared after: 4/1, and a letter from the water office, and I answered it.*

**And there it is, eleven years in, in a school exercise book in a kitchen: the cost of the clearing, every time, to the penny. Forty-one entries, because he has had eleven years in a good one and none at all in a bad one, and a man who strips ground only strips it in a season that pays, and eleven times in a season is a fantasy and forty-one in eleven years is a trade.** **Nobody in this basin has ever asked a man for that, because nobody ever came to the door.**

---

**And the reason he did it on the Tuesday evening is that he was in a room on the fifteenth of the month and a woman said a sentence.**

She was a clerk of Hearthward, thirty-one, twenty years in a ledger, and she had written the ninth ward's line in a Council's own form at the request of a warden and carried it in off the street herself, and in the room over a locker's yard, after a district had spent its reach on purpose and the assessor had read a cost out loud before she would draft a line of it, she said:

*You cannot be compensated for a thing you have not got written down, and you cannot get it written down afterwards, and the only time anybody will let you write it down is before you need it.*

**Garrick Dellow has bought the same forty minutes of the same reach eleven times, and he has never once written down what he would do with it, and on the evening of the twenty-ninth he wrote it down, and the sentence that did it was said in a room by a clerk of another district and he heard it from the back.**

He did not do it for the Council. He did not do it for the firm. He did it because he is forty-four, and eleven years is longer than his father's time on the ground was, and because a man who has been doing a thing for eleven years without writing it down has a thing in his head and not in a book, and a thing in your head is gone on a Tuesday.

**And he was also, though he did not put it that way, doing the thing he has wanted to do since the fifteenth and had not been able to start, which is to find out what a salvage man is.**

Because on the fifteenth a woman in a room had asked a room what a reach was worth, and a warden had said *the reach*, and a counter clerk had counted ten parties in a firm's order book and said every one of them is a ward, and an underwriter of twenty-nine had said a number in a yard, and Dellow had been standing at the back in a good coat with his hat in his hand the entire time, **and nobody had asked him anything, and he had eleven years of the answer in a box under his bed and had never once had a reason to open it in front of a person.**

He came in at about seven, put the kettle on, ate nothing, and did not tell anybody he was doing it, and his wife asked him what he was doing with the good paper and he said *figures*, and she said *figures*, and that is the domestic record of the most valuable document in this basin.

---

**Here is the whole of it, and it took him until about eleven o'clock, and it is on two sheets of ordinary paper, and it is in a fair round hand, and it prices a preparation.**

*What forty minutes is worth to me, and what I do with it, being the Bellweather reach at the south end of the freight apron, in the time the water is off the flat.*

*Crew: four men and a foreman. Mine, not the ward's, and the ward's is six with a water steward and that is a different number and I have never once pretended otherwise.*

*Tackle: chain, winch, two bars, the lot's on the flat in a hut and it is roped.*

*Two carts, my own, and a boy on a cart if there's time, and there is not time for a boy.*

*Order of the ground: N end first, always N end first, because if I go in at the S end I put the silt over the copper and then I've got the copper in the cart and the copper in the cart is worth nothing. This is the whole of what forty minutes is for and it is not about the yardage.*

*Spoil goes into the third block's drains and I pay for clearing them after, every time, to the district's man and a cart, and it has been 3/-, 3/6, 4/1 over eleven years and I have written all forty-one of them down.*

*Yardage on a good forty minutes: a hundred and sixty, if the water is off at the top and the chain's hooked at the S bend. Less if it's short. It is a hundred and twenty when it's short and I have had both in one month. Eleven years, forty-one times, a hundred and sixty at the best of it and a hundred and twenty at the worst, and the average of it is about a hundred and forty and I have never once been asked what a reach is worth and I could tell you what my own is worth in a minute.*

*So: forty minutes is worth to me a hundred and sixty yards of the lowest ground in the ninth ward, stripped and carted, with the drains clear by the following Tuesday. At the worst of it, a hundred and twenty. In eleven years, forty-one times.*

*The firm's price is 7/6. My own figure for the work is the clearing, the crew and the tackle, and it is nowhere near 7/6 and it is not supposed to be, because 7/6 is a price for a barge coming and a crew standing off and a hull's hire and I am not buying that, I am not using it. I have never said that to anybody and I have thought about it every time I have paid it.*

*This is the first time I have written any of it down and I have written it because a woman said in a room on the fifteenth that the only time anybody lets you write it down is before you need it, and she is right, and I would not have remembered the sentence if it had not been said.*

— G. Dellow.*

---

**And at about eleven o'clock on the Tuesday night he put the two sheets on the counter in the shed on the south bank, under the chain, on the flat top of a paint drum, and he stood looking at them for a while, and he did not send them to anybody.**

He had thought about it for about twenty minutes and here is the reason in his own words, and he said them to an empty shed, which is where he says most things.

"Because the day somebody asks me for it, it stops being mine."

And then he worked out the rest of it, standing at the gate with the rain coming off the apron roof in a way it does about four times a season, and he worked it out slowly, the way a man works out a thing he has never had to work out.

"There is no claim. A claim wants a sponsor and a sponsor's a ward, and I'm a man with a deed and a bad back and two hundred yards, and the ward's got a solicitor on the Nacre Gate telling it not to spend anything and me standing in front of him saying *I'll wait*." He shifted the papers half an inch. "There's no box in that book of theirs for a fella who isn't a ward. That's not me complaining, that's how it's built and I can see the box from here, it's got a bit over the side for a district and a bit over the other for a company and **there is no bit for a fella who takes copper off low ground for a living and has been doing it since before the war**."

"And there's no form for it, and no bell for it, and no copy of it but the one, and nobody's asked me for it, and the whole thing's in a book I bought off a boy in a shop in the west end for a shilling, and there's a sailing ship on the inside of the front cover."

**He put them on the drum and put the chain across the door and went home, and the two sheets are on that drum this morning and were on it yesterday and will be on it on Friday, and nobody has come up that hill road about them, and there is nobody in this basin who knows they exist including him.**

---

**And that is the whole of it, and it is the most valuable document in this basin, and it is worth nothing.**

It is not a claim: a claim requires a sponsor and a sponsor must be a district, and a freeholder of two hundred yards is not a district, and he knows the difference and has known it since the twenty-first of Sleetmonth, when a warden read a cost out loud in a room in front of nine people.

It is not compellable. A private book in a kitchen is not a record, and if he is ever asked for it in an argument about a breach of contract he will be told to produce it and he will not be able to, and the reason is that it was never entered anywhere and there is no copy of it and there is no bell for it and there is no form.

It cannot be invoiced. **The Council's assessor has opened a temporary compensation ledger and has not been told this exists, and a ledger pays a claimant, and a claimant requires a claim, and this man cannot be a claimant in any of the three definitions in use in this basin, and he would be told so in four minutes by a clerk and he would be correct.**

**And there is no rule requiring anybody ever to report an empty box to the Board, and no rule requiring anybody ever to go and knock on this door, and both of those are on the record and both of those have been on the record for about a month, and neither of them has anybody's name on it.**

---

**At about a quarter past eight on the morning of the thirtieth, a shed foreman of sixty-eight came down eleven steps with a chain over his shoulder and put a kettle on at the end of the bench, because a kettle is the first thing anybody who comes up that hill intends to put on, and putting one on is how a shed foreman says stay as long as you like.**

"Morning."

"Morning."

"You're up early."

"There's a tide on the low side of the sixth this morning and it's the second of them, and the second one is the one I've been waiting on since Michaelmas." Petrie did not look up. He was reading a level book, which he does at six in the morning the way other men read a newspaper, and which is the ninth ward's only continuous record of its own water. "It's got a nine-foot-three on it. There'll be forty tons on that quay wall by about ten if the stone's over the bar, and the stone's over the bar, and the masons are in at seven."

"Warrick's?"

"Warrick's. He's got the chain barge over the bar and the lighters will be loaded by the middle of it." Petrie turned a page. "Then the tide turns and it's gone, and he goes and does it again next month, if there is a next month, and there isn't much of one."

"You'll want to be down there."

"**I shall want to be nowhere near it**," said Petrie, "because I have got a load in this shed that has been here since the sixth of the Bellweather month and a wall in its third season that wants forty tons of stone, and the wall can want what it likes. You'll not get me down there with a chain and a staff and a tide, not at my age, not with three seasons of it on the book."

He shut the book and put it on the bench, and then he said:

"You'll not want to be up there either. The council's bell's been going all morning, and it's the sixth ward's not ours, and there's a man in a room over a chandler's been in there two days."

"Wardening?"

"Lightening." Petrie got the chain over his shoulder. "A lighter's claim. On a tide. I don't know the word for it and I'm not going to guess."
