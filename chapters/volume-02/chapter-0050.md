# Chapter 50 — The Window Market

Kestrel Yards on a cold Tuesday in Sleetmonth, at seven in the morning, with the lake grey and the hills on the far side white, is a place where people are pleased to see each other for about four seconds and then get on with it. There are eleven crews on the Line's name and there are thirty-odd other hulls alongside them working for other people, and a good morning's work in the yards is decided in the first hour and mostly by other people.

The Line's board is on the wall of the yard office, and the Line's board is the most-read object in the western city, and the reason for that is a month old.

It is a slate about four feet by three with a wooden frame, and it is ruled in three columns, and the headings were painted by Nessa Tarrow's hand and are: **LINE'S READING** · **OFFICE** · **DIFFERENCE**. Under the headings is a line for every day since the seventeenth of the Bellweather month. The first column is what the Line's own copper box on its bracket said, with the hour on it. The second is what the Weather Office published. The third is the difference, and the third column is empty on most days and is not empty on fourteen of them, and each time it is not empty it has a date and an hour on it and nothing else, and no excuses, and no column headed whose fault.

Under the slate, on the yard's own board, in the yard's own paint, in Tarin Senn's own hand, are the four conditions.

The first four have been up since the seventeenth of the Bellweather month. They are the ones about the Office: we fly the lane on our own reading, not yours; you publish ours with the hour on it and yours beside it and substitute neither for the other; if the two differ, the difference goes up as a difference, in a column, with the dates; a claim built on the Line's glass names the Line, in the sponsor's own line and not in a footnote.

There is no footnote. That is the entire point of the fourth one and it is the only one of the four that cannot be paraphrased by anybody who was not in the yard.

What the yard had not worked out, and what Ilva Sorn worked out at about ten past seven on this particular Tuesday, is that a rule about *publication* has nothing to say about a rule about *sale*.

---

There was a table outside the chandler's.

It was a trestle, borrowed from somebody who had not been asked, and there was a cloth on it — a good cloth, clean, with a fringe — and there was a printed sheet under a brass weight, and there was a young man behind it in a good coat with a small stove going at his feet and a cup on a saucer, and there were four men in front of it at seven in the morning, and one of them was reading the sheet with his finger on a line.

The sheet was headed:

**CLOUD EXCHANGE — WINDOW LIST — WESTERN DISTRICT AND THE YARDS. Quotation, not forecast. Prices in shillings per window. Hours are the operation's hours, not the sky's.**

And then eleven lines, each of them in the same shape:

**W 1 · Barge movement, Ternway western lane, 4 hrs · from a stated tide · 3/0 · refund at 1/6 if not opened**
**W 2 · Barge movement, Ternway western lane, 6 hrs · from a stated tide · 4/4 · refund at 2/0 if not opened**
**W 3 · Passengers, Kestrel corridor above the yards' mark, 2 hrs · 7/0 · refund 3/6 if not opened**
**W 4 · Passengers, Kestrel corridor, night, 3 hrs · 5/6 · refund 2/6 if not opened**
...and so on to W 11, and under the eleven, in smaller type:

*A window is not a forecast. A window is an undertaking that an operation may be depended upon in the hours named. The Exchange takes the risk. If the operation cannot be performed the price is returned under the refund rule and the withdrawn price is published. Enquiries at the counter.*

Ilva Sorn read the whole thing standing up, twice, and then went and got Tarin.

"Chandler's," she said, when she found her. "There's a table."

"Buyer's table," said Tarin Senn. "I've seen it. It's been there three days."

"You've *seen* it—"

"I saw it Monday. I came in at eight and there was a woman at it with a boy and a slate and I thought it was a girl selling match cards." She put down a spanner. "What is it."

Ilva Sorn told her.

She told it in the flat voice she used for a launch book, because the flat voice was the one that did not get interrupted, and by the end of it Tarin Senn was sitting down on a coil of hawser with her elbows on her knees and looking at the water for about a minute and a half, and then said, "Say the middle part again."

"Which part?"

"The part where he is a man who has been to the yard office and read our board."

"He has. It's a public board. It's the only public board in this yard and everything on it is published under a condition we wrote and agreed to."

"Then say the rest."

Ilva Sorn said the rest.

---

Here is what had happened, and it had happened entirely in public and by nobody's fraud.

A **window** is not a forecast. A forecast says what the sky is going to do, and the sky is not a party to anything and pays nobody. A window says: *this operation may be depended upon in these hours*, for money, with a refund if it cannot be. That is an undertaking about the world, which means somebody has to be prepared to be wrong about the world, and the Cloud Exchange is, and it is the only party in the basin willing to be.

The distinction is not a legal nicety. It is the whole of the product. A forecast that names a place, a time, an effect, and a witness is a **claim**, and a claim is entered in a ledger and struck with a bell, and the Office is the only body in this basin that may enter one, and a claim costs a branch, and every person in this city who has ever read a notice in this city knows what a claim is. A window has no place in it, because a window is a class of hours and not one hour. It has no trigger, because a window does not wait for anybody to accept it; you buy it, you have it. It has no witness, because the only witness in this arrangement is a clerk at a counter who has taken your money.

Which means — and this is the part that the man with the stove would have said in his own words and did not need to, because it is written into the shape of the form — **a window spends nothing and guarantees nothing, and the reason it can be sold at all is that it costs nobody a possibility to be wrong about it.**

"Fine," said Tarin. "So it's a bet with a refund."

"It's a bet with a refund, priced in advance, by people who are not obliged to tell you how they got their price."

"Who is he."

"The Exchange's agent. Ivo Slate. Licensed. He's—"

"I know Slate. He's the one who sat in on the room in the spring and never said a word worth hearing." Tarin turned her head. "Then what's the middle part."

"The middle part," said Ilva Sorn, "is that W 3 and W 4 have been on that list since Monday and both of them say *Kestrel corridor above the yards' mark*, and the corridor is ours, and the mark is ours, and there is not one word on that sheet about the Office and not one word about us, and every number on it is within a quarter of an hour of a number on our board."

Tarin Senn was on her feet.

"That is not a forecast," she said. "That is a *resale*."

"Yes."

"Sell what."

Ilva Sorn had a piece of paper out. She had been up since five and she had done it properly, because that is what she is for.

"The Line's board has been up since the seventeenth of the Bellweather month," she said. "It is on its eightieth day. There are fourteen days on it where the third column is not empty, and on nine of those days the difference was an hour and a half, which is the corridor, and an hour and a half is the largest number on that board and it is the number a barge operator would pay to know."

"And somebody's taking our board and cutting it up."

"Somebody has been taking our board and cutting it up since about the twentieth." She put the paper down. "I've been through it with Nessa. On Monday, the ninth of the month, our reading for the western lane was eleven-twenty and the Office's was nine-forty in the morning, and a difference of an hour and a half. Slate's W 3 was posted at eight-forty on the twenty-second of the month with a price of seven shillings, and the note against it says *from a stated tide*, and and so the buyer of W 3 on the seventeenth got a two-hour advantage for seven shillings and did not need to know where it came from, because it is on the list with a price and a refund and no source."

"So he's selling our name without our name."

"He's selling our *reading* without our name," said Ilva Sorn. "Which is worse, and cheaper, and legal. There is nothing in the world he can be done for. We published it. We published it on a wall, under a condition that says the reading goes up with the hour on it, and that is what we wanted, and he has taken a thing we published and done a thing we did not publish, and the only rule we wrote is about the second half of that sentence."

---

Tarin Senn went and looked at the table.

The young man in the good coat stood up when she came up, which she noted, and offered her the sheet, which she did not take, and said that he was very glad to hear anything the yard wished to say about the list and would take it to the counter.

"I have something to say," she said.

"Then I'd be obliged if you'd say it in front of the sheet, because I have to write it down and I've not got a chair."

So she said it in front of the sheet.

She said it standing at a trestle table in a freezing yard at about twenty to eight in the morning, and it is in nobody's minute, and it was on the Line's board in paint by noon.

"This is our board," she said. "That list is cut out of it. Here is what that means and here is what we do about it, and there are five things and the first four you have already got, because I put them up on this yard's wall in front of you in the Bellweather month and you have honoured every one of them, which I have noticed, and which is more than I can say for the building that is supposed to be regulating the market.

"Five. A window cut from this yard's published figures carries this yard's name on it, and the hour and the date of the line it was cut from, and neither of them can be removed by anybody, including us, including the Exchange, including a court if there ever is one.

"And I will tell you why, and it is not a commercial reason, so you may write that down or not. Because a man buys a window and he does not know what it is made of, and today he is buying it out of our board and he doesn't know that either, and on the day somebody puts a line on your sheet that is *wrong*, and it is wrong because somebody in a shed cut a number off a slate in chalk and did not write the hour — on that day the man who bought it comes to this yard and he will not come to find the man who did it. He will come to find us. And we will have published our own figure with the hour on it and there is no way on this earth to say that is not our figure. So the fifth thing is not for you. It is for the day. It is a name on a number so that when it is wrong there is one place to go and be angry at, and I would rather be that place."

The young man wrote it down. It took him some time.

"That will be referred," he said.

"It will be accepted or it won't. It's five lines and you can put it on the sheet in a quarter of an hour."

"Mr. Slate has said that anything the yard wishes printed on the list is printed, and that the Exchange does not print anybody's name on a window."

"Then your Mr. Slate and I want the same thing," said Tarin Senn, "and he can have it for nothing, and I would like it noted that I said *for nothing* in front of a witness."

---

Ilva Sorn got the number in the end, and she got it the way she got everything, which was by asking a question in a form that made the answer easier than the refusal.

She asked Slate at the counter on the Tuesday afternoon, and she framed it as a bookkeeping question, because it was one.

"I want to know how many windows there are with the yards' mark in the description," she said. "Not the prices. The count. It's for our launch book, because if a barge comes into this yard on a window somebody else sold him, I need to know whether I've got the ground covered and he's flying on nothing."

Slate looked at her for a moment, and then said: "Nineteen."

"Nineteen since Monday."

"Fourteen of them since Friday. Five of them carry the yards' mark in the line, and nine more say *Kestrel corridor* and are cut out of the same place, and there are two on the Ternway list cut out of the second column of your board, which is a different sin, and which nobody has complained about because the people buying them did not know what a second column is."

"Have we complained?"

"You are complaining. To me. Just now." Slate closed the little book. "It is the correct complaint and I have already put it in front of Mr. Hesk, and I should tell you — because you have been extremely fair to me and I do not want to be less fair to you than that — that the answer you have come here to get is no, and I gave it to you this morning in about ninety seconds, and I could have made you wait until Friday."

"Then say why, so that I can write the right thing in the book."

"Because there is nothing in the term under which we are not entitled to read a public board," said Ivo Slate, "and there is no hour in the day when nobody may stand in front of it."

He shut the book and looked at her and, for the first time, said something that was not procedure.

"Your five lines are going on the sheet this afternoon. Have you told Captain Senn that the sixth one you would have got for nothing is that a window cut from your board cannot be sold back to you?"

There was a pause of about two seconds.

"Get out," said Ilva Sorn.

---

The fifth line went up on the list in a hand that was not theirs, in a quarter of an hour, in a yard, in front of four men, and it cost the Cloud Exchange nothing at all, and it is the first thing anybody has got for nothing out of this business in nineteen years.

Under the eleven lines and above the notice, from that afternoon:

*W 1–W 11: where a window is cut from a yard's published figures, that yard's name is printed on it, with the hour and date of the line it was cut from, and neither can be removed by any party. Yards: the Kestrel Line.*

And on the yard's own board, in Tarin Senn's paint, the five conditions. The first four about the Office. The fifth about anybody.

Nobody thought at the time that the fifth one was the reason a market stopped being able to keep a district's weather to itself, because at the time it was about attribution, and it was about attribution for about three weeks, and then it was about something else.

---

On the Thursday the Line's column of differences was eighty-one days old.

The yard's glass does not look at a reach. That is not a fault in it; a copper box on a bracket in the yard is a lane instrument and it is a good one, and it is a lane instrument, and there is no version of a yard's box that tells anybody anything about the level of water in a river a hundred and forty miles south.

But the young man at the trestle table had a second page, and Ilva Sorn saw it on the Wednesday morning and read it and then went and read it a second time and then went and found Tarin Senn and made her read it, standing up, in the cold, at about a quarter past seven.

The second page was headed:

**REACH WINDOWS. Stated level windows, priced by the window. For barge, salvage and pier work. Levels are the Exchange's own. The Exchange has an instrument.**

And there were six lines, and the levels were given in feet above the apron sill with the hour of the ebb, and every one of them was for the Bellweather reach, and the last one said:

**W 4 · BELLWEATHER REACH, apron sill to bottom, 40 min, stated window · 4/0 · refund at 2/0 if not opened · *The reach price is provisional and is revised at each board. The autumn-low figure is 7/6 and is the figure that matters; the provisional figure is on this page because the page went to print on the seventh.*

Beneath, in a smaller type that had clearly been added later and had clearly been added more than once, because it had been corrected:

*Withdrawn quotations are struck through and the price is published. See the refund rule.*

And the Exchange had been struck through exactly once, in pencil, by hand, and the price was in the margin, and it was in pencil too, and it was not a price the Exchange had offered anybody.

**It was the morning of the thirtieth of the Bellweather month. And the reach. And forty minutes.**

"The Office has never quoted a reach in its life," said Tarin Senn, reading it twice. "I asked Malle about it in the autumn. I actually asked him, in a yard, in front of people, and he said there was no instrument in his building that looked at a reach and that there was not going to be one in my lifetime."

"Then somebody's built one."

"Somebody's built one." She handed the page back. "And they've been at it since the sixth of the Bellweather month, because this is a two-page list and page two is printed on better stock than page one, and page two has a line struck through on it in a hand that is not theirs, and page two has been corrected at least twice, and you do not correct a price list twice in the first week of selling it unless the prices are wrong."

Ilva Sorn looked at the yard, at the lake, at the low line of the hills, and at the water on the far side of it doing nothing in particular.

"Who built it?" she said.

"Ask him," said Tarin Senn. "He'll tell you, because he told you the last four things you asked him and he hasn't once made a lie out of it."
