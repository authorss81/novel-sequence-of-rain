# Chapter 62 — The Fifth Question

Ivo Slate had been a licensed agent of the Cloud Exchange for six years and he had built his whole working life on one instruction, which was in his licence and which he had never once had to think about, because a thing you do every day does not need thinking about: **you may quote a product. You may not make a forecast.**

He could stand at a counter in a yard or on a wharf in the rain and take a man's money and put a figure on an operation and an hour on a figure, and he could do it for eleven years without ever once being in a position to be wrong, because a window spends nothing and guarantees nothing and is not a weather.

On the Monday morning of the seventh day of Drownmonth he asked four questions and none of them was about the weather, and it was the first time in eleven years he had had a day in which that was true, and he noticed it about eleven o'clock in the middle of the fourth conversation and then went on asking.

---

**The first was at about eight, to a barge operator who was standing at the end of a counter with a mug in his hand and who had been on the Exchange's books for nine years.**

Slate asked him what he would use the seventeenth's reach window for.

The man said he would hire a lighter off Nacre at a day and a half, put a man and a pump in her, take the reach at slack water on the ebb, and be off by the middle of the afternoon with a hull and a day.

"That is what I would have said," said Slate, "and I want to know if you said it last time."

"I said it last time."

"Same words?"

"Different words." The man drank. "I said, I'll take her down on the morning and I can have her loaded by eleven and be back by three. That's what I'd said. I said the same thing in a different way and I'd have said the same thing in a different way again if you'd asked me tomorrow."

**Nobody had asked him.**

"You were not asked last time?"

"You quoted me a window, son." He put the mug down. "You quoted me seven and six and an hour and a refund and I paid it and I was in the water at twenty past six and I came off at eleven and I paid for a day and a half and I said nothing to nobody, because what was I going to say, I'd have been quoting a price at you."

"You knew the window of the twenty-third was not going to open."

"I did not." The barge operator said it without any embarrassment whatever, and then, after a moment, with a great deal: "I knew the band was on it. I had a glass on my own bow and I had been in that reach in March and I knew what a band does to a morning. I did not know the water office's own mark was going to be under mud at seven in the morning, and that's not the same thing and I was paid a refund on the Friday so I wasn't out of pocket and I have never once gone back to look at whether the water came down on the twenty-third." He picked the mug up. "It didn't, did it."

"It did not," said Slate.

"Right," said the barge operator, and drank, and went away, and was not suspicious of anybody and was not disappointed and was not going to remember the conversation.

---

**The second was at about half past nine, to a chandler who had a trestle table outside his shop and a stove and a printed list, and who was the Exchange's own retail counter on a wet day.**

Slate asked him what he thought the district would do about the low lane on Thursday.

The chandler said, "Nothing. They always do nothing on the low lane, that's the whole of the ninth ward, it's a place where people do nothing very carefully and then complain about a pump."

"That is a schedule of responses," said Slate.

"It's a joke, son."

"It is not entirely a joke and I want you to notice that you have just said something to me that is on the form. **What the ward can be relied upon to have and to be able to do.** You said: a barrow, a warden, a bad back, a pump house with a hand on it, and forty people who will not leave. I did not ask you a single question to get it. You have just supplied a district's available response to a season's water, from behind a counter, in about nine seconds, for nothing, about a district you are not in."

The chandler put the list straight.

"Then what are they going to pay me for."

"That," said Slate, "is the best question anybody has asked me this year and I have not got an answer to it either."

---

**The third was at about ten, in a doorway, to a carrier's clerk of about thirty who had been eating a bun and who had a book of his own that was not an Exchange book.**

Slate asked him how the yard on the Kestrel side decided on Saturday morning what to load.

The clerk said, "Tarin tells us. That's it, that's the whole system, and I have stopped finding it funny."

"What does she look at?"

"Her own board. Two columns, her figures and the Office's, and if they differ there's a third column with the difference and the hours, and it's been on that board since the autumn and it's been on it every day and I'd say she'd be written out of this city if she took it down." He wiped his hands. "And if they differ and she's wrong, there's a line in the third column that says the yard was wrong, in the yard's own hand, with the hour on it. Every time. Twice now. Twice the yard's come out wrong and put it up itself."

"How many times has the Exchange's sheet been wrong in the same period?"

"Never," said the clerk. "Once. It printed a reach figure for two days running off a reading from the wrong end of the drum, and they printed the correction themselves, in their own column, on their own sheet, with the date, and nobody made them do it." He looked at Slate. "Why are you writing all this down?"

"I'm not," said Slate. He had a card out and he was not writing on it. "I do this every day. Ask, write it down, read it back, put the date on it, file it. Six years. I have four hundred and six of them."

"For what?"

"For the agency file." Slate put the card away. "I have never in six years used one in a room."

---

**The fourth was at eleven, to a man from the works who came in on the Exchange's business and sat down without being asked, and who Slate had spoken to perhaps nine times in six years and had learned nothing from on any of the nine.**

Slate asked him what would be on the reach on the seventeenth, which is the season's second back-cut and the biggest of it, and which is ten days off.

The man said, "There'll be a reach and a half-day of it if the band's gone by Tuesday, and there'll be four inches of a nothing if it isn't, and I know which of those because I have a mark on a post at the water office and four inches of mud over it, same as every autumn, and it is not a glass and it is not a sequence and it is a man with a chain, and I read it on the last day of every month and I write it in a book that nobody has ever asked to see, and I shall read it on the last day of this month, and the front of it will be on that wall in about a week whatever I do."

**Which is the fifth question and the best one, and it came out of a chair.**

Slate put his own card down on the desk.

"That," he said, "is a schedule of what you can be relied upon to have and to be able to do. Date, place, interval, and a method. It is the exact four things a ward is asked for on a form that has been sitting in a press on the ground floor of the Council for two hundred and forty years, and it is a *job*, and you did it as a matter of course, for a book, for a rate of pay, and you have never in your life been asked for it by anybody, and the man who asks you for it gets it for nothing because you would be flattered."

The man from the works looked at the card.

"Would I."

"You would tell me, and I would write it down, and I would have to pay you the ordinary price, and you would take the money and be embarrassed about it."

"Yes," said the man from the works. "I would."

---

**And then at about eleven o'clock, in the middle of that, Slate noticed something and it took him about nine seconds and then he never forgot it.**

He had asked four questions on a Monday morning. Four people had answered all four. And not one of the four questions had been about the weather.

He had asked what a man would *use* an hour for. He had asked what a district would *do* about a lane. He had asked how a yard *decided*. He had asked what a man *knew* about a reach.

Not one of them was: what is the water going to do.

**And the reason is not that the people of this basin are reticent about the weather. The reason is that all four of them already agree about the weather without being asked, and the agreement is the product, and the window is the receipt.**

Four unrelated men, a barge operator, a chandler, a carrier's clerk and a works clerk, had between them described this basin's entire behaviour in a wet quarter, in a language nobody had published, and had done it for nothing, and would have gone on doing it for nothing.

A firm that stops asking stops knowing anything on a Monday. A firm that starts asking a district anything is a firm that can be refused by a district. And a firm that can be refused is a firm that has to be able to afford being refused, at a rate, on a Tuesday, in an ordinary way, without a fuss.

That is what the Exchange had spent ten years building. And it could not be built in one week and it could not be sold, and the only man on the floor who had worked it out by eleven o'clock on a Monday morning was a licensed agent with four hundred and six agency reports in a file and no standing to raise it.

---

He wrote two things that afternoon.

**The first was a memorandum to the firm, filed in the ordinary way, in the ordinary form, in the ordinary hand, with the date on it.**

*The Monday question is a question about people and not about glass. We have stopped doing it. The day we started paying for readings instead we stopped asking the only people who answer for free. Four conversations this morning, all four returned a schedule of a party without being asked for one. None of it was obtained in a way that could be produced. I do not know what it is worth and I have not put a figure on it and I would ask that nobody put one on it either.*

**The second was a letter to the ninth ward, and it was the harder of the two, and he drafted it in his own words and did not show it to anybody for two days, and it is nearly twice as long as a letter asking for a schedule has any business being.**

It began, *Sir or Madam*, and it asked the ward what its available responses to a season's water were, in the form the Council used, and it said that the Exchange would pay the ordinary price, and it said that a refusal would be recorded as a refusal.

And then, about halfway down the second page, it said the thing that made it a letter and not a form, and he put it in without any idea at all that he had done anything, and Hesk read it and made him leave it in:

*We are aware that your ward's own quarterly return of the second of Sleetmonth is the only schedule of your water in the world and that we hold it, having paid eleven shillings for it, and we are aware that you have never been asked to fill in anything and that nothing you have been asked for has had room in it. We are therefore not asking you for your schedule. We are asking you whether you will tell us, in your own words, on one sheet, what your ward can do about a season's water; and if the answer is that you have nothing, then the answer is that you have nothing, and we will record that, in our own hands, with the date, and we will pay nothing for it.*

Hesk read it on the Monday afternoon and read it again on the Monday evening and signed it in the second paragraph of the last page with his own name and no second name under it and sent it out on the Tuesday morning of the eighth of Drownmonth.

"It is twice as long as it wants to be," he said.

"It's twice as long as it has to be," said Slate.

"The rule says we don't put our commercial question on a district's return."

"The rule says we don't put our commercial question on a *return*." Slate stood there with his card in his hand. "That's a different instrument, sir. A return is a form a district fills in for a public body and signs and owes. A letter is a thing you send a man. You may not answer a return and you may answer a letter. That is the whole difference and it is the only difference and it is worth nineteen years of the firm."

"It is also, in about four months, a firm's ability to be shown anything at all."

"Yes," said Ivo Slate. "It is, and I have written that down, and the memorandum is in the file, and the memorandum says we stopped asking, and I have put my name on it, and I would like it observed that I am not resigning over it and I am not a man with a grievance. I noticed something on a Monday. I wrote it down. That is my job and it is the only thing about my job that is worth anything."

---

**Four days later, which is the Friday of the eleventh of Drownmonth, the Exchange received an answer, and the answer is one of the four or five best documents produced in Veyra this winter,** and it is in a hand that has been used every working day of its author's life for twenty-two years, and it is a refusal, and it is correct.**

It came in the ordinary post to the second floor and it was addressed to a partner and not to an agent, because the letter had been signed by a partner.

It is worth setting down in full, because almost everything that has happened in this basin since the twenty-first of Sleetmonth is downstream of it.

*Mr. Hesk,*

*Thank you for your letter of the fourth and for the second one, which I am told also came from you, and for putting on the face of the first that we might refuse.*

*We refuse.*

*You ask what this ward can do about a season's water. The honest answer is that we can tell you what we did about a season's water, and we have, and you have it, and it cost us eleven shillings and it was not mine to sell.*

*The ninth ward holds six catch pits, a barrow, two wardens, a water steward, a pump house with a hand on it and a pump house on a call. It can get nine men to a flat by nine in the morning and not nine by six. It has a shed with a load in it that has been in the shed since the sixth of the Bellweather month and the load is paid for and the crew is on our books and the wall it is for has now gone two seasons without being worked on, and there is a table at the Council's basin house that says a front of this order returns in two years and about eleven months and it is a Friday.*

*That is the whole of our available response to a season's water and I have given it to you now because you asked for it in a letter and not in a form, and because you said on the face of it that a refusal would be recorded as a refusal.*

*I furnished a return to the Council two seasons back in answer to a spring form, in a fair hand, and it was stamped, and it is not in the register, and it is not in this ward, and the reason it is in neither place is that nobody asked us for anything that had room in it. There is nothing in the Council's book about this ward and there is nothing in yours that is not ours, and I have looked for a way to give you a schedule of this ward and there is not one, and I would rather tell you that in a letter than have you go and look for it and find that I sold it to you already.*

*There is one more thing and then I will sign.*

*Three weeks ago this ward closed a window of its own on purpose, in writing, with a form and a bell, so that a man who had already paid for it could not get at the water, and it cost us a wall and a year, and there is no column in the world that says what it cost, and there is not going to be one. I am not going to write to the firm that sold it.*

*You will pay nothing. I have kept your letter. You may produce it to anybody, including me, including this Council, including a court, and it is a true letter and it says what you did and not what you meant.*

*N. Kade, Storm Warden, ninth ward.*

There were four things in the Exchange's second-floor correspondence file that week that the firm had not had before.

**A letter from a partner asking a district a question and offering it the right to refuse. A letter from a district refusing and being right. A memorandum from a licensed agent stating that the firm had stopped asking and did not know what it was worth. And a form from the Council's technical cell with four lines of fair hand at the top of it and nothing whatever underneath.**

Three of them were about asking, and one of them was a silence, and the silence was the oldest of the four.
