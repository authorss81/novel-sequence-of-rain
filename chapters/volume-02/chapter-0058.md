# Chapter 58 — The Reach Does Not Come Down

The ward ledger bench is on the ground floor of the Weatherglass, at the end of the records room, under the north wall, and it is a bench with a rail on it and a hood over it and a bell on a bracket about four feet above the rail, and it is the oldest working thing in the building.

Nobody in this basin is quite sure what the bell is for. It is not the certification bell, which is a hand bell and lives in a case in the technical cell and is rung once by an assessor. It is not a fire bell. It is a brass hand bell about five inches over with a bracket on it and it has been on that wall for longer than the building's accounts go back, and it is struck when a claim goes on the rail, once for every ward the claim touches, and the ward's head on the wall above the rail lights for the length of the strike and then goes out.

The ninth ward's head has not lit since the middle of the autumn before last.

---

At four minutes to ten on the Saturday morning of the twenty-first day of Sleetmonth, the claim was on the rail.

It is a piece of foolscap on the Office's own printed form, and the form is the same one that has been used for two hundred years and it has six boxes and a seventh box that a District fills in and a bell bracket, and it is not a long document. It took the ward clerk about nine minutes to write it and she wrote it in one hand without lifting the pen, and Marek Vale stood on the other side of the rail and watched her do it and did not once offer to help.

*Place: the Bellweather reach at the south-bank freight apron, and the ninth ward's low ground, its drains and its ground floors.*

*Trigger: the ward's clearing rota run, and its first marked depth returned to the ward ledger before the second watch on the twenty-third.*

*Effect, as entered: the reach does not come down to the quoted mark within the quoted hours.*

*Listed alternative, as required: if the quotation for the morning of the twenty-third is withdrawn before the entry bell, no claim binds; the reach behaves as the weather decides; the district keeps its window; and the district is answerable to the people it did not move.*

*Expiry: the end of the quoted hours on the twenty-third, being the same line as the effect.*

*Sponsor: N. Kade, Storm Warden, ninth ward. Concurring seat: the ninth ward.*

*Attestation: a reader's attestation of the order of the movements only — M. Vale, reader, not on field duty, not in this office's service.*

And then the seventh box, which is the box a District fills in, and which is headed **TURNS DISPLACED**, and which has been on the form since a clause was adopted at the end of the Bellweather month and which had never been filled in by anybody in the world before this morning.

The clerk wrote it in the ward's own words, about ninety of them, and read them back, and did not enjoy any part of it and did not editorialise and did not look up while she wrote.

*Reaches come down about nine days a year. This morning's is the first. The water office's table says the front returns under four years; it was three years at the end of the Bellweather month. The load has been in the south-bank shed since the sixth and it is paid for and the pump is on a guarantee and the wall is in the reach. A crew is nine men. The ward's own return says the third block runs three days in the season with a private gang in the drains, and the Exchange's man said that out loud in this room on Friday and it is true and it is the reason for this claim. THE WARD IS CLOSING ITS OWN REPAIR WINDOW TO STOP A SECOND SALE OF IT. That is the cost and it is not money.*

The ward's ninth seat concurred at about half past eleven on the Friday night, in writing, in one line, and the clause came down off the ridge with the certification on it, and Sabin Orle had initialled the page with the limitation on the reader in it and had not initialled the page with the limitation on the reader as a *finding*.

Marek Vale signed the attestation at ten past ten.

It is worth being exact about what that signature was, because it is the only attestation he has given in seven weeks and there is a great deal riding on it.

He is a reader. He is not on this office's watch lists, its field lists or its rosters, and he is not suspended from the benches, and he is not a public officer and holds no office in this building. He is therefore, on Saturday morning, the twenty-first of a month, a private person standing on a floor.

An attestation is a reader's act. A reader's act is not an act of this office unless it is done in this office's service. He attested the **order of the movements only** — Gather, then Weight not finishing, then Break on top of it, then Route twice, then Release, then Witness — and then the one fact that is not a movement at all and has no name in any office in this basin and is called a reach only by the men who work on one: that the Release of the twenty-third carries a bend in it, and that a bend is the only place on that day a loaded flat could have gone. He attested the order. He did not state an hour, because an hour would have been a forecast, and he has been censured once already for making an assertion he could not support and he has no intention of doing it again in any building.

The clerk struck the bell.

It came on no instrument in this building, because there is no instrument in this building that will say a thing like that out loud, and it did not say which possibility was going. It stood on the claim sheet, in the middle of the sixth box, in the fair hand of a district clerk who has never seen one before and did not write one, and Marek Vale read it twice with his finger on the rail, and put it in his coat pocket on a slip of bench paper in his own hand, the way he has put six things in that pocket since the eighth of the Bellweather month, and it is the first time in his working life that it has come to paper and he has no idea at all what it is made of, and it is a piece of paper with no chain behind it and it is the only reason he will ever be able to say what this claim cost anybody, and it is a piece of paper about a claim he did not make, by a ward he has met twice, on a form he did not write.

Marek could not hear the bell. He is on the same side of the rail as the clerk and the bell is four feet above the rail and the room has a stone floor and there are nineteen people in it and a press going somewhere, and he got nothing. He saw the clerk's wrist go and he saw the sound arrive in the rest of the room about a fifth of a second late, in people's shoulders, and he did not ask her to strike it again, and she would not have.

The ninth ward's head on the wall above the rail did not light.

It has not lit since the autumn, and it did not light on the thirtieth of the Bellweather month when a claim touching that ward's own floodplain went into the world, and it did not light now. There is a bubbled temporary in a brass frame where a pane ought to be, and the brass is very good work, and a four hundred and forty people live on a low ground and none of them can see this from the street.

**The claim bound anyway.** That is worth being exact about, because everything that follows happens because of it, and it is a fact about bells and witnesses and not about glass. The bell is struck in a room with nineteen people in it and a reader in it and a clerk in it and a sheet of foolscap on a rail with six boxes filled in. The glass on the wall is not the witness. The glass on the wall is a notice, and a notice is a thing a ward is *told*, and a ward cannot be bound by a thing it is told.

He has thought about that for a very long time and has not got past it and is not going to.

At about a quarter past eleven on the Saturday morning, in the west end of the records room, a copy clerk made a true copy.

She is twenty-seven and she has been on a copy desk since twenty past two on the afternoon of the third day of the Bellweather month, and the demotion is permanent, and the glass-tender's examination is in the spring and is held by somebody else and she will not sit it, and nobody in this building has thanked her.

It is in her job. Every claim entered at that rail gets a true copy in the minutes of the entry, made in the hand of a copy clerk, dated, timed and initialled, and it goes into the tray and the tray goes to the technical cell, and it is the only place in the building where the words on the sheet are recorded as they were written rather than as they were classified.

She did it in eleven minutes. She did not do it quickly; she has never in her life done anything quickly. She wrote out the place, and the trigger, and the effect, and the alternative word for word, and the expiry, and the sponsor, and the attestation, and then she came to the seventh box and she read it out to herself, silently, from the rail, twice, and then she wrote it out word for word, in her own hand, and initialled the sheet, and dated it, and timed it at eleven twenty-six, and it is the longest of the seven items and it is in capitals in one place and she copied the capitals.

Nobody asked her to. Nobody in that room knew there was anything on that rail except a claim. Nobody in this basin knows that a true copy of a claim sheet is made by a specific pair of hands on a specific Saturday morning, and that this one went into a tray, and that the tray went down to the technical cell, and that a tray is the safest place a piece of paper can be in this building.

She put the sheet in the tray. She put the other two copies on top of it. She wrote *entered 21st, Sleetmonth, 11:26* on the tray card in her own hand because that is what is done.

And she did not offer it to anybody, and nobody thought to ask, and there were two hundred feet of records room between her and the rail and a stone floor under both.

He went to see a counter-reader on the Saturday afternoon because there was no other version of the twenty-third that worked.

She had a room over a drying floor above the Ninth Baths and she had four notebooks and a strip of sequence glass on a bench that she had bought rather than cut, and she was thirty-four and she had published a corridor and an hour and no place for two years running and had been refused four times by people who were correct, and she had started a fourth notebook headed *Declined. Who. Why, in their words. Date.*

"Eleven shillings," she said, when he told her what the ward had voted.

He had not expected her to price it and he was wrong.

"I know what you're doing," she said. "You're doing the thing where you tell me it's for the ward and you don't tell me it's a favour, and I have to work out which. So here it is, priced. Eleven shillings is what the ward sold the same information for, on the second of the month, to a firm that publishes it twice a day for nothing. I am not dearer than that and I am not cheaper, and the reason I am not cheaper is that the ward's sheet was true and mine is true and neither of them is any good without somebody standing at the end of it."

"And the term."

"There is a term, and it is not mine, and you will recognise it when I say it." She wrote on a slip and pushed it across. "Sable Orin. Attestation of the order only, same as yours. I have read the reach out of the ninth ward's own feed and out of the Exchange's level, and the two do not agree, because one of them is a number and the other one is a turn. The bend is on the twenty-third, in the third watch, and it is where the Exchange's mark will not be."

"**The reading goes on a board a warden is welcome to read, free, and I do not pass it to anybody who has to pay for it.**"

She said it in Neris Kade's words and she said it without looking at the slip, and Marek understood that the woman across the bench from him had copied a sentence out of a letter to a yard and had not touched the copy since.

Marek Vale sat on a bench in a room over a drying floor above the Ninth Baths and could not remember the last time anybody had improved on a thing Neris Kade had written in a letter to a yard in the Bellweather month.

"It is a warden's term. I have adopted it and I am going to be insufferable about it." She tore the slip in half. "It goes up on a shutter at the district office by noon on the twenty-second and the district's own women have four hands and will copy it if somebody gives them something to copy, and after that it goes on the board at the Sump Lane school at eight on the morning, and the school opens at nine, and a reading that is up at eight is read by forty children walking up from the low blocks before the day starts. And the Exchange can have it. Give it to them. The Exchange pays eleven shillings for the same truth twice a day, and I want it to have the exchange's name on it as well, and I do not care in the slightest."

**The back-cut came on the morning of the twenty-third, and it came, and the reach did not go down.**

It cleared on the Saturday night, hard, from the north-west, which is the wrong quarter entirely and is why nobody in this basin can price it in advance, and by Sunday morning the sky over the upper basin was the clean empty cold blue that the old men will tell you is the most dangerous thing there is. The upper reservoir dropped nine inches in nine hours. The western draw backed off the ridge and came round southerly, which put a long band over the whole basin and held it, and a long band is a fixed pressure gradient with no bend in it at all.

The reach came down four inches by the Sunday evening and then stopped.

The post at the turn of the causeway has a mark cut round it at knee height, weathered, put there by men who are dead, and on the twenty-third it was under six inches of mud and the mud was not going anywhere. **W. Farrow** is a decent man who reads a post when he is standing at one, and he was standing at that one on the twenty-third with his chain and his staff, and the reading was six inches and the mark was there and the two did not agree, and he wrote the disagreement in the shed's own level book in the correct column and went off up the hill and has no idea.

Petrie had nine men in the yard at nine o'clock on the Monday morning and a gang of eleven on the books at the contractor's and a foreman from the works waiting on a cart, and at about eleven he put the kettle on, because the mark was not going to show.

"Nothing's coming off," said Doak, the municipal apron man, who had been on that apron since five. "There's a band on it. That's all. The band'll be there till the band's done."

Garrick Dellow's barge came up the reach at about half past ten, tied against the apron wall, and did not discharge. Dellow came up in a hired lighter at about one and stood on the apron for two hours in a good coat, and was completely silent, and went back down at about three.

In Low Spindle the second pump house ran all night and all day and the low lane ran and about an inch came into the third block's low side and went out again by the Tuesday, and no ground floor was wet anywhere, and two wardens, a barrow, six catch pits and two grates cleared on the rota, and the ninth ward had a better Monday morning than it has had in this basin for four years, and there is no column anywhere in the world for that.

**And about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands, and a replacement dewatering pump at four hundred and ten on a pallet, stayed where they have been since the sixth of the Bellweather month, in a shed, under a painted board, dry, with the pump's cover that has been on since the eleventh of the Bellweather month, and the wall at the Lower Sill went into its second consecutive season of not being worked on, and three years became two years and about eleven months, which is a Friday, and it is not a prophecy, it is a table on a wall at the Weatherglass.**

The Exchange struck the line through on the Monday at about eleven.

Under its own rule, on its own wall, in the middle of the day, in public, with the price in the margin in a fair hand, and by the middle of the afternoon there were nineteen people standing in front of it, and a barge operator from the west side of the city was saying, in a voice of great composure, that a window is a window, and that it does not matter what the sky does, and that the refund rule exists precisely so that a man who cannot get at the water is not the man who loses.

"Seven and six," said somebody. "That's the quote. The refund's three and nine and it says so on the wall, and it was paid before the end of the day, and that is what the rule *is*, and I have got my money and I am not complaining."

"That's what the rule is," said Edda Tarn, from behind the counter, with her hands flat on the cloth. "And the rule does not say the second one is the same as the first one, and we have never claimed it was, and I would like everybody in this room to notice what has happened to that wall this month. The last reach line on it was struck in the days after the storm with two shillings in the margin, and the two shillings were put there by a partner in a hurry because a young man was on the floor and it had to be published by Friday, and nobody in this building has ever defended those two shillings because we did not have anything to defend them with. And there have been two lines struck on that wall since, both of them in public, both with a price against them, both in a room this firm rents." She took her hands off the cloth. "We cannot keep a product like this in front of the public and be the only party in the basin that will not say what a missed window was worth."

And then the thing that made the week.

"Doak," said somebody.

"Sorry?"

"The apron's a public apron. The depot's a district depot. Is there a chain on the gate."

"There's a chain on the gate. It's got a chain's worth of slack in it and the padlock's been broken since the spring."

"Then the apron's open and the depot's open and the load's sitting there in the open on a pallet, and there's nine hundred pounds of timber in four bands and four hundred and ten pounds of pump on that apron, on a Monday night, with no shed, no chain and no watchman, and the water office ordered it and the district is the custodian and this apron is a public apron." He put his hat on. "I'll have the district's men put a tarpaulin over it and rope the frame and I'll leave four men on it tonight and the same four tomorrow, and I'll put it in the returns as a district watch on a public apron, and I do not want the word *tarp* anywhere near my return."

"Then it was nothing to do with the water at all," said the barge operator, with enormous satisfaction. "It was nothing to do with the weather. It was a shed with a chain on it. It was on the eleventh, wasn't it, the cover went on the eleventh, and there was a board painted on the door in the autumn and nobody's read it since and the load's been out in the open since the sixth and it's forty foot from a river."

Nobody answered him, because there was nothing to answer, because he was completely right, and the Exchange's rule had been a beautifully made instrument for pricing the weather and had not one single word in it about a chain on a gate.

The twenty-first was a Saturday and the twenty-third was a Monday, and the notice came on the Wednesday.

It came at about ten in the morning to the district office on Tannery Walk, and it was not served by a process server. It was brought by a man who had come down the hill on a carrier's bicycle and who was about thirty and who had been Dellow's clerk for six years and who stood in the doorway of a small office above a locker's yard and held a folded sheet and did not come further in, and who said, before anybody asked him, that he had come himself because there was a man in the firm who was owed an apology and he was not that man, and that he had a train at two.

It was four paragraphs and it was polite, and the fourth paragraph is the one that took the room.

**THIRD. WHERE A WINDOW SOLD BY THE FIRM IS WITHDRAWN BY THE ACT OF A THIRD PARTY, THE BUYER'S REMEDY IS LIMITED TO CANCELLATION OF THE CONTRACT AND TO THE SUM PAID THEREUNDER, SAVE WHERE THE THIRD PARTY'S ACT IS A FORCE MAJEURE EVENT AS DEFINED AT CLAUSE 9, THAT IS TO SAY AN ACT OF GOD, THE KING'S ENEMY, RIOT, OR THE DIRECTION OF A PUBLIC AUTHORITY.**

"Read it again," said Gant Ollis.

"Read it again," said the man, patiently, and read it again.

Nobody in that room said anything for about nine seconds.

Then Neris Kade said, in a voice nobody on Tannery Walk had ever heard her use, and she did not shout it and it went to the back of the room perfectly clearly:

"Define it. Define *force majeure*. Define it out loud, in this room, to me."

"An act of God," said the man. "Or the direction of a public authority. And I am instructed to say that the firm does not say which of those two it considers this to be. I am instructed to say the firm has considered both and declines to say, and that the firm would rather not be asked again in writing."

"Because if it is an act of God, nobody owes this ward a penny." Gant Ollis had his hand flat on the table. "The rain did not come down. The rain was *held up*. That is the opposite of an act of God. That is a thing somebody did, in a room, with a form and six boxes and a bell on a wall, and if the firm says it is a thing somebody did then this ward is the thing's somebody, and there is no doubt about that in this basin and there is not going to be any doubt about it in a month."

"They have known since the call on the Saturday morning," said the man. "Mrs. Tarn took the ward's telephone call at about eleven and she was in the room when she took it, and I am instructed to say that she said the firm would take advice, and I am instructed to say that she said it in front of four people."

Marek Vale said: "The bell was struck at ten past ten on the Saturday morning. In a room with nineteen people in it and a form on a rail and a reader and a clerk, and there is no instrument in that room that will say one word about what happened, and there never has been, and the only account of it in the world is a sheet of foolscap with a bell's worth of sound on it, and the sheet has no chain behind it, and I put my name under the attestation in my own hand and I am going to be asked about that in about a month by a man who is going to be extremely fair about it."

The man on the bicycle looked at him.

"I'm a clerk," he said. "I've been a clerk six years and I take my fee from him and not from them, and I have not got an opinion about any of it and I'm not going to have one on a doorstep."

**What came back to the ninth ward on the twenty-eighth of Sleetmonth was a question, and it is worth setting down exactly, because almost everything that follows is a consequence of it.**

Dellow had bought the window of the twenty-third for seven and six. The firm had sold it to him. The firm had then been the instrument of a claim that made the window unopenable, and clause 3 of a contract between a firm and a buyer does not contemplate a third party, and the buyer's remedy was therefore against the firm.

So the firm paid its own buyer. Seven and six for the window, and five and six for a barge and a gang hired to a day and stood off a reach for a day and a half, and the standing costs were proved and were not extravagant, and the total was **thirteen shillings**, and it was paid on the twenty-eighth of Sleetmonth, in full, without argument, on a printed receipt.

Then the firm did the thing that its clerk in a good coat had told it to do on the Saturday morning and had declined to say in writing, which was to look at clause 9 of the same contract, which the firm had written itself, and to conclude that a claim is not weather.

And the firm sent the ward two things on the same day.

**The first was a letter, and it was not a bill, and the ward is owed nothing by it and can be owed nothing by it.** The ninth ward did not buy a window. The ninth ward declined a window, then bound a claim that the window could not be opened, and then stood still. There is no contract between the firm and the ninth ward. There is no purchase, no price, and no sum paid, and a firm that cannot identify a claimant cannot pay a claimant, and it said so in one paragraph without any particular coldness, and the paragraph ends with the words *we do not consider that any sum is due to the ward or from it.*

**The second was a copy of clause 9, and a note in one line.** The note said: *The firm asks the ninth ward whether it contends that a certified claim is a direction of a public authority within the meaning of this clause. The firm has not advised on the question and will not.*

Nobody in the ninth ward could answer it, and the reason nobody in the ninth ward could answer it is that it is a legal question and the ninth ward has a warden, a copy clerk, a tower keeper, a chair of twenty-two years' standing and a man who sweeps Tannery Walk, and it has a solicitor on the Nacre Gate, and the solicitor's letter of the second of Drownmonth is one paragraph and says that the question has never been tested, that the firm is not likely to press it, and that the ward should not spend anything on it.

**The Exchange's letter of the twenty-eighth also had a postscript, and the postscript was not a lawyer's, because lawyers do not write postscripts.**

It said that the firm disputed nothing about its own buyer. It said that the firm had paid. It said that the firm had no claim against the ward, that it had considered one, and that it had decided against it, and that it wished the ward to know that it had taken advice on the question and had not been told to take it. And it said that the firm would like it noticed that it had never before declined a claim it was in a position to bring, that it did not intend to establish a practice, and that any further correspondence on the same matter would be referred to counsel.

Marek read that postscript four times.

On the second of Drownmonth he sat on the end of a bench in a room at the Weatherglass's north end that is a waiting room and has a chair in it and a view out of a window of the eastern ridge and nothing else, and worked out why, and it took him four readings and both of the first two were wrong.

They had not done it out of kindness and they had not done it out of guilt. They had done it because the alternative was a hearing, and a hearing produces a *finding*, and a finding is a thing that can be cited, and a firm with one adverse finding in one ward has that finding on the wall of every underwriter in the Ninefold Basin by the spring. Thirteen shillings bought the absence of a finding. It is the same trade, at a very much smaller size, that this Council has been running for two hundred years, and it is the same trade his own signature was in front of at ten past ten on the Saturday morning on a form in a fair hand.

And the second reading was worse. In seven weeks the ninth ward had gone from a district that could not show anybody what its rain was worth to a district that had been asked in writing what its rain was worth, and had answered, accurately, in a fair hand, and been told — by a firm, politely, in a printed letter, with reasons — that it was owed nothing and that it owed nothing and that both of those were correct.

**The district that lost forty minutes at the bottom of the Bellweather reach on purpose, at a cost of nine hundred pounds of timber and four hundred and ten pounds of pump and nine men and a wall, is the only party in this transaction that cannot put a figure on what it lost, and the reason is that it never bought anything, and there is no form for a thing you did not buy.**

**Drownmonth is the second month of the wet quarter, and it is named for what it does. A Veyran who has lived here a year has heard the word used as a joke. A man who has lived here thirty years has not.**

**Tovan Iles made a second true copy on the Wednesday morning, at about nine minutes past ten, and she was not asked to and there was no reason on earth for her to.**

A claim has been entered; the minutes of the entry are already made; the card on the tray says *entered 21st, Sleetmonth, 11:26*; and that is the end of the procedure, and the procedure is correct, and always has been.

What she did was go and get the original. It stays on the rail for ninety days, because a rail is a physical rail and the sheet is pinned to it and the pin is a pin. She took the pin out with a paper knife, which is not a thing anybody does, and carried the sheet to her desk at the west end of the records room, and read the seventh box again, and it was the same ninety words, and then she copied it out, in her own hand, dated, timed and initialled, and put it in her own box with the other one, and locked it, and put the key back in her pocket.

The key has been in that pocket since the third day of the Bellweather month.

There are two things in that box. The first is a true copy of the thirtieth's day sheet as it stood before the classification was changed. The second is a true copy of a ward's own words about a window. **They are the only two documents in this basin that say, on the same two months, that a cost can be written down in a fair hand and be worth nothing to anybody.** And there is no form in the Charter of Public Notice, or in this building, or in this basin, under which a copy clerk's private copy of anything may be produced to anybody, and she is a copy clerk, and the demotion is permanent, and the glass-tender's examination is in the spring and is held by somebody else and she will not sit it.

She wrote the tray card in her own hand, as she always does: *entered 21st, Sleetmonth. Rail copy returned, 2nd, Drownmonth, 10:09.*

Which is true. Which is the whole of what she has.

Neris Kade went to the Council's technical cell on the Wednesday afternoon with a ruled page in her coat and a figure in her own hand, and she did not get what she went for.

That is the whole of the last part of it and it is worth being exact about, because for four years she has believed that the reason a district's rain is not worth anything is that nobody in this basin can say what it was worth, and she went into a room on the second afternoon of Drownmonth with the price of forty minutes worked out on the back of a form and found out that the problem is the other end of it.

The technical cell keeps, in a press on the ground floor, the **Standing Response Register.** It is a bound volume a foot and a half thick and it is not a record of anything; it is a form, filled in by hand, once a season, for two hundred and forty years, by whichever clerk of the ward's district was sent. It has a column for every one of the nine wards and a line for every season, and against each line the ward is to set out **its available responses to a season's water** — what crews it holds, what it can put on a reach or a canal or a roof, what it can be relied upon to have and to be able to do, in a season, with a date against it.

Marek was not in that room. He was in the corridor, because he is a censured reader and there is nothing in a technical cell he has any business in, and Neris went in alone with a list and came out with a face he had seen on other people and never on her.

"Ninth," she said, when she got him. "I asked for the ninth. Two seasons back and the one before that. He did not go and look. He did not go to a press and count along. He turned round to the press and he put two fingers on a line and said, *that's the ninth, that's ours, and there's nothing under it, and there's been nothing under it a long while.*"

"How long?"

"He looked. Properly looked. Then he stopped looking and looked at me instead and said the thing." She turned her collar up against nothing. "He said, *That'll be the four-and-fiftieth, maybe. There was a fella wanted it done properly and then he went, and after that nobody's wanted it, and I couldn't tell you the year if you asked me.* And I asked him what the line underneath it said, and he said, *Nothing, there's never been anything under it. It's a form, that's the whole of it, you put a season in and you put whatever the district's got, and if the district's got nothing on the day then there's nothing, and there's no rule says there's got to be something.*"

"And the seasons after it?"

"Same." She was not looking at him. "Twice now, and thereabouts. Nothing, nothing, and then two blank seasons, and then a column with a season in and nothing under it, and then four of those. And the fourth ward's the same way and the sixth's got a run of about nine. And he said, and I'll give it you exactly because I have not stopped hearing it, he said, *A response that isn't recorded wasn't available, miss. That's what the form's for. If it wasn't written down, it wasn't there to use, and I'm not saying it wasn't. I'm saying nobody wrote it, and I wasn't asked, and the fella as left it wasn't asked either.*"

Marek Vale stood in a corridor on the ground floor of a building he has worked in for eleven years and worked out, in about the time it takes to say a thing, that Neris Kade had walked in there holding the one document that could have valued forty minutes at the bottom of the Bellweather reach, and that it is a fair hand and it is complete and it is four names and a gang of six and a clearing rota on a Tuesday, and that it went to a Council two years ago in an ordinary return in answer to a badly designed form with one box on it marked *local flood precautions, list*, and that a clerk stamped it and filed it, and that it is not in a press on the ground floor.

"So the Exchange's schedule of the ninth ward," he said slowly, "is the only schedule of the ninth ward."

"That's what I've been standing here thinking." She got it out and then she got angry at how small it was. "Eleven shillings. We sold the same sheet twice. It's on their wall and it's on their price list and they've printed it and it's more useful to a salvage man than it is to me, and the reason is a form with one box on it and a stamp on it and four years old and in a press downstairs, and the thing in the press is not wrong, Marek. That's the — that's the *worst* of it. The thing in the press isn't wrong. There's nothing in it that anybody could go to a council about. It's just not there."

Nobody in the basin has ever been able to show what a claim cost, and it turns out that the same reason works in both directions: **a district cannot show what a claim was worth to it either, and the reason is a form, and the form is honest, and the form is empty.**

At the end of the corridor there is a bench, and on the bench in the last of the afternoon light there was a counter-reader with a satchel and a fourth notebook, who had evidently been waiting some time and did not say so.

"You are going to want a second reader," said Sable Orin, "and I want eleven shillings, and I want the reading on a board a warden is welcome to read, and I want it there before the next one goes on the rail, and I am not going to argue about the eleven shillings, and I am not going to argue about the term either, and both of those have been arranged."

"Both of those have been what for exactly a week?"

"Both of those have been what I said they were going to be, since about eleven o'clock on Saturday morning, in a room with nineteen people in it, and I was not in the room." She put the notebook down on the bench. "And before you say anything at all, I know what I sound like, and I have thought about it, and I have decided I would rather be eleven shillings with a term on it than free with none, and that the reason I have decided it is that being free with none is what I have been for two years and it got me four refusals and a fourth notebook."

She did not offer her hand and he did not ask for it, and both of them were careful about that in the way two people are careful who have each been burned by an arrangement that was not written down.

**Go back eight days. It is the Monday morning of the twenty-third, and it is the reason for the whole of it.** Neris Kade was in the lane at about half past nine with a ruled page in her coat, and this is where she was in the morning.

It has been a page of seven lines in a drawer since the middle of the Bellweather month, and it is headed **NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came**, and it is stamped and dated in her own hand, and the seventh line is the load-bearing one and it has been in two files in two buildings and has been ruled not compellable in both.

The eighth line is still unwritten and she still cannot write it and it is the same line it has always been, and it is the ninth ward's own shelter rota, nine names on a card, in a drawer, and the card is written and the sheet is not, and the reason is that a card of nine names is a list of people she is fond of and a sheet is a document that says she is answerable for them, and she has had four years to write it and has not managed it once. And the original of the sheet, when there was one, cannot be found, because the woman who wrote it out cannot remember writing it out and is not playing at anything.

She had two wardens, a barrow, six catch pits and two grates, and about an inch of water on the twenty-third and no ground floor wet anywhere and the best Monday morning this ward has had in four years.

And on the ninth ward's low ground at about a quarter to ten on that Monday morning there was a woman standing at the head of the Sump Lane school steps in the rain holding a printed sheet, because a counter-reader had given her the reading at eight that morning with a condition attached, and the woman had taken it, and the condition was that the reading go on a board a warden is welcome to read and be free, and Neris Kade had said, in a room over a drying floor, *the ward's own women have four hands* —

and it was on the board at about a quarter to ten, and it was in a fair hand, and nobody in Low Spindle had ever seen a sequence reading in their own language before, and there is a plate glass case at that school that has had the ward's weekly copy in it since before anybody living put it there, and the reading went into the case, and the glass at the school is not the tower's glass, and the school is on the low ground and the low ground can read.

Two hundred and forty children in that school. Forty of them walk up from the low blocks.

"Read it out," said Ampow, from the back, to nobody, in a sergeant's voice, and then, to Pet, who was fifteen and furious and a runner: "Pet, go down to the Bellweather apron and tell the foreman with the book to leave it be four days. And tell him if anybody from a carrier's office comes up there asking what's in the shed, he's to say the load is paid for and stored against an order and there is a painted board on it, and that's the whole of what he says, and he's not to be clever about it."

The ninth ward's signal glass, two thousand feet above the low end of the city, on the corner of Tannery Walk and Sump Lane, showed nothing at all, as it had shown nothing at all since the middle of the autumn before last, and the frame above it was a permanent brass surround bedded by hand around a bubbled temporary, and the reason was money, and there is no hand in it anywhere, and no form for it, and the district's line item has never once in two years covered a man and a crate sent forty miles.

And the claim sat in a tray in a records room with a card in a fair hand on it, and a second true copy sat in a locked box on the same floor, and the first thing in that box has been there since the third of the Bellweather month, and neither one of them was a document anybody could be shown.
