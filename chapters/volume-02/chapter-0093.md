# Chapter 93 — The Ledger Pays

On the afternoon of the sixteenth of Miremonth, in the hearing room in the basin house, the temporary compensation ledger pays one claimant and refuses another.

The room is fuller than it was on the Tuesday and the rain has not let up. There is a bench along the wall that was not there on the Tuesday, and the ward's concurrence sheet is on the trestle in a fair hand with the vote minuted under it, eight to one, and the one was a man with a cart on the reach and wanted it minuted. The seven boxes are in the file in order, all seven, and the seventh is not read out loud, because the seventh is not part of a claim. The three uncompellable books are not in the room, because nobody has asked for any of them.

Sabin Orle puts her pencil down, which is a thing she does about four times a year, and reads the two minutes out loud before she drafts a line of either, because she did it in the Bellweather month in front of nine people and has apparently got a habit, and there is a clerk in the room who will minute whatever she says and she would rather be corrected here than there.

"First," she says, "The ninth ward."

The ward gave up the window of the morning of the seventeenth of Drownmonth on purpose, in writing, to stop a second sale of it, with the seventh box on the sheet in capitals saying the cost is not money. The Exchange sold that morning three times — the fourth at 11:40 to a salvage interest, the fourth at 11:52 to a firm on its own onward sale, the fifteenth at 10:20 to a barge interest on a photograph, ten days after the sales had closed on the fifth — and the ward closed it, and the reach did not come down on the Thursday, and came down for forty minutes on the Friday with nobody on it.

**The ward is a district. A district can be a claimant. The loss is documented: the entered claim, the seventh box, the concurrence, the Exchange's published price, the three refunds, the struck-through lines, the salvage schedule as the measure of what a preparation is.**

She reads the documentation out one item at a time and the clerk ticks each one on the sheet in front of her, and every tick is a document somebody put in a file in a building, and not one of them is a document about a morning.

The claim sheet is on the rail with the bell struck at four for the ninth ward and once. The seventh box is in capitals and says the cost is not money. The concurrence is in a warden's hand with a vote of eight to one and the one minuted, and it is the only line in the file with a man's cart in it. The Exchange's paragraph is in print on three places, with two lines struck through in public, one price, and the receipt for eleven shillings under the figure. The three refunds are a firm's clerk's hand and they are half of seven and six three times over. The salvage schedule is two sheets of ordinary paper admitted yesterday and priced to the penny, and it is in the file as the measure of what a preparation is worth, and it is the only one of the six that a person wrote for himself.

"The ledger pays the ward for the window it gave up," says the assessor, "at the Exchange's price."

Seven shillings and sixpence. Not one and four. Not two and six.

**Because the Exchange's price is the only price in the basin. The ward's own figure of one and four is refused for want of a record: never entered by the ward, never published by the ward, computed by the firm out of an internal district record the ward cannot withdraw, owned by the firm for eleven shillings. The Council may not supply a figure and may not invent one. A loss which is a period cannot be entered as a sum, and the only sum anybody published is seven and six.**

The clerk writes it in a fair hand. The rule is on the page: an internal district record is a document in this building and not in yours; a claimant must be a district and a figure must be entered or published by the claimant; the computation came after the claim and the return went before it; the interim stands for the season; the minutes copies went into the tray and the tray went to the cell.

Neris Kade signs the entry as the ward's claim, because the ward signs its own claim, and reads it before she signs it, and asks one question, which is whether the figure on the page is the figure in the seventh box, and the assessor says there is no figure in the seventh box, and Neris Kade says yes, and signs it anyway, and puts the pen down flat on the table instead of leaving it in her hand.

"Where does the money go, Mrs. Kade?"

"The keeper's evening hours and the oil, and the week after next if there is anything left of it." She says it before anybody has suggested it to her, and the clerk writes it down, and she looks at the clerk and says, "Write that it was said by the ward and not asked for by anybody, and put the date on it, and do not thank me for it."

**Anwen Sarr's clause four defeats her a fourth time, correctly, in a room, with a clerk. The column names what closed and by no means who closed it, and the ward that wrote the column is paid under it at somebody else's price, and the ward's own price is refused because nobody wrote it where it could be paid.**

Anwen does not lose her temper. She is not consoled. She is owed nothing. She folds Circular 14 back into her coat and nods once, the way Dellow nodded yesterday, because there is nothing else to do with a correct defeat.

"Second," says the assessor. "Mr. Dellow."

The salvage schedule is on the trestle, priced, admitted, forty-one entries to the penny over eleven years, a hundred and sixty yards on a good forty minutes and a hundred and twenty on a short one.

It was refused in this room yesterday, in four minutes, on the ground that a claim requires a sponsor and a sponsor must be a district. It goes in the book today because a refusal that is not in the book is a conversation, and this book is two inches thick and has four lines in it and one of them is a statement of ignorance.

**The ledger refuses him, in the same afternoon, in four minutes, correctly, with the clerk writing it down: a claim requires a sponsor and a sponsor must be a district, and a freeholder of two hundred yards is not a district. The schedule is retained as the measure. The man cannot be paid from it.**

The temporary compensation ledger is two inches thick, expires at the end of the financial year, is a ledger and not a fund, and pays documented loss only. It now has four entries: the first line, nineteen words, the figure unknown, the Council pays 2/6 because 2/6 is the only number in the room; the ward's hand, received, claims nothing further at this date; the payment at seven and six with the refusal of one and four beside it; and the refusal of the salvage man on the same page, in the same afternoon.

**The 2/6 is not re-derived and does not change. It stands for the season and not for each event, and the ward is paid for a morning and not for a wall.**

Maro Hesk, at the back, takes off his hat and puts it back on. He has struck a line through twice in public and withdrawn a product with no price on it and printed somebody else's figure with the receipt under it, and he has declined in writing to bring any claim against a district it has just ruined, after taking advice it says it was not told to take, because a hearing produces a finding and a finding is citable.

He is competent, helpful and correct, and the reader is more afraid of him now than at the start of the season, because a market that pays without being asked to has discovered something about itself.

Nobody in this basin has worked out yet what a market discovers when it pays without being asked to, and it will not be worked out in this room, and the only line anybody has put down about it anywhere is the fourth entry in a two-inch book with an expiry on the inside cover.

Neris Kade sits with the payment in front of her and the refusal beside it, and says nothing, because the ledger has paid her district at somebody else's price and refused her district's own, and both of those are correct, and there is nothing to say that the room has not already minuted.

The room empties the way a Council room empties, all at once, into rain. Dellow is not in it, and was not asked to be, and has been told by a licensing clerk that a refusal went in the book on the Wednesday, which is the whole of what anybody owes him, and he has a chain to oil at half past four.
