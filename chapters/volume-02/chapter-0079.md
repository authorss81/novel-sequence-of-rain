# Chapter 79 — The Hours In The Low Blocks

The shutter went up on the tannery side of Tannery Walk at about ten past seven on the Tuesday morning of the twenty-ninth day of Drownmonth and it was copied out twice in a fair hand by four women with pen and ink, once into the plate glass case at the Sump Lane school and once onto a shutter at the district office, and the district office copy was the one that mattered, because in the ninth ward a notice is not a notice until four people have copied it out by hand and one of them has said it out loud to another one.

And at about seven forty, in front of the shutter, in the rain, which was not doing anything remarkable, about forty adults stood and read each other's copies to each other in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

**They read a disagreement.** Not a forecast. A *difference*: nine minutes, on the hour the reach comes off, Route doubled against Route single, and under it two names and an hour and a date and the words *nobody must do anything because of this.*

And a woman said, out loud, to a man she has known thirty years: "Nine minutes. That's not nothing, that's the chain."

"That's the chain," he agreed.

"That's my whole trade," she said, "and it isn't even mine, it's a shed on the south bank, and the fella's sixty-eight."

---

**And there was one other thing about the morning, and it is small.**

**The district did not put the hours on the door of its own office.**

There is a district office in the ninth ward with a notice board on it and a bell in it, and the ward's own four shutters are the copies, and the copies are the notice, and everybody who lives in the low blocks has to walk to one of them. It is a question of geography and not of principle, and the ward knows it: the district office is on the canal side and the low blocks are the other side of the pump house, and on a wet morning it is a nine-minute walk each way for a woman with a child.

**Nobody proposed moving the shutters. Nobody proposed anything.** The first set went up on the tannery side of Tannery Walk at ten past seven and they were copied out twice in a fair hand and one of the copies went into the plate glass case at the Sump Lane school and one went onto a shutter at the district office, and that is all that has happened, and it is the correct procedure because there is no other procedure, and in about a month somebody in this basin is going to write a paper about consent and about where a reading is put, and the paper will be correct about the money and it will be wrong about the ground floor.

Ivy Tranter brought a pot of tea in a cloth up a ladder at eight and stayed to be asked whether the nine minutes ought to be on the low blocks' copies as well as the others, and Kell Bray was not there and Neris Kade was not there and the answer was yes, in a fair hand, on all four, in about four minutes, by a woman of fifty-eight who brings tea and has never been thanked and did not mention it.

---

**Neris Kade came down at half past eight because a runner had gone up to the district office at about a quarter to eight with a pencil note, and it is her office, and she read it on the way and did not need the runner to say anything.**

She is thirty-seven and she has been a Storm Warden for nine years and she has spent her district's reach twice in four weeks and written every word of it down in a fair hand, and she had a hand's worth of paper for two months of spending and a line in a Council register forty-five years late and two and six in a provisional ledger for a morning's work.

And on the Tuesday morning she was going to be handed, for nothing, by two people who were not her district and had not been paid by her district, the thing she had told an assessor she could not get anybody to give her:

**When her own reach comes off.**

**At about half past one, on the second day of next month, and at about eleven minutes past four at the south end, and the two are four hours and ten minutes apart on the water, and one of the two answers is nine minutes out, and the nine minutes is written down in public by a counter-reader with her own name at the top of it.**

She stood in front of that shutter for longer than anybody else did. She did not touch it. There is a great deal of pressure in a district to touch a notice, and she had a rule about that which she had had since the winter, and the rule was that if she puts her hand on a thing in public she owns it in front of a room, and she cannot afford to own anything this month.

"Miss Kade." The woman who had copied it out, and who brings a pot of tea in a cloth up a ladder at eight and has done for four years and has never once been properly thanked. "Ma'am. Do you want it copied again for the low blocks?"

"Yes. All four. The two schools' gates, the pump house, and the chapel, and put the hours on it and not the movements, and put the nine minutes on it as well because I am not having anybody in the third block thinking they have got a clean hour."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Thank you," said Neris Kade, and Ivy Tranter said *you're welcome* in the way she says it, which is as though it were a small joke, and it was, and it is the only joke anybody makes in the ninth ward before nine in the morning.

---

**And then the firm came, and it came at about eleven, and it came properly, and it is the last decent thing anybody in this basin did to that district in the whole of the season.**

The counter clerk was a man of about twenty-five called **Coll Marsden** and he had a card and a form and a satchel, and he asked for the warden's ear, in a doorway, in about eleven words, and Neris Kade gave him the room over the locker's yard because there was nowhere else to stand.

"Mr. Marsden. The firm is not asking the ward to buy anything and the firm is not asking the ward for anything at all, and I want that said first and I want it minuted if you like, and the form says so, you may read it."

"I've read it, ma'am. It's a request. It says the ward may refuse and that a refusal will be filed as a refusal and will not be held against the ward in any transaction."

"Right." She turned the form round and looked at it, and it is one page, and the question on it is at the bottom, and it is a good question, and she has never seen a good question from that firm in her life. "Then it asks whether the district will permit the ward's own reach hours, as published on the district's shutters, to be printed on the firm's public list, once a week, with the ward's name on it, and it says the district may name a rate."

"He said you'd say that, ma'am."

"He said I'd say *something*. What rate."

Coll Marsden put a figure on the corner of the form. It is a shilling a week. It is not a large figure and it is not a small one, and it is a little over four shillings a month, and the ward got eleven shillings once, in the autumn, for eighteen pages, off a councillor, in a coat, on a vote taken at four o'clock on a Wednesday while the warden was on a pump. **Nobody has ever offered that district anything before that and nobody has offered it anything since, and four shillings a month is a thing you can spend**, and Neris Kade looked at it and thought, quite clearly and in about two seconds:

**That is a month's worth of the district's discretionary money and it is a free thing and the ward has nine hundred pounds of timber standing in a shed because the ward could not afford to be wrong about a reach.**

"Yes," she said.

---

It took about ninety seconds. There was no vote and there was no council and there was nobody else in the room, and the form is one page and her name and the ward's name went on it in a fair working hand and he wrote her a receipt on the firm's paper for one shilling and dated it and initialled it and put it in her hand.

And here is the whole of what happened and it is important that it be told exactly:

**Nobody in that room had asked the two readers whether the reading might be printed.** The board on the shutter carries Marek Vale's name and Sable Orin's name and the words *this board is not a notice*, and the ward is not a party to the arrangement between those two people at all, and the district had obtained the hours by being the place the board is painted on.

**And nobody had asked the ward's council, or its chair, and a district cannot sell the use of a public document without a vote, and a vote takes a fortnight, and there is one shilling on the table and the district is forty thousand pounds short of a wall.**

And nobody said *this is not consent*. That sentence exists in this basin in a box on a form that a councillor filled in on the second of Sleetmonth and it is one of the best sentences anybody has written this year, and it is in an Exchange file with a receipt for eleven shillings on it, and **no living person has read it in the last three weeks.**

And Neris Kade did not think any of those things at the time, and would have said so afterwards if asked, and did not think them then because she was tired and because it was ninety seconds and because she had been up since five.

She put the shilling in the district tin, which is for the shelter fund and is a different tin, and she wrote nothing in the district book about the week, because the district book does not have a column for a shilling.

---

**And she went up to the basin house at about half past two that afternoon, because a copy has to go in with the district's return and the return goes to the Council, and the records room is two hundred feet from a rail.**

She had never been into the west end of the records room in nine years of walking past it. It is four desks, a window, a press, and a counter, and it is the quietest room in a building that is not quiet, and at the third desk on the left, with her back to the window and a sheet of ruled foolscap in front of her and a pen in her hand, was a copy clerk of twenty-seven with a fair hand and a very good wrist.

"Morning. I've a page to go in the district return. Will you copy it fair and put the date and the hour on it, the way you do."

"I will." Tovan Iles looked up. "Are you the ninth ward? Because there's a form for it and it's a different hand, it's not the return, and I'd have to put it in the right book and the right book is in the press and the press takes a minute."

"That's all right. Use the return."

"Right." She began to write. And then, without looking up, in the flat voice of a competent person in a room where nobody important is present: "Can I ask you something, Mrs. Kade? It's not about the work."

"Go on."

"**When the Office makes a true copy of a claim sheet, how many does it make?**"

"One."

"It makes one," said Tovan Iles, and kept writing. "It's the minutes copy. It's the only one and it goes into the tray and the tray goes wherever the tray goes, and it's stamped and it's timed and it's in the book, and that's the end of it." She finished the line and turned the sheet round and showed her the date and the hour and initialled it. "There's a stationery card if you want a receipt for it, the tray's still got the card in it from the sixteenth."

"That's all right."

"That's all right." Tovan Iles put the sheet on the tray and squared it, and went back to the third copy of a set of minutes she was making for the technical cell, and the room was quiet again.

Neris Kade stood in the doorway of the west end of the records room for about nine seconds longer than she needed to and then went back down the corridor, and there is nobody in Veyra who could say what she was thinking, and **nobody thanked the copy clerk and nobody ever has, and nobody in that building has thanked her since the third of the Bellweather month, and there is a key in her pocket that has been there since the third of the Bellweather month, which is twenty weeks and three days.**

---

**And at about four o'clock that afternoon she sat down at her own desk in the district office with a pen and a clean sheet, the way she does about twice a month, and she wrote her page.**

*NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY. One line per notice, in the order they came.*

She read the seven lines. The first three are about the Bellweather bulletins and the fourth names a man and a plate in a coat, and the fifth is about the ward's own public glass and a fortnight, and the sixth and the seventh were written in the Bellweather month and are about a habit she got out of a corridor, and they are the two best lines on the page.

She put the pen down and picked it up and put it down and left the sheet in the drawer, open, at the seventh line.

Because there is an eighth, and she has known there was an eighth for a fortnight, and it is the line that a shutter on a tannery side would not fix:

*It does not say who may have this, and nobody has asked, and I said yes.*

**She has a card of nine names in a drawer and the sheet she cannot bring herself to write, because a card of nine names is a list of people she is fond of and a sheet is a document that says she is answerable for them.** That has been true since the winter before last and it is still true at the end of this week and she is not going to manage it, and the reason she is not going to manage it is that managing it would mean writing it down, and writing it down would mean being asked about it, and there are nine people on that list who would find out what she had written about them.

The page is seven lines. There is no eighth line and she cannot write one.
