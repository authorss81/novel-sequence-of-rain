# Chapter 75 — The Local End

The ninth ward tower ladder is nine iron rungs and a landing and then the feed seat, and it is bolted to the inside of the wall on the side away from the door so that a man going up it has his back to the room, and the reason for that is not anybody's safety, it is so that a man going up it cannot be seen from the counting floor of a district office by a witness who would then have to write something down.

Sable Orin went up it at about ten past six in the morning on the Friday of the twenty-fifth day of Drownmonth, in a good coat she had not worn since a funeral in Ternway, and Gant Ollis held the foot of it and said nothing at all, which she noticed and was grateful for in a way she had not expected to be on a Friday morning.

---

He is sixty-three or sixty-four and has kept the tower eleven years and has a rule, which is that a keeper's book is not a record and is not a record anybody may ask for, and he has never once broken it and has never once tested it and does not intend to.

That rule is ninety years old. It came out of a dispute between a district and a water office about whether a keeper had to produce his run sheets in a case about a flooded cellar, and the answer was no, and the answer has been no for ninety years, and **no council has ever asked a keeper for his book, and that is why it is worth nothing, and that is also the reason it is right.**

"You'll want to see the run sheets," he said, at the top of the ladder, before she had got her second knee on the landing. "They're the book on the ledge. Mind the elbow, the catch's been bent since the spring."

She looked at the book for about twenty minutes and it is the only continuous record in the ninth ward of what its own sky has done, and it is a keeper's book, so it is not a record.

---

**The room at the top is about eight feet by eleven and it holds a feed seat, a frame, a shelf, a stool, a window that faces the low ground, and the light.**

The feed is warm. She put the back of her hand on the plate in the holder, the way you would, and it was warm to within a degree of her palm and she wrote that down in the fourth notebook in three words, *feed warm, run clean*.

The frame on the outside wall carries a bubbled temporary in a hand-bedded brass surround, and the surround is the best work in this ward by a distance, and the temporary has been there since the autumn before last, and the ward's head in the frame has not lit since the middle of the Bellweather month. **A district that cannot read its own notice cannot be shown to have been right and cannot be shown to have been wrong, and the ninth ward has been in that condition since the autumn before last and lives off four women with pen and ink who copy the ward's weather onto four shutters, one of whom is a nail in a tobacconist's, and one of whom is a plate glass case in a school.**

On the shelf under the feed seat there is a plate in a wooden box with a date written on the lid in Marek Vale's hand and the word *spare*, and the plate is not fitted, and she saw it and did not ask about it, because she had been in a room for two hours and she had learned in that two hours that a man keeps a tower and a keeper keeps the tower and neither of them is going to explain the shelf to a counter-reader from the low end of the city.

And on the ledge, in the book, in a column headed by a date in October, there is a fifth column.

*Exchange sheet, 4 times a day. Compared with this column.*

**Twelve agreements out of twelve, in three days, in a keeper's hand, in a column nobody has asked for, with the conclusion written at the foot of it: *The Exchange's reach figures are good to a quarter of an inch. Nobody has asked me for this.***

"You did that on your own."

"I did it because the tobacconist's sheet comes four times a day and it is on my nail," said Gant Ollis, "and because I have a book and no reason to have a book."

He showed her the whole of the fifth column, and the two-line note to a shed on the freight apron a hundred and forty miles down the hill road, and the sentence that came back: *it is the chain.* And he did not show her the holder, and she did not ask, and she was in that room for the best part of six hours with a working plate in its seat carrying a twelve-year-old Witness in its substrate, and neither of them ever went near it, and neither of them ever mentioned it, and that is the whole of what happened in that tower that day.

**And she asked him two questions in six hours and both of them were about the weather and one of them was the best question anybody asked her all year.**

The first was at about half past seven, when she had been on the ladder and the feed for an hour and had worked out that the plate in the seat has a certain kind of mark on it that she had seen on about nine hundred pounds' worth of strip of sequence glass in her life and had never once seen on a plate that was being read by a person.

"That's not a reading," she said. "That's a wear mark. Somebody's had that plate out and put it back and it's not sat square since."

"Aye," said Gant Ollis. "It came out at ten past six on a Friday morning two months ago and it went back up at ten that night and I was not told anything about it and I was not asked and I have not been up that ladder with a plate in my hand."

"You weren't?"

"I carried the bundle down. That's my job on a Friday and it's been my job since I came." He turned a page. "I don't open holders. Nobody's ever asked me to open a holder and there's no reason anybody ever should, because a keeper who opens a holder is a keeper who's touched the instrument and then everybody's got a question."

The second question was at about one o'clock and it was better, and she asked it in the middle of looking at a strip, and it is in the fourth notebook afterwards.

"Mr. Ollis. Why is it a keeper's book and not a record?"

He thought about it for long enough that she looked up.

"Because a record is a thing somebody can be asked for," he said, "and I have had this chair eleven years and I have never once been asked for anything out of it, and I'd like to keep it that way, and if it was a record I'd have to have somebody come and take it out one day, and then they'd be here every season, and then I'd be a public officer instead of a man in a tower, and a public officer gets told what to write down."

**And Sable Orin wrote that down verbatim, at the top of a page, in the middle of the fourth notebook, and did not know then why it mattered, and it was about two hours' ride away that a foreman on the ridge read a Director's circular and thought of a shed foreman and a tower keeper, and did not write that down either.**

---

**He came to the tower at about ten past two in the afternoon on the Monday of the twenty-eighth, which is three days later, and the reason for the gap is the fee.**

She had said eleven shillings a read and a retainer of a shilling a week, in coin, before she starts, and on the Friday morning before dawn, at the bottom of nine rungs, in a good coat, with the cold coming off the low ground, he counted out eleven shillings and one shilling in sixpences and threepences and a penny on the top step of the ladder because there was nowhere else to put it.

**She counted it faster than the time before, and did not say thank you, and he noticed the speed of the counting and did not say anything about it, and it is the only thing that happened between the two of them in three days that either of them wrote down.**

The fee was for the day. The retainer was for the week. Both of those words had been hers and she had not changed either of them and she was not going to, and when he said, on the Friday, standing at the foot of the ladder, *I am going to ask you to do this three times and I am not going to be able to pay three times*, she said:

"You are going to pay the retainer, and the retainer is a shilling a week, and the week is the week, and I am not going to be paid twice for the same week by a man who has four pounds in the world." She got the first nine inches of the ladder and then stopped and looked down at him. "**And I am going to say the other thing again because we are three days off it and I have thought about it, and I have thought about it because somebody asked me on Monday whether I was worried about the shutter being on a list.**"

"You have not been asked by anybody. I have not been asked anything and I would have told you."

"You have not been asked," she agreed. "**A term is not a favour. A term is not consent. And on Monday, in a firm, in a counting floor, a man wrote a notice saying that a level is not a sequence — and I read it twice and I was proud, and on the way home I sat on a step and worked out that the reason I was proud is that I could be located.**"

She went up the rest of the ladder.

"A reader is a person. I am a person. A person has an address, and now I have one at eight o'clock in the morning by an appointment, and there is no version of the eleventh of this month where the appointment is not in a book, and the book is in an office, and I have never in my life been in an office and been hard to find." She turned round at the top and looked down at him. "**You can have your board. I am not moving it and I am not going back to the room over the drying floor. If either of us ever comes to believe that a term has discharged the other thing, then the eleven shillings is worth nothing, and so is the shutter, and I will take it down myself and you may watch me do it.**"

---

**The first attempt failed at about eleven o'clock on the Monday and the second failed at about four, and neither failure had anything to do with the thing anybody would have expected.**

A two-ended read is not two people looking at two pieces of glass. It is two channels and the sequence has to run through both of them and come out at the far end in an order you can name, and the difficulty is that the two ends do not agree and neither of them is the sequence. She read the local end properly. He has one channel and the other one is not there and that has been in a yard and in a Council corridor and in a file, and it is not going to be the subject of anything.

The first failure was the **order**, and it was hers, and it was about Weight or Break.

"You have Weight arriving late on top of a Release that has not finished," she said, at about eleven, with the strip flat on the ledge. "And the sky over the low ground has been doing Break on top of a Weight that *had* finished, every day this month, and I have watched it out of that window for eleven days, and the low ground does not get Weight. The low ground gets the turn."

"That is what I have too."

"You have what you have got off a level and a plate a hundred and forty miles away and it is a different object." She put her finger on the ledge. "**You are reading the far end and calling it the order. That is the mistake you made on the Saturday of the twelfth and I caught it then, and you have made it twice since and I have only caught it once.**"

He agreed with her in about four words and they went at it again for two hours and got a strip and did not agree about it, and **that disagreement is the fifth one on the shutter and it is still on the shutter, and nobody has taken it down, and the shutter has a date on it.**

The second failure was the **basis**, and it was his, and it was the fault she caught on the Saturday of the twelfth and it is the reason the shutter exists.

"Your sheet says the bend is at half past one and my sheet says the bend is at the hour, and you have gone and taken the level's word for what hour it is." She was not being cruel about it; she was doing the thing she does, which is to say a thing once, in a flat voice, and then stop. "**A level instrument cannot be asked what hour anything is. That is the fault you have got, and you have got it because you have been reading a gauge as though it were a clock, and the shutter on Tannery Walk is the receipt for it and two names are on it.**"

He agreed with her in about four words and then they had it again, and at about six in the evening it went wrong a third time in a way that was neither of those things, and at about nine at night the second end of the run came in and they had a Release, and a bend in it, and an hour on the bend.

It was half past one. She was right on the movement and he was right on the hour, and they both said so, and it went up on the shutter on the Tuesday morning at about seven o'clock in the tannery side of Tannery Walk, in three hands, none of them his, with her correction in her hand and his agreement in his and the date on it and the line at the bottom saying that nobody must do anything because of it.
