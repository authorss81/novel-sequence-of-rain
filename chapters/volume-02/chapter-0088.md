# Chapter 88 — The Small Back-Cut

The season's third back-cut comes on the morning of the eleventh of Miremonth, and it is the smallest of the three, and nobody makes very much of it, because there is not very much to make.

The first was in the middle of Sleetmonth. The second came on the morning of the seventeenth of Drownmonth, and it was the largest of the three, and the reach did not come down on it. This one clears for about three hours before noon, dries the washing on the lines in Low Spindle, and goes back over by two.

**The season's rhythm is rain, clear, rain, clear, and this is the last clear, and it is the smallest, and that is the whole of the weather.**

---

On the tenth, which was the day the ward's window was for, the reach came off at about half past one on the low ground and about eleven minutes past four at the south end, four hours and ten minutes apart on the water, and the flat took a gang for forty minutes.

There were six men on it and a water steward, and the pump gang stood by with the gear, and the man with the cart had his cart on the stone because he had said so in a concurrence and had it minuted, and had taken it off the flat at ten past one without saying anything to anybody, which is what a man does when he has been right to insist.

Sable Orin was on the flat with a working copy of the tower's feed rolled in a oilskin under her arm and a strip in a tin, and she did the order of the movements and nothing else, because the order of the movements is all that anybody had asked her for and she is paid a shilling a week and has not yet worked out whether she is paid for the hour or for the standing.

"The south end is late again," she said, from the third block's wall, to nobody in particular, and a man on the flat said, "It's always late," and she wrote that down, because it is a reading and it is hers, and because a foreman saying a thing on a flat is a datum and a foreman saying a thing in a shop is a complaint.

Beddoe worked the flat N end first, because that is the order of the ground, and everybody who works that flat has worked it N end first for twenty years without ever once putting it in a document, and he got the lower drains running and the pump gear up on the stone and about ninety yards of the worst of the silt off the copper line in forty minutes, which is ninety and not the hundred and sixty a salvage man gets, because a salvage man has a chain and two carts and a foreman and a district has six men and a pump, and neither of those numbers has ever been compared with the other one in any document in this basin.

Then it was over, and the six went back to the district's other work, and nothing was refunded, and nothing reopened, and the third block's drains ran clean for about a week afterwards, which is what forty minutes is worth, and there is no column anywhere in this basin for that either.

Beddoe wrote it in his own book at the pump house that evening, with the hour and the gang and the gear and the yardage, in the hand he has written in for twenty-two years, and nobody has asked for the book, and it is the only record in this basin of a district getting what it asked for.

---

Petrie reads his level book at six in the morning the way other men read a newspaper, and writes the water in it, and does not write the plan in it, because a level book has the water in it nine hundred times and the plan in it never once, and its whole function is to settle a row afterwards.

The *no work, reason* column, empty for thirty years, has one entry in it now in a warden's hand, in a copy, in a different book — the first line in the basin that says the work was not done and why — and the original column in the shed still says *reach came back* in the work-done column, because a fact is not a reason.

The load is unchanged: about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands, in a second consecutive season in the shed. The Lower Sill wall is in its third consecutive season of not being worked on. **Two different counters, and the table on the water office's wall says two years and about nine months, and the line between the two pegs cannot be produced by anybody.**

Warrick is on the low side at about seven with his two men, watching the small tide take the lighters at half. There is one more tide after the spring and it is the small one and it takes the lighters at half, in about nine hours instead of six, and he said in writing that if it is a bad month he will get three days at half and will take that as it comes, and it is a good month.

Forty tons of stone will go on in nine hours instead of six. The masons are paid for the day. The chain barge lies over the bar with eight and a half on it and does not try. A barge crew stands on a quay and is paid.

**Nothing is refunded. The wall waits and it will get its month, and the sixth ward's head on the wall does nothing, because there is no claim on it this morning, and the ninth ward's has not lit since the middle of the Bellweather month.**

---

Tobias Mear sits in the Exchange's second-floor room at about ten with two documents in front of him, and does not put them together.

On the left is the Office's weekly sheet, printed on the Office's press, four lines under the heading this week and five next week, pinned to the firm's wall by a tobacconist who can read neither: Hearthward terraces week of the second; the ninth ward, the reach and the wall, week of the seventeenth; the Bellweather reach, quoted and not opened, the morning of the thirtieth of the Bellweather month, forty minutes; the sixth ward's tide, morning of the thirtieth of Drownmonth; and now the ninth ward's Miremonth window, morning of the tenth.

On the right, three feet away, is the firm's own paragraph about the same reach: the float, the chain, the drum, the staff, the glass datum cut by a maintenance hand, good to a quarter of an inch at an interval by two people with two chains, and the figure of one and four computed out of eighteen pages furnished for eleven shillings.

**The third of the Office's lines is the Bellweather dry line — the morning of the thirtieth of the Bellweather month, which is a different thirtieth from the tide line printed under it in the same week — and the firm's paragraph is about the same reach, and the two documents are three feet apart on one wall, and nobody joins them.**

He reads both of them for a long time and writes nothing on either. On the tab in the assessor's file, in her own hand, is the line his pencil note put there a month ago: the fourth party has not been asked. He puts the pencil down and goes down the hill to look at a wall, because a wall is a thing he can look at.

---

Anwen Sarr reads Circular 14 for the forty-first time in her coat, standing in the Hearthward assembly rooms with the rain coming off the market steps.

Four paragraphs. A window is not a claim; the Office will not certify, endorse, price, regulate or advise upon one; the Office has no instrument which looks at a river or a reach, has never had one, will not build one, and does not consider the absence a defect in its work; and any claim for the value of a reach is a claim against a party that sold a window and not against the Office.

**There is not one lie in it. He is correct, and he is never wrong about the weather, and he has just told a ward in print to go and sue a shop.**

She folds it and takes out Circular 15, four paragraphs, sealed and issued the twenty-sixth of Drownmonth, signed C. ROOK. Paragraph two is a thing a foreman has been saying in a shop for nineteen years. Paragraph three closes a trade in glass with a seal and a date. Paragraph four is about a district and says that a party which had a published figure and did not ask whether it might be used has a matter between the two parties.

**He did not have to do it. A Director may close a trade in glass with a seal and a date and never so much as send for a form, and there is no appeal and no counter-signature and no objection period.**

She puts both of them back in her coat. She has been refused three times. Her standing requirement has a column and a refused demand, and the refused demand's language is better than the column's and she knows it. She will be refused a fourth time, correctly, in a room, with a clerk, and she will not lose her temper about it and she will not be consoled.

"Ma'am?" says Kell Bray from the doorway. "The hearing is the fourteenth."

"I know," says Anwen Sarr. "Clause four defeats me again. I wrote it and I will defend it for the rest of my life, and it is going to beat me in that room, and that is what it is for."
