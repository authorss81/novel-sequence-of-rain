# Chapter 112 — Two Answers In Nine Days

The thing that made it a problem was not that the fourth ward had refused. It was that the ninth ward had answered.

Sabin Orle had the two of them on her desk on the Thursday of the sixth, in a tray, in the order they had arrived, and they had arrived nine days apart and they were in each other's way, and she found that out at about nine in the morning and then could not put them down again.

*The ninth ward, received on the twenty-seventh day of last month, four lines, in a fair working hand, on the Council's own form, answering item four: it can put six men on a pump and a steward with a lantern, and it has nine people who can stand in a doorway for four hours — and inside the answer, in the right-hand column, in the warden's own working hand, a refusal to say who the nine are, with a reason of about eighty words which is the best thing anybody has sent this office in six years.*

*The fourth ward, received on the fifth, in an envelope with a Hearthward minute number on it, which is the office that asked the question. Four lines. It declines to fill in the form of the twenty-first, declines to be entered in any list, return or index in respect of a reading, declines to have a reading printed, and declines to be asked the question again in a form. Carried eight to one with the one minuted against the minute, on the district's own board, in the district's own name, at the district's own cost.*

And in the same tray, from the sixth ward, received on the third, four lines that say the ward cannot answer item four, that its book is retained, and that the book is offered for the use of a district on request.

**"Two answers in nine days that do not agree with each other, and I have three, and one of the three is a name I am not going to write in a minute."**

She said it out loud to an empty room, which is a thing she has done perhaps nine times in eleven years, and then she did the thing she does when a room will not answer her, which is to write it out as she spoke it in the hand she uses for things that will be minuted.

*Item four cannot be answered by this office and cannot be answered from any register, for the reason given in the minute of the twenty-first. Two districts have replied within nine days and the replies are not reconcilable, because the ninth ward has answered the question and the fourth ward has answered the question about the question. A third has declined the question and named the person who used to hold the answer. The technical cell requires a procedure that will accept an answer, a refusal and a name in the same season, and there is no such procedure, and the reason there is no such procedure is that no office in this Council has ever received one of the three.*

---

**And what she reached for, in the end, after four days, was a schedule, and she has been an assessor for eleven years and she knows exactly what a schedule is.**

The alternative was obvious and she wrote it out first: ask each district quarterly, in a form of the cell's own choosing, and take what comes. Nine wards, four times a year, thirty-six pieces of paper, and a clerk to open them, and at the end of the year a table.

"That is not a survey," she said, to the wall. "A survey asks the same question of everybody in the same words, and the fourth ward will send me nine words and the ninth ward will send me a page with a refusal in it, and at the end of the year I will have thirty-six pieces of paper that cannot be compared, and I will present them to a Board that asked me for a date, and one of the two things I was sent to find will be in my hand."

So she built the other thing. A standing day. The fourth Thursday of every month, which is a week after the Council's own term day and a week before anybody's district meeting had been fixed for the season, and it is the only day in the month on which all nine clerks are in their own offices and are not in court.

She wrote the head of it herself, on the Thursday afternoon, and it took her the best part of two hours, and it is one sheet with a printed head and six ruled lines and a column for the district and a column headed *what the district can do about its water* and a second column headed *who can do it*, and at the foot, in a smaller hand, the line that she is proudest of and least sure of:

*The district is not obliged to fill this in. A district which cannot answer may say so, and saying so is an answer, and will be recorded as an answer and not as a failure to attend.*

Then she sent it down to the west end of the records room to be set, and it went down on the Friday with a slip on it and the slip took four minutes, which is what a slip has always taken.

---

Tovan Iles is twenty-seven and has been a copy clerk since the third of the Bellweather month, the demotion is permanent, and nobody has thanked her.

The head came down on the Friday and she read it standing at the counter, and then she read it a second time, which is what she does, and then she said the one sentence that the assessor thought about for two days afterwards.

"There's no where-put column, ma'am."

"There is no where-put column."

"There's a where-put column in the book the entries go into." Tovan Iles put her finger on the head without touching it. "Eleven years of it and four before mine. Day, from, what, and where it was put. That last one's got a heading printed at the top of the column in the same type as the rest of it and it is not optional, and whoever set that book up thought somebody would fill it in. Nobody ever has. I know that because I have counted the entries that have it, and there are some, and I know it is not most of them, and I have never told a managing clerk a single thing about it in four years, because I am a copy clerk and that is a copy clerk's business."

"Will it be a problem?"

"Ma'am, it is a design."

And then, before she could be asked, because she had worked it out and because a copy clerk's whole life is a design:

"A printed head is the only part of any of this that will still be here in twenty years, and it does not have anybody's name on it, and that is not a mercy. If a thing is going to last, it is going to last without us." She squared the sheet against the edge of the counter. "Set it. And if there is a fee on it, put the fee at the top where the district can see it, and not at the bottom, because at the bottom nobody reads it and then they find out at a counting floor."

There is a fee. It is two shillings, it has always been two shillings, it is on nothing else the Council has ever sent a district because the Council has never sent a district anything, and it goes at the top of the head in eleven-point type and it is the first money this Council has ever asked a district to pay for.

**Nobody thanked her. Orle wrote *no where-put column* in pencil in the margin of her own copy, which is the thing she uses for the parts that will not go in the minute, and the pencil is also how she records the things she is frightened of.**

---

And then there was Corrin Malle in a corridor at about half past four, with a roster he did not want, in the way of a man carrying something he has been given to carry.

"Two answers in nine days," he said.

"Yes."

"There is no procedure for two answers, ma'am, and there is no procedure for two answers in eleven years, and there never will be, and you know that, and you are not doing it because you do not know it." He shifted the roster. "**A schedule is a procedure that runs itself. It is the only thing this department has ever built that does not need anybody in it to want anything.**"

"That is not a reason against it."

"No, ma'am." He was a moment. "The other reason against it is that in a month you will have nine papers and they will all be true, and the whole Board will know more about the nine wards than it has known in a hundred and forty years, and every one of the nine will be wrong, because they are what nine clerks could write down in a quarter of an hour, and a wrong answer on a printed head from this Council is not a district's fault."

"**That is the best thing anybody has said to me about my own work in eleven years,"** said Sabin Orle, "and it is the reason I am going to do it anyway, so you may put it in your file with the date on it."

---

She had it set and printed and in nine envelopes by the Friday the seventh, and she addressed nine of them by hand, and the ninth ward's is the only one that does not say *to the clerk of the ward*, because the ninth ward does not keep a clerk and has never kept one, and it is addressed to a warden's office in a room over a locker's yard, which is a person, and the eighth envelope she addressed to a chairman whose clerk had been at a meeting in his chair's absence, and the fourth envelope is the one she looked at longest.

*To the clerk of the fourth ward. From the Weather Council, technical cell.*

*It is requested that the district state in writing, on the fourth Thursday of each month, what it is able to do about its water. This request is made under the standing direction of the twenty-third of Drownmonth. The head of the return is printed and enclosed. The district is not obliged to fill it in. A district which cannot answer may say so, and saying so is an answer.*

*Fee, two shillings, payable at the counting floor.*

She sealed it and wrote the date on the top in her own hand, and then she sat for a moment with the pen flat on the blotter, which is a thing she does.

"It is not addressed to anybody," she said, out loud, to a room with the fire in it. "It is addressed to a place. And it is the first thing this Council has sent a district that has a place on it, and it will be answered, and the answering of it will be the first time this Council has ever held a district's answer to a district's own question, and there is nobody left in this building who will be here to be asked what that was for."

Then she put the nine envelopes in a tray, and the tray went out on the Monday the tenth, and it was the first ordinary post this Council had sent to a district office in the working life of anybody who had put it there.
