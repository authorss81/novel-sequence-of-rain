# Chapter 100 — A Corridor With No Place In It

The sheets go up on a Wednesday. That has been the rule since the beginning, because a sheet that goes up on a Wednesday is read on a Wednesday by people who buy milk on a Wednesday, and a sheet that goes up on a Friday is read on a Friday by people who already know what they think.

She had eleven out that week and this is the first of the eleven she has not been able to account for.

*The corridor below the north wall carries a draw from the west, off and on, between the fourth hour and the seventh, and the draw backs at about the fifth hour. Basis: my aneroid, my wet cell, one strip of glass bought and not cut, and the barometer at Raal's read in a public room and not copied. I cannot say why it is right and I cannot say where it is.*

**It is the fourth of these in three years that has been acted on, and three of them were acted on by people who asked me, and this one was acted on by a man who did not.**

She knows it is wrong before she gets to the fourth ward, because the copy is on a shutter on the north side of a chandler's yard with a place written across the bottom of it in a different paint, and the paint is not her sheet's paint, and the shutter is not one of hers.

*The corridor below the north wall, from 04:00 to 07:00, draw off and on. I cannot say where it is.*

And underneath, in a broad white hand, large, done by somebody who wanted it to be read from a step:

**LOWER FEN ROW, OFF THE NORTH WALL, 4 TO 7. TAKE THE SCHOOL AND THE CHURCH ROW OUT FIRST.**

She stood in the middle of a yard in the rain on the Wednesday afternoon with a satchel and eleven sheets and read it four times, and the thing that came into her first was not *I have been wrong* and it was not *somebody has stolen my sheet*, it was a piece of arithmetic, done instantly and without any feeling at all:

**The corridor below the north wall is four miles long.**

---

The fourth ward is a mile and a half north of the river and the north wall is a river wall, and there is a district of two thousand houses on the inside of it called Fen Row and there is also a street in a different ward four miles away with the same name, because there have always been two Fen Rows in this city and both of them are on a printed sheet somewhere in a printer's file.

He had picked the wrong one. He had not guessed. **He had looked it up, and he had looked it up properly, and he had found two and chosen one and written it in a hand four feet wide where anybody could see it.**

She found him at about four o'clock. Perrin Shaw is a plasterer, seventy-one, and he has copied weather sheets onto his own shop shutter for two years without being asked and without charging, because his own ward's glass has never once in his working life shown him anything he could act on.

He came out with dust in the lines of his hands.

"You're the one," he said. "I'm the one. Come in, it's filthy, you'll not want—"

"You put a street on it."

"I put a street on it because a corridor is no use to anybody." He said it without heat at all, which was somehow much worse. "**A corridor is four miles long, miss. There's nine hundred houses on that wall and every one of them's got a low door.**"

"You've put it on a shutter the size of a door and you've told a district to empty two buildings off a reading that said *I cannot say where it is*."

"Aye."

**And then he said the thing that she has carried out of four weeks of talking to people about this, and it went into her the way the others went in, which is to say she could not put it anywhere.**

"**You printed the low corridor and I've got the low corridor, and it's four miles of it, and I didn't pick a place out of the air. I went to a printer's office in the north end on the Friday and I paid a shilling for a printed list of the street names and I put Low Spindle against Fen Row because it's the lowest ground in this city and the water comes off a river wall nowhere else. And you were right, by the way. Your thing was right."**

"**Ten o'clock.**" He wiped his hands down his thighs. "And it came down the *wrong side* of the wall, and the water never got above a doorstep in Fen Row, and the crew that came off the church row stood down in a wet field for six hours on a Thursday morning and got nothing, and the school went out at four in the afternoon and got everybody home by six and then the rain came at ten and everybody was in their own house like sensible people, which is a thing a school assembly does not usually get credit for."

---

**And the part she took away, and the part that is going in the fourth book, is the part about a house on Low Spindle.**

"There's a man at number eleven Fen Row," said Perrin Shaw, "on the ground floor, and he's seventy-nine and he can't get up four stairs, and his daughter had him out on a board and up the church row steps at about half five with a blanket round him and a lamp, and the four of them stood in the assembly hall from six till nearly nine, and then she got him home down those steps in the dark, and he has a chest on him and it isn't a chest he'd have had in an assembly hall." He was not boasting. He was reading it off. "**So nobody's hurt and everybody's spent something, and I'll not be telling you it was your fault, because it wasn't your fault, you'd have written it exactly the same way again and so would I.**"

"Mr. Shaw—"

"**But the reading was a corridor and the reading had no place in it, and the place got put in by a plasterer, and the place is mine now. I've had it up nine weeks.**"

**He had said *my* about it. He did not appear to know he had said it.**

She asked him to take it down. She asked him the way she asks, which is that she asks, and she has never once had a person refuse her and then come back, and she said: *Take it down, please, and let me put a copy of my own on the shutter with the corridor on it and no place, and it will be a worse thing to read and it will be the true thing, and I'll pay for the paint.*

**And he said no, and he was polite about it, and she went away.**

---

**On the Wednesday evening, in a room above the Ninth Baths with two presses and a rack of eleven boards and a smell of wet rag, Sable Orin wrote the fourth of her notebooks, and the head of the fourth is four columns and has been four columns since the first of them:**

*DECLINED. WHO. WHY, IN THEIR WORDS. DATE.*

She turned to a clean page and then did not write for about a quarter of an hour, and the reason she did not write is the reason the page is hard to fill, and she had known it for two years and had only now written it down:

**The book is for the things she says no to. He did not ask her for anything.**

That was the whole of the entry on the first line, and it took her four attempts:

*21st M., 4 p.m., fourth ward. P. Shaw, plasterer, N side of a chandler's yard. Copied my sheet of the 14th onto his own shutter, nine weeks. Added the place himself: LOWER FEN ROW, OFF THE N WALL, 4–7, and a direction to empty two buildings, in his own hand, over my line, in paint.*

*Reason he gave, in his words: "A corridor is four miles long. There's nine hundred houses on that wall and every one of them's got a low door. I didn't pick a place out of the air. I paid a shilling for a list of the ward names and I put Low Spindle against Fen Row because it's the lowest ground in this city and the water comes off a river wall nowhere else."*

*What it cost: a crew stood down six hours on a Thursday for nothing. A school out at four and home by six and no credit for it. Four people at number eleven, a man of seventy-nine, out on a board and up the church row steps at half five and down four stairs in the dark at nine, with a chest on him.*

*It was right. It came at about ten and it came down the other side of the wall and it never got above a doorstep in Fen Row. I would have printed it exactly the same again.*

*He would not take it down. I offered to pay for the paint.*

Then, at the foot of the entry, in the same hand, smaller, and underlined once, which is the only underlining in four notebooks:

***I cannot decline this. He did not ask me for it. There is no answer in this book for a thing nobody asked me.***

---

**She sat with that for about an hour, and the hour was the whole of the moral work of the week, and what she came out with was not a rule and was not a decision, and she wrote it on the same page under the line, because if it went in the head of the book it would become a rule and a rule is a thing she has never been able to keep.**

*Three years of printing a corridor and an hour and no place, and a place has never once been put on it by me. It has now been put on it by a man with a shilling's worth of street names and a shutter, and he has done it correctly, and there was nothing wrong with the act, and if there was nothing wrong with the act then the thing I have been guarding against is not a place being invented.*

*It is that I have been handing out the part of a reading that cannot be refused, without ever once having asked whether it ought to be handed out.*

**She had told Marek Vale in a tower in the autumn that a reader is a person and a person has an address, and it is the reason she took a shilling a week and stayed findable, and it is true, and it is also, she understood at about seven o'clock on the Wednesday, a lever with her name on the end of it, and a person cannot refuse a thing that has already been made true about her.**

So she did the only thing that was not a rule and not a decision. She wrote a second sheet, the same corridor and the same hours and the same basis and the same sentence about not knowing where it was, and underneath, in a hand as large as his, and in paint, because it was going on a shutter:

**THIS IS NOT A PLACE. IT IS A CORRIDOR. IF YOU HAVE TO CHOOSE A STREET, YOU ARE NOT USING THIS SHEET. YOU ARE USING SOMEBODY ELSE'S READING AND YOU WILL NOT KNOW WHICH ONE.**

*22nd M. — S. Orin.*

And she put it up herself, on the Thursday morning of the twenty-second, at twenty minutes past seven, outside the chandler's yard, on the shutter, over his line, without asking him, because a shutter is a public thing and she had learned in four weeks that the one thing a district cannot do is come and take a public thing down.

**Then she went and found the eleven people who had asked her for a sheet in eleven weeks and asked each of them one question, which is whether their district could say where a corridor was, and nine of them could not, and she wrote the nine answers in the fourth book, and the head of the fourth book is the right head for all nine of them, and it took her until about eleven o'clock at night.**
