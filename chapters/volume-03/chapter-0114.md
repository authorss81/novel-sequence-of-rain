# Chapter 114 — The Rule Book

Wend Lyle was nineteen and had been on the second floor for eleven months, and on the Monday morning of the tenth he copied out the fourth ward's page.

That is the whole of the crime and it took four minutes, and the firm's rule is that anything that goes on a public board this month gets copied, because the firm does not charge for a thing anybody can already read. The page was on a shutter on the tannery side and the shutter is public, and it is the largest thing that has ever gone on that shutter, and it is a district's written refusal with a reader's four lines under it and the ward's four-line objection under that.

**He copied the ward's half. He did not copy the reader's half, and he noticed himself not copying it, and he wrote a note about the not doing on the back of the form and did not know why he had written it.**

Then he went to the basin house at about half past ten with a request, and the request was on the firm's paper and had the firm's minute number on it, and it asked whether the Council held, in respect of any district, a paper received by the Council other than a notice; and the covering line said the request was made in a customer's interest and would be treated as confidential.

What it did not say, and what he decided at about the fourth mile up the hill, was that there is no customer, and that the firm does not hold a customer's interest of this shape, and that the item on his own form that this whole business comes out of is item seven, and that item seven is a shutter, and that the shutter belongs to a district, and that a district is a person.

A person asked him a question. He read the answer off a wall and mistook it for an instruction to him.

The counter clerk in the basin house was a young man who was not Skell and had never been Skell in his life, and he read the request and said the correct thing.

"This office doesn't hold district papers."

"Yes," said Wend Lyle. "The firm's request is about papers the Council does hold. The Council is not a district. If the Council has received a district's paper at any point in any year, the firm is asking whether that paper is here, and the firm's customer is entitled to know, because the alternative is that the firm prices the thing it cannot produce."

It was a good answer and it was not true, and he had it ready, and the counter clerk wrote on the slip that a district's interest had been asserted and that a district's interest must be asserted in writing by the district.

Which is the moment he should have stopped.

---

**He did not stop, and the reason he did not stop is that a district had published a page saying that anybody may come to the office and copy it at the ward's cost, and he had copied it, and he had it in his coat, and a man with a form in his coat and a page in his coat is a man with standing, and nobody in this city checks.**

He went back down to the counter on the Tuesday morning of the eleventh with a different request and no form, and said that a district had asked him to look, and did not say which district, because if he had said which district the clerk would have sent a letter.

The clerk took him down a corridor and a flight of stairs into the west end, which he had never been into, and which is four desks, a window, a counter and a press, and eleven years of a day book in a copy clerk's hand, and a girl of twenty-seven at the third desk copying a set of minutes without lifting her pen.

"I want the rule book," said Wend Lyle.

"There is a rule book."

"I want to look at the return for the wire basket."

And the copy clerk put her pen down flat, which nobody in that room had seen her do, and asked him the only question that anybody in that building had asked anybody in a fortnight.

"Is that for a district or is that for your firm."

"That's for a district."

"Mm," said Tovan Iles, and got up, and took the rule book off a shelf at the end of the counter, which is not a press and is not in a press and is not anybody's record, and opened it on the counter, and turned it round, and did not keep her hand on it.

---

A rule book is a book of rules. It is the only object in the records of this Council that nobody has ever wanted to read, and that is not because it is hard to get at.

There is no custody of it. It is on a shelf at the end of a counter and it is not lent and it is not signed out and it is not in the index, and the reason is that nobody has ever needed it, and the reason nobody has ever needed it is that every rule in it is about a thing somebody else is already doing.

He read the basket return with her standing beside him, and it is nine lines long and it has been in print since before either of them was born, and it is a good return.

*Friday. Basket emptied, corridor, wire, No. 2. Contents to be carried to the post room. Return signed.*

There is a day. There is a week. There is a line. And there is no column for what was in the basket, and there is not one in the four years of it that this Council has printed, and the man whose name is against a Friday is not a custodian and is not an officer and is not in the index of offices, and the hand is the same hand in every year and it is not anybody who has been in the west end in this building's memory except the girl standing next to him.

"Does anybody read it?" asked Wend Lyle.

"No."

"Then what is it for?"

""It is for the basket," said Tovan Iles. "A return is a receipt for transit. It says the basket went. It does not say what was in it, and it was never printed with a place for it, and anybody who wants you to think otherwise has not been in here."

He thanked her and she went back to the third desk and picked up her pen and did not lift it for the rest of the afternoon, and nobody thanked her for anything, because nothing had happened, and because she is a copy clerk, and because the demotion from the third of the Bellweather month is permanent and there is no post in this building in which being right about a rule book is the description of her work.

**And on the stair he stopped, and went back down, and asked her the only question he had wanted to ask all afternoon, which was not about the basket.**

"You said the return does not require anybody to look. Who wrote that?"

"The rule," said Tovan Iles. "I did not write the rule."

"Then who knows whether it was ever required to?"

"Everybody who has been in the west end for forty years would know that, and there are two of us, and I am one, and the other one signs the first of the month four times a year and does not come in on a Friday." She did not look up. "**You have just asked me whether a rule has an author, and I have told you that the rule has no author in it, and that is the answer, and you may go up the stair now.**"

He went up the stair.

---

And then, because he had asked for a return and got a return, and because a boy of nineteen who has been told no by a counter for eleven months does not stop at no, he asked the other thing.

"The day book. *Daily, district notices received.*"

"That is a press."

"Put a slip in it."

"That takes four minutes and it is a lending slip, and I have to write your name on it, and I am going to write your name on it, and you are going to tell me the name of the district."

**And he lied, and he was seventeen months worse at it than he had been at the counter, and the reason was not the firm.**

"It's the fourth ward," he said. "There is a transfer sheet. It went to this Council on a Friday in Sleetmonth, two winters back, and the district has asked me to help them trace it."

"A district has not asked you anything."

"A district clerk wrote a line in her own file about it and showed it to me at her own counter, and she is the clerk, and she has a minute saying that anybody may come to the office and look, and I looked."

Tovan Iles wrote the name on the slip. She wrote the date. She wrote the four minutes, and she put the slip in a book she has maintained in the same way since the third of the Bellweather month, and she did not say one word to him about whether it was true, and she has not said one word to anybody about it since, and there is no rule that requires her to.

"I'll copy it," she said. "I don't do reasons."

"You'll let me have the book?"

"You will not have the book. "Nobody has ever had this book. You will have a copy of eleven days of it on a sheet, made in this room, in my hand, and it will be true, and a true copy is true whatever the reason it was asked for, and if your firm sells it I will not know and I would not be able to tell you that I had not known."

He took the slip and went up, and it was the fastest four minutes of his working life, and he did not sleep much that night, and the two things he thought about were that the basket return had no column for its contents and that a person had stood next to him in a room and told him so, and that she had told him so before he had asked, and that she had told him so as a matter of course, in the tone of a woman correcting a printer.

And in a drawer on a middle desk on the second floor, under a lehr log's worth of other paper, under a glass weight, with no index over the top, there were three Council forms in pencil in a nineteen-year-old's hand, and a fourth ward's refusal copied onto the back of one of them, and a district clerk's two lines about eleven people in a dead man's handwriting which he had not initialled because it was not his.
