# Chapter 111 — The Coal Steps

Joss Amery is thirty-four and has been the clerk of the third ward for seven years. The third ward is a lock ward. Its whole geography is one feature — the slack behind the lock, four hundred yards of it — and in the slack there is one wall that goes first, and the wall is the coal steps, and the steps go into the water twice a day in an ordinary week.

On the eleventh of the month the council asked him to write out, for the minute, what the district had done on the fourteenth of Drownmonth, and the reason it was doing it eleven days late is that a district which has acted on something it cannot check has to decide whether it did the right thing or the lucky thing, and a district cannot do that on the day.

**What he wrote out is four pages and it begins with the sheet, and the first thing it says about the sheet is that it had no name on it.**

It came into the office off a woman he had met once, on a wet afternoon, in a drying room, and it said what it said: a corridor, hours, a draw backing and re-drawing, and *cannot say where*. The hours were right to within the quarter. There was no place on it and there was no name on it, and he put it in a drawer for two days, and two days is what it takes to find out whether a district can place a corridor.

The third ward can. Everybody in it can. There is one slack and one wall at the head of it.

"Half past three on the fourteenth, the woman brought it," he said to the minute, in his own words, which the council required. "**And I want it on the record that I did not go out and look for a place. There was no looking. I asked Rennick, who is forty-one and has been on that wall since he was nine, whether a draw off and on between the fourth and the seventh hour would put water on those steps, and he said it would put them under twice and off twice and leave the second one in for the best part of an hour, and he said it the way a man says the price of bread.**"

The district had a place, and a place in the wall, and a man who could be produced out of it. The whole of the work his sheet had left out was done in about nine minutes by two people in a district office in a lock ward, and it was done correctly.

They did it on the district's own authority. No bell, no claim, no entry, no notice. The ward opened a door in the wall at the third house above the steps, moved six households up into two upper floors and a schoolroom, and brought the school out at half past two, and the parish clerk took a woman in labour down the hill at four in a cart and paid for the cart out of the poor box and has the receipt.

The water came at about twenty to five. The steps went under at about ten past five, came out at about a quarter to seven, and stood an inch deep for four hours. Nobody was hurt. Nothing was lost but an afternoon's work, a cart's hire and the price of eleven candles.

**The good case is one sentence and he wrote it into the minute in those words, because the council required the finding to be in the clerk's own hand and he wanted it to be findable:**

*The district can say what it did, and why, and on what day, and can produce the paper, and could take it to anybody in this basin tomorrow; and the district cannot say who told it.*

---

He had never had a district do anything on its own authority that he could put a minute on. He had, in seven years, four minutes he was proud of, and three of them were about a boundary, and the fourth was about a wall that came down in a gale and stayed down and cost the district nine pounds it had not budgeted because nobody had ever written the rule that says who pays for a wall that a gale takes.

Then, on the night of the fourteenth, about ten o'clock, with the water a foot deep four hundred yards away and nothing happening on the steps, the district clerk of the third ward stood in a schoolroom with thirty-nine children in it and a lamp on a barrel and did the thing that made it a district act rather than a piece of luck.

He wrote it out, in longhand, on the back of a requisition for thirty-nine candles, in about nine minutes, and he read it out to the room, and he asked three people to sign it, and two of them signed it and the third was a woman of sixty-eight who said she would sign anything for this ward and he made her write her name in full.

*Fourteenth day of Drownmonth, about ten at night, the schoolroom. The ward has put the lower room out of use, and the six households at the third house and above are in the two upper floors, and the water came at twenty minutes to five as the sheet said it would, and the wall is the coal steps, and the place was put on the wall by Rennick, who is forty-one and has been on it since he was nine, and the reading was brought into this office by a person whose name is not on it, and there is nobody else to ask.*

The third signature is the whole of the good case and it is on a piece of paper in a schoolroom in a lock ward, and it is the only document in this basin that says a reading was right and a person put water on the right street, and the person is not named on it, and the document is produced without asking anybody, because the district made it for itself on the night, in a room, in front of witnesses.**

---

**The week after it was the longest of his working life and he has said so to nobody, and what made it long was that the district kept trying to be able to name her.**

Within the week he wrote to the drying room asking for the name that goes on a sheet. He asked Rennick whether a man in a yard had seen her come and gone. He went into a firm that had taken out an advertisement about the coal steps in a lock ward, which is not why they advertise there, and asked them whether they had ever bought a sheet off a woman, and the man behind the counter was polite and helpful and said that they bought no sheets and dealt in windows and could not help.

The district did the thing that made it worse, which was to write the whole of it up properly — the sheet, the copy, the two lines of Rennick's answer, the hour, the door in the wall, the cart's receipt — and to lodge it in the minute book with a tab on it, so that it could be produced.

It can be produced. He is the clerk. It is the only thing in the third ward in seven years that anybody has ever come to ask for, and every person who has come has come because they heard about it, and not one of them has been a district, a warden, a Council or a court.

Four of them in nine days. A man pricing a market for the steps. A woman with a notebook who wanted the copy for a shop window. Two clerks from a counting floor who said the same thing in the same words, which is that they wanted to know whether the district would *endorse the sheet*, and he said no, and they said thank you, and went away, and the fact that they said thank you is the part he has not been able to put anywhere.

"They did not want the reading," he said. ""They wanted the district's name next to it. And the district will not put its name next to a thing it cannot check, and that is not a rule we have got, it is just what happened when we tried.""

---

The council rehashed the fourteenth in full and confirmed the action nine to nothing, and then a man named Ferrow said the thing that has been sitting at the back of Amery's own head ever since.

"We'd have done it," Ferrow said. "Two hours later, and we'd have got the schoolroom wet, and it wouldn't have been anybody's fault, and we'd have done it off the same sheet, because there is no other sheet."

"So the third ward has a document proving it was right, produced in front of nine men who voted on it, entered in a minute book with a spine on it, available to anybody who comes to the office and asks," said Amery, writing it out. "**And it has no cause. It has a corridor and an hour and a woman who will not be named in it, and the district cannot say to a council, a firm, a newspaper or a court: here is why we did what we did, here is the paper, and here is the person who told us."

It is the only district in this basin that could produce a record of a correct act and the only district in this basin that could not produce a person.

He wrote the letter in the last week of the month and read it out to the council at its next meeting and they approved every word of it without one amendment, which is the highest thing that has happened to him in seven years.

*To the reader who came to this office on the fourteenth of Drownmonth. The district has your sheet and a copy of it and has had it since that day. The district placed it at the coal steps and the water came at twenty minutes to five, as you said. Nobody was hurt.*

*The district wishes to ask one question and it is not a complaint. May the district put your name on its copy, so that the next time a sheet comes into this office there is a person in it whom the district can go and ask whether it is still standing there?*

---

And the half of it that he cannot get out from under, which is that the district asked its own men afterwards whether the coal steps are the only place in the slack that goes first, and the answer was no.

They are the first wall because they are the lowest, and because they are stone, and because nobody has ever built on them. If the next one had come up four inches higher and softer — and the four yards above the steps are exactly that — the district would have had a street instead of a wall, and a street is four hundred yards and nine doorways, and a district does not empty a street on a sheet that names none of them.

The third ward got the right answer out of a corridor because its low ground had one name in it. Every low ground in this basin is a street.

That is not a criticism of the reader and it is not a criticism of the ward. It is the reason the letter is in the post. The ward that had the wall was saved by a thing it owns and nobody else has, and it wrote that in its minute, and the minute goes on to ask any body that issues weather to consider naming a place in it.

And the last thing Rennick said, at the end, when Amery asked whether he would do it again off another one, is the sentence he wrote down last and is not sure he has put in the right words.

He said: "Aye. But I would want to know, first, that nobody else was going to be told the same thing, and there is nobody I could ask that."

The letter went in the ordinary post on the twelfth at twenty to five, at a cost of twopence, out of the district's money, and the district minute book says *Item 7. Letter sent. Unanimous*, and the unanimous is true.
