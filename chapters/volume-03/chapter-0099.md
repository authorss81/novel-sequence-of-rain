# Chapter 99 — The Office That Received Them

The assessor for water scheduling has a good answer for about two thirds of what is put to her and a habit of saying so out loud, and on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-first of Miremonth she said the other third out loud to an empty room, which is a thing she has done perhaps nine times in eleven years and has never once regretted.

The minute wanted the office to which a district's statement of what it is able to do about its water is addressed.

**The answer is that there is no such office, and there has never been one, and the reason there has never been one is that a statement like that is not made to anybody. It is made to a district.**

She wrote it out as she spoke, on a sheet of departmental paper, in the hand she uses for things that will be minuted: *A district's account of its own available responses is a local record. It is received by the district. It is not lodged with this Council, is not indexed, is not copied, and is not held. The Council's Register of standing response was established to carry the season and the district and the date of issue of a stub, and the stub is a request, and a request is not a return.*

Then she stopped writing, and read what she had written, and put a line under it, and then read the item four again.

*State the date of the last statement received from each ward.*

**Which asks her for a date for something that has never happened, eight times, in a form the Board expects to be able to read, and the form expects the dates to be consecutive and the seasons to be in a column, and there is nowhere in that book to write *none*.**

The second column of the printed head says *date of last statement received*. It has said that since the book was set up and it has been empty since the day it was opened, and eight lines carry a date anyway, and the dates are the day the Register wrote them, and nobody has ever been harmed by that, and this week a Board has asked her to certify it.

---

**She went and found the man who can tell her the truth about staffing, because that is what a Deputy for Field Forecasts is for, and because she has never had a difficulty with him in eleven years and he has never once made her feel like a woman asking a man for a favour.**

Corrin Malle came out of a room with a roster under his arm and a bad afternoon on him, and she said the question standing in the corridor, and he said the answer standing in the corridor, and neither of them sat down.

"The index of offices," he said. "It's the Council's. It lists offices of this Council. The nine wards are seats on this Council; they are not offices of it, and their clerks are not officers of it, and nothing a district's own clerk writes is a paper in this building unless a district chooses to send it, and no district has ever chosen to send one, because no district has ever been asked in a way that produced one."

"Right," said Sabin Orle. "Then who holds the district clerks' names?"

"Three places, and two of them are the districts, and the third is a press in the west end that I have never had cause to open, and neither has anybody else, and I would not know what was in it."

"You have never had cause?"

"No, ma'am," said Corrin Malle, and then, in a lower voice and for no reason that anybody in the corridor could be expected to guess at: "Nobody has ever asked for one. That is not a complaint about the department. That is the department."

**And he was gone down the corridor with the roster, and it was not a complaint and it was not a defence, and it was a fact about supply.**

She noted in the margin of her sheet, in pencil, and the pencil is the thing she uses for the parts that are not going in the minute: *Malle, 21st, corridor. He was frightened about something else and this was not it and he gave me the answer anyway and did not once make a condition of it.*

---

**Then she went down to the west end of the records room, which she has not done a great many times in eleven years, and which is four desks, a window, a press and a counter, and which is the quietest room in a building that is not quiet.**

She wanted the wartime book, and the press is a press, and the four desks belong to a young woman of twenty-seven who has been a copy clerk since the third of the Bellweather month and whose whole work in life is making true copies and putting them where they go.

Tovan Iles did not get up. She turned her sheet round so the assessor could see the head rule and the first line of the work, and said, "Which year section, and do you want it with the tabs or without."

"With the tabs, please. I am not going to write anything in it."

"It's a day book, ma'am, not a bound volume, so there aren't tabs. I can put a slip in it and copy the index out for you separately, which takes about a quarter of an hour, or I can put a slip in it and you can have the index off the shelf, which is a lending slip and I can put that in about four minutes."

Sabin Orle stood in the middle of a records room and understood that she had been offered a quarter of an hour and four minutes by a woman who had not been asked for either, and who had worked out in about a second that the assessor was going to want to put her hand on the book.

**"The four minutes," she said. "And you will tell me the name of the officer who made the last entry and I will minute that I asked and did not get it."**

"It is not an officer. That is the finding." Tovan Iles wrote the date and the number on a slip in a fair hand. "There is no wartime district book in that press, ma'am, and I have looked twice. What there is, and what I can give you in the four minutes, is *Daily, district notices received*, and there are eleven years of it in my hand and four years before mine in a man's, and I can tell you the man's name because he signs it himself on the first of the month four times a year, and I cannot tell you what office he was in, because the head does not say, and the head being printed is the whole of the answer to the Board's item four."

**She was right. It was the answer, and it took her eleven seconds, and the assessor wrote it in the minute the same way, and neither of them said thank you, and the copy clerk went back to her third copy of a set of minutes and put the pen down flat and squared the slip against the edge of the desk.**

Sabin Orle read the four years in eleven minutes. It is four lines a week. It is *received from the [x] ward, one sheet, no enclosure* and *received from the [x] ward, nothing this week* and, eleven times in four years, *received from the [x] ward, a request for a form, none held, replied* — and the [x] is always a district and the district in the eleven is always the same district, and the assessor looked at the eleven requests for a form and then read the head again, and put her finger on the word *daily*.

**The book is a record of things received by a Council. Nobody has ever lodged anything with this Council. The eleven requests for a form are the only lines in eleven years that anybody ever sent us, and they are a district asking us for a piece of paper we have never had.**

She wrote one sentence in her own minute, and then read it twice and left it:

*That the Standing Response Register records the issue of a request and the receipt of nothing, and that eleven requests in four years for a form this Council does not hold are the only papers this office has received from any district touching a district's own capability, is a defect in the record and not in any district.*

And then she said, out loud, to a room with one other person in it who was not listening because she was copying:

"That is the best sentence in eleven years and it is about a book nobody has read, and I have been paid to maintain it for six of them."

Nobody thanked anybody in that room. The copy clerk locked the press at five and counted the trays and put a key in her pocket that has been in her pocket since the third of the Bellweather month, which is under twenty-four weeks now, and there are things in a box in the west end of that room and nobody in Veyra could say how many of them, including her.

---

**She went up the hill at about half past five, which she has not done for years, and she went into the maintenance shop because she wanted to know one more thing, and the one thing was this: has this Office ever, in fifty years, physically supplied a district with an instrument to keep a record in?**

A glass shop on a ridge at the end of a wet day, and a foreman of sixty-one who was putting a rack key in her apron when she came in, and who did not stop.

"I want to ask you something as a person who has been in this trade for nineteen years and not as an office," said Sabin Orle, "and I'll tell you which of the two it is before you answer it."

"I'd not answer it at all if you didn't."

"**Has this Office ever sent a district a book, or a form, or a plate, or a rule of any kind, for keeping a record of its own water?**"

**And the foreman put the rack key back in her apron and said, "No," and then made her wait, and then said more.**

"No. And I'll tell you the near thing, because the near thing is why there is no yes. We cut and grind and bed and anneal every plate in this building for nine ward towers, and we have never cut one for a district, and the reason is not the Office, it is us. **Nobody ever came to a shop and asked for a book. A shop is a shop. If you want a book you go to a printer and they will print a hundred for you and cannot tell you which of the hundred is true.**"

"And the day book."

"Day book's got the lehr in it and the rack in it and a column for the hour, and it goes back to the year the shop took the tower work, and there is not one line in it in forty years about a district's water, and if you asked me for a page of it I would let you look at a page and I would not let you take the book, and that is not a records rule, that is because a book of work done is not a book of what happened." She shut the drawer of the rack. "**I have a book in here with a Director's circular in it, copied out entire on a Sunday, with the seal and the date, because a thing that is done to you ought to be written down at the time it is done to you. And I have three books in this basin that nobody has ever asked for, and I have a day book, and the day book's a fourth, and none of them is a record and there is no form on which any of them could be one.**"

"Which three?"

"A keeper's up the hill with a tower book, a shed foreman on the south bank with a level book going back to 1888, and the ordinary minutes of a ward that are not minutes." She took her coat off the peg and did not put it on. "And you can add the fourth, and I'd say the fourth's the honest one, because it's the only one of the four that's ever had anything put in it on purpose. It's got no weather in it at all. It's got that circular in it and three lines about it."

Sabin Orle did the arithmetic on the step, in the rain, with her pencil, in about eleven seconds, and it is worth setting down exactly because it is the reason the whole of the next month happens:

**Three uncompellable books. One drawer copy in a nineteen-year-old's hand. Nine districts that have been asked what they can do and have answered in nine ways, none of which is in a book this Council can send for. And a minute item asking for the office to which a district's statement is addressed, which cannot be answered, and the reason it cannot be answered is that the answer is a person, and persons are not indexed, and no office in this city has ever sent for one.**

"Thank you, Mrs. Doff," she said, on the step, in the rain, meaning it.

"Don't," said the foreman, and put her coat on her, which is a thing she does and has never once done for an officer of the Council. "If you write it in a minute it'll be a minute, and then somebody'll be able to say you had it from an office, and I don't want a district to be told the Office knows I've got a book with nothing in it but a circular."
