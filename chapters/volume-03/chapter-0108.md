# Chapter 108 — Declined

The fourth ward's page came into the drying room above the Ninth Baths on the Friday morning in a boy's hand, at about twenty past eight, and Sable Orin read it standing up, twice, and then went and got the fourth notebook, which is a foolscap book with a limp cover, and opened it at the first clean page, and did what she has done for two years, which is to write the words down as they were given to her and then never to touch them again.

*DECLINED. WHO. WHY, IN THEIR WORDS. DATE.*

*29th M. Fourth ward, by its own district office, out of its own minute, on its own board, in its own name, 8 to 1 with the one minuted.*

*Declined: to fill in the Council's form of the 21st. To be entered in any list, return or index in respect of a reading. To have a reading of its own printed by anybody. To act on a reading that names a corridor and an hour and no place.*

*In their words, and the whole of the second paragraph is theirs and not mine:*

*"A district is not obliged to act on a reading it cannot check, and a reading that names a corridor and an hour and no place is not a reading. It is a piece of the work left out, and the ward that acts on it is doing the reading itself, badly, at speed, in the dark, and is then told by anybody who likes to come that it was given no notice at all."*

*Date the twenty-ninth of Miremonth. The ward's own name at the top of it. The ward said no to me before it said no to the Council, which I did not know and which does not matter and which I am not going to pretend mattered.*

She wrote the rest of it out — the whole page, both paragraphs, the eighth-to-one and the name of the one — because the fourth book is not a book of the words that were right. It is a book of what people said when they were right.

**And then she sat with the pen and went back over the eleven weeks of it, and the working is worth having because it is short.**

*Nobody has asked me whether a reading may be printed. Eleven people asked me for a sheet in eleven weeks and ten of them asked in a way that meant it, and I printed for all eleven.*

*No district has ever asked me whether it may be listed, and I have listed a district. I do not know where four of my eleven sheets go each week. I have never asked, and the not asking has been in the same hand as the asking, and the two of those are the same sentence, and I have had it in my book for two years.*

*No district has ever refused me anything, because there was nothing to refuse, because I never put a place on a sheet and there is nothing in a corridor and an hour to refuse.*

**And the fourth ward has done it, in writing, in its own name, on its own board, at its own cost, in a form it is not going to fill in — and it has become the first party in this basin to refuse me a thing, and it cost the ward a meeting and a dissent and a shilling a week, and the ward does not get one hour of weather out of it.**

---

**So she published it, on the Friday morning of the thirtieth, at about half past nine, on the tannery side of Tannery Walk, on the shutter, underneath her own hand and above the seventh disagreement, and it is the largest thing that has ever gone on that shutter, and it went up in a fair hand and it is not a notice.**

*THE FOURTH WARD HAS DECLINED. Their words, not mine. It is on their own board in their own name and it is copied here because a thing on one board is a thing one street has read.*

*They will not fill in a form. They will not be listed. They will not have a reading printed. And they will not act on a reading that names a corridor and an hour and no place.*

*And I do not agree with one clause of it, and I am putting my name under that, and they have not asked me to.*

*S. Orin, counter-reader. My clause is the second one. I do not think a ward should be listed or not listed on its own board. I think a refusal that stays on the ward's own board has not happened, it has only been said, and in a month the Council will have a form with a box and the ward's name in it again and there will be no evidence that the ward ever said no, because nobody will have heard it say so.*

*Which means my sheet is a private arrangement between a reader and a wall, and mine is the one that is not consented to, and I am not withdrawing it.*

**And then, at the bottom, in the same hand and the same paint, two lines that took her a long time and that she did not soften:**

*I have eleven sheets out in this city and I cannot account for four of them and I have never asked a soul. The fourth ward's page says a district is not obliged to act on a reading it cannot check, and there is no clause in it about a reader, and there ought to be one, and I have had a reading of mine used commercially for months and I did not know and I am not going to find out this week who is doing it.*

*I am a reader. I am not a district. I do not get a vote.*

---

**It came back inside two hours, and it came back in a boy's hand, and it was from the fourth ward, and it was four lines, and it was not rude and it was not softened and it did not ask for anything to be taken down.**

*From the fourth ward's office to the reader. 30th M.*

*Our terms permit copying by hand at the ward's office, at the ward's cost. They do not permit a reader to write the ward's words out with her own four lines under them and put the ward's name at the top of the lot.*

*The ward does not object to the reading being true. The ward objects to the practice, and the practice is not new, and it is the same practice with the ends the other way round, and in a month somebody else will be doing it to a reader and a reader will be doing it to somebody else, and we would like to be the first to say so on our own board and not on yours.*

*We will not ask you to take it down. We never ask anybody to take anything down, that is our entire position, and you know it is.*

*— V. Sly, clerk, fourth ward.*

---

**Sable Orin read that standing in the road on the tannery side of Tannery Walk at about half past ten on the Friday morning, in the rain, with about eleven people reading over her shoulder, and she did not take the shutter down, and she wrote the ward's four lines into the fourth notebook in full, exactly, word for word, with the date, and then she did the only thing she had left to do with it, which was to rule under the entry in the same hand:**

*Kept. Not published on a shutter without asking. This reader would like the record to show that the fourth ward said so in writing first, on a Thursday, on its own board, at its own cost, and that this reader put it on a wall on a Friday without asking it, and that the ward's objection is correct, and that the objection is in this book and not on a wall because a reader does not get a wall of her own.*

**And she did not withdraw the four lines, and the two things are both on the shutter, and the shutter is two feet by three and has been painted in three hands and written on in two and has never once been improved, corrected off the face, or taken down, and it is the only document in this basin that says a district no and a reader no in the same paint on the same afternoon.**

---

**And at about ten to six on the Friday evening a woman of sixty-one came up the hill road to the maintenance shop on a bicycle she was too old for, with a brown paper parcel under her arm that was not tied, and Marek Vale was on the bench under the north window with a lamp over it and two commissions' worth of paper and a client who had gone.**

"Ten to," he said, looking at the clock on the wall, which is four seconds fast and has been four seconds fast since the spring.

"Then I'm early, and I've been early for thirty years, which is a ward's habit and not mine." She came as far as the bench. "You'll not have the book and I've not brought the book. I've brought one page, and I've brought it in a parcel, because a parcel is a thing you can put down."

"Have a chair."

"I've a parcel and I'm not staying." She put it on the bench and did not take her coat off, and he did not offer to take the parcel, which is the only thing he did right all week and he has not told anybody that either.

"Twenty-two years," she said. "One page. You've asked me for a fortnight's worth of interviews and what you actually wanted was a page, and I'm going to give you a page, and I am not going to tell you what it is, and that is the whole arrangement and I'd like it said out loud in a room while we're both standing here."

"Then say it out loud."

"**There is one of these in the world and it is in a house and not in a press, and you'll have a page of it and not the book, and when you've had the page you will not come up this hill road again about it, and if you do I'll not see you.** And in return you will not put my name in a place where somebody can be asked about me, and I don't mean my name in your book, I mean my name in a press or a list or a minute or an index, and I am not a person who can be asked about because I have never in my life held an office of anything." She did not sit down. "**Those are the terms and they are not negotiable and I have had them since before you were in the room.**"

"Agreed."

"You'll want to shake on it and I don't, and there's no reason we should."

"There's no reason we should."

---

**She untied nothing, because nothing was tied, and she took one sheet out of the parcel, and put it on the bench face up beside his lamp, and put her hand flat on it, the way people do.**

"It is the fourteenth of Greengage, three years back, which was a Tuesday, and it is in a hand I have not seen since and it is not mine."

The sheet is a duty clerk's copy of a reading brought into the sixth ward's office. It is a strip of district paper and it is headed with the printed head of a book that has been taken out of a cupboard in a terrace, and it says:

*Corridor, below the north wall, fourth to seventh hour, draw off and on. Cannot say where. Brought in by a reader, 06:40.*

And under it, in the same hand, in the column for what was done with it:

*Entered. No place given and none asked.*

**"The Office's sheet for that Tuesday says ordinary," said Hester Skeat. "It says ordinary and it says nothing else and there is a copy of it in a tower four miles from here in a book that isn't a record. There was a corridor on that Tuesday at a quarter to seven in the morning and nobody in this basin was told, and I wrote it down at about seven o'clock, and I wrote *no place given and none asked*, which is the only sentence in that book that is any good and I have never once known why I wrote it.**"

"And who brought it."

"**No.**"

"Sable Orin may have—"

"**No,**" said Hester Skeat, and put two fingers on the sheet, not on the writing. "You'll not do that to me and I'll not do it to you. **I've been the only copy of that book for two years and I did not keep it to be asked about by a man with a lamp, and I never knew a name in twenty-two years and I'm sixty-one and it's too late for a name to be any use to anybody including me.** There are three hundred and about sixty days in that book that say nobody brought anything, and the young man who came twice a week for two years stopped on a Tuesday in the spring and there's no line saying he stopped, and I could tell you which of those days I would give a great deal to see, and I am not going to tell you which one it is, and you are not going to ask me, and that is the third of the terms and it's the one I'd have made last.**"

The rain came on the north window. He put his hand flat on the bench on the other side of the sheet and did not touch it.

"There are more than seven of those in this basin and I have stopped counting and I can put seven names to them," he said. "A keeper's tower book. A shed man's level book that goes back to 1888. A shop foreman's day book with a Director's circular in it. A sheet clerk's duty book in a house. A plasterer's day book in a yard. A drawer in a ward office with four hundred sheets in it and a label on the drawer. And a drawer on a middle desk on the second floor of a firm with three forms in it and a glass weight on top. And there is no name for any of them and no form and no bell and no office, and in about a fortnight somebody is going to ask me what to call the whole of it, and I have got until the end of next week before I have to have an answer, and I have not got one."

"You've not," said Hester Skeat. "**And that's the other thing you've not got and I am the only person in this basin who can tell you, so you can have that for nothing, because it costs me nothing and it's true.**

"**You cannot call it anything, because the moment you call it a thing it is a place, and a place is a thing that can be asked for.** And then you will have to write down what is in it, and what is in it is nine hundred entries in a book on a dresser, and a level book that goes back to 1888, and a woman's reading of a river four times a day in a tower, and a boy's day book in a yard, and a foreman's day book in a shop, and that is five people, and four of them would find out what you had written about them, and the fifth would rather it was burnt. **So you will not call it anything, and you will go on asking people one at a time, and the whole of it will stay ordinary, and you will get to the end of your life having helped about a dozen people with a thing they had in a drawer, and not one of them will ever know it was an arrangement.**"

She put the sheet back into the parcel and folded the top over and did not tie it.

"That's the whole of what I came to say and I'm not going to say it twice and I'm going now, and it's a bicycle and I'm sixty-one and it's uphill in the wet."

He walked her to the gate, and at the gate she stopped and looked back up the hill at a shop with a lamp on under the north window, and said one more thing, and it was not to him and it was to a ridge.

"**The Tuesday after that one, the fourteenth, there was a blank day and I wrote *nothing brought this day*, and I could be wrong, and I was not.**"

And then she got on the bicycle and went down the hill road at the speed of a woman of sixty-one in the wet, and did not look back, and he stood in the yard in the rain with a brown paper parcel on the bench under a lamp and one sheet of district paper in it, in a hand that had copied nine hundred entries out of other people's hands for twenty-two years, and the line at the foot of that sheet in a fair round hand that was not hers, and he read it once and put it away again without writing anything on it. The inside of his left cuff has three blocks of pencil in it and there is no room for a fourth, and there is no column in this basin that says what you do with a day when nobody came.

**Nobody was hurt this month. A district said no in writing and put it on its own wall, and a reader put it on hers without asking and was told so in writing four hours later, and a woman of sixty-one came up a hill and gave away one leaf out of nine hundred and took the rest of the book home with her in a parcel that was not tied, and a ward's notice went out in a form on a Monday and a ward's notice went out on a wall on a Friday, and the lamp in the ninth ward tower was out and would stay out until somebody put money in a tin.**
