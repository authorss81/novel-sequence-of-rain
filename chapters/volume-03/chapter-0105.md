# Chapter 105 — The Book That Is For Afterwards

The boy from the ninth ward's own district came up the hill road at about half past seven on the Tuesday morning of the twenty-seventh with a bicycle and a folded sheet of paper and a list of three questions, and he was seventeen, and it took Petrie about four minutes to establish that he was not going to be told anything and about another four to establish that he did not mind, which Petrie considers a fault in the boy and the making of him.

"Mr. Petrie, I'm sent from the ward office to ask whether we can have a copy of the level book, made properly, with a man sworn to it, so it's evidence."

"You can have a copy of the level book this morning. That'll be the lad with the tray. It'll take him till about two."

"That's — right. Thank you." The boy got his pencil out. "Can I ask you the three questions, then? It's only three."

**"You can ask."**

"Question one. How do we know the level book is right?"

Petrie put his chain over his shoulder and did not stop what he was doing, which was getting the kettle going at the end of the bench, because a kettle is the first thing anybody who comes up that hill intends to put on and putting one on is how a shed foreman says stay as long as you like.

"You don't," he said. "Nobody knows. That's the answer to the first one and it's the whole answer. **A level book is a record of a man reading a staff in a well with a chain, and the reading's right to the foot, and there's four people in this basin that could tell you how far off he was and three of them are me.**"

"You?"

"I've never had it wrong by more than an inch and I've had it wrong by an inch about nine hundred times and it's never once been in my favour. You can't be a man who checks a district's figures if the district's figures are going to be used against the district, because a man in a shed with a kettle on is the only kind of man in this basin that can't be bought and can't be got at, and the day he's got a name and a wage and an office he's got a reason to write down what somebody upstairs wanted."

**"I'm not on a roll of anything," he added, without any weight. "There's no name on this shed, and there's no name on the order, and there has not been one for thirty years, and I have told a stranger that before and I'll tell you now and it isn't a mystery. It's just that if your district ever wants to be able to say the water came off the reach when a man of mine says it did, then the man had better be a man that nobody can go and ask a favour of.**"

The boy wrote that down, which Petrie thought was probably right of him.

---

**"Question two, Mr. Petrie. If a district wanted to write down what it can do about its water — the pump order and the gang and the reach and the shelter — what would it write?"**

Petrie sat down on the end of the bench, which he does not do while he is talking to anybody, and it took him about a minute and a half.

"It'd write the gang and the order and the pumps," he said. "That's three lines and they're all true and any district could do it. And then it'd come to the reach, and there's no line for that, and the reason there's no line for it is that the reach is *forty minutes*, and a man takes forty minutes or he doesn't take it, and there's no way of writing a man down."

"You did it," said the boy. "For the sixth ward. In a claim, this month. The tide one — the lighter's, down on the low side. He wrote down that he would have done forty tons of stone in it, forty tons at six hours, eleven days of masons."

"And a heavy cold sea didn't do that; the tide got in the way." Petrie nodded. "He could write it because it's stone and it's stone every time and there's four lighters and a chain barge and a lighterman and two men and they're the same four lighters every year, and a man can name those. **You cannot name a gang of five in a wet quarter.**"

"So how does a ward write down what it can do?"

"It can't," said Petrie, "and I'd not write it if it could, and here's the whole of the reason in a bit of language, and you'll want to write this part down."

He put the chain down.

"**Forty minutes is worth a hundred and sixty yards of somebody's low ground. And a hundred and twenty when it's short. And the difference between those two numbers is not forty minutes, it's what's in the water at the top of it, and if the man in the well is watching a staff he's not watching the top of the reach, and if the man on the reach is watching the water he's not watching the staff.** There's four wanted on it. Two of the four watching a number. And no book in the world has ever had room for a man watching water."

---

**"Then what's in your book, Mr. Petrie? Because you've got nine hundred of them."**

"**The water.**" He turned round and got the level book off the bench and did not open it, and held it, which is a thing shed men do with books. "Nine hundred and about forty pages of it, and the water's in every one, and the plan's in none. The plan's in the heads of about four people and two of them are dead."

He said that last part without stopping, the way a man says a thing he has said to himself, and the boy wrote it down, and Petrie watched him write it and then said:

"Dead is the right word. Don't you put that down in anything for anybody else. I'm not being grim. **Two men who used to plan that reach are dead and there's no list and no book and nobody's asked me for it, and the reason there's no list is that it was in their heads, and the reason it was in their heads is that a reach is not a thing you can write, it's a thing you know.**" He put the book down. "And that's how a ward loses one without anybody noticing. Not a day. A *capacity*. And nobody knows it's gone until the day somebody wants to know what the district could do, and by then there's a man of sixty-eight on a bench and a boy with a bicycle, and that's the two of them."

---

**He said the last thing at about eleven, with the boy's copy finished and drying on the bench under a weight, and he said it looking out of the door at a yard and a chain and about nine hundred pounds of shoring timber in four iron bands which has been in that shed since the sixth of the Bellweather month and is in a second season of being in it, and which is not the same counter as the wall on the low side of the ninth ward's reach that is in a third season and wants forty tons of stone.**

"Question three," he said. "You never said what it was."

"Question three's whether Mr. Vale would check our copy."

"**No.**"

"He'd do it for nothing."

"He'd do it for nothing and he'd do it wrong," said Petrie, "and he knows it, and so do I, and so does the man who wrote you that letter." He pulled the tray round. "A man with a glass comes at it with a glass, and a glass wants a lamp and a dark room and about four hours at a stretch, and the fourth ward can't afford a lamp and hasn't had one lit since anybody's memory. And what he reads is a sequence, and a sequence is not a level, and there's a sentence about that in a Director's circular that got pinned up on a Sunday and that I'd advise you to read because it's four paragraphs and every one of them's true and the third one is the one that matters to you."

"What does the third one say?"

"**That this Office will not cut and grind and anneal a piece of glass for anybody but a ward tower, and won't let anybody use its stock or its furnaces or the work of any of its people to do it, because a piece of plate in a private hand is a reading no public body can check.**" He shut the tray. "Your district is a ward and your district has a tower. So on paper you've got a door. And in practice the door's got about two years and a glasswork line behind it, and the fourth ward's had no legible notice in this chamber's time. That's not my business. That's just what a Director can do with a seal and a date and no form, and it's in print, and it's four paras, and there's no appeal in it."

---

**The boy left at about two with the copy under his arm, and the lad who made it had been paid one and six out of the shed's own money for a day's work and Petrie had taken it out of the same tin he takes his own out of, and there is a receipt for it nowhere, because the shed keeps no book of receipts. It keeps the level book, and the level book is for the water.**

And a district somewhere in this basin has now been told, by a man of sixty-eight who would rather you did not have his name on anything, that it cannot write down what it can do, and that the reason is two men being dead, and that the reason nobody noticed is that nobody came up the hill.

**The boy went down the hill road at about two with a copy of nine hundred and forty pages drying under a tray weight, and four inches of writing on a folded sheet, and no idea whatever that on the Monday of the same week the ninth ward had answered a Council form in a fair working hand, out of a form, a pump house, a level book, a tower column and the head of the woman who wrote it, and had thereby issued the first document of its own life and issued it as a copy; and that on the Thursday a fourth ward was going to put a refusal on its own board in its own name and was not going to send a form at all.**

**Two wards, four days apart. One of them has a return in a Council tray on a Monday. The other has a wall in a goods yard and a shilling a week refused in writing, and by the end of the month the second of those is going to be the colder place to be standing in, and neither of the two women is going to hear it from me.**
