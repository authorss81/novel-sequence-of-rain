# Chapter 102 — What The Tin Will Do

There is no column in the ninth ward's district book for money.

There is a column for the date and a column for the pump hours and a column for the gang and a column for the reach, and a column for weather received, and there are five of them and they have been there since before her father had the job, and there is nowhere in the whole of it for seven and six, or for a shilling a week, or for the fact that a tower lamp is lit on a Saturday night and put out on a Tuesday.

So she counts the tin twice on the table with the district clerk standing at the other side of it not helping, because a man standing on the other side of a table helps.

**Four and six in the district tin on the Saturday morning of the twenty-fourth, and that is the whole of the ninth ward's public weather service, and the arithmetic is the arithmetic.**

It is worth setting out in full, because every part of the rest of the week comes out of it and none of it is a feeling.

The Council's ledger paid the ninth ward seven and six on the eighteenth, for the window the ward gave up on purpose, at the Exchange's price, with the ward's own figure of one and four refused in a column beside it. Out of that went the keeper's evening hours for a week and the oil for a week, being one shilling and three and six, and the lamp was lit on the nineteenth and the tin carried three shillings. The firm has paid the ward a shilling a week since the twenty-ninth of Drownmonth, and the second of those came in on the twentieth, so there is four shillings in the tin. She counted it twice with the district clerk standing at the other side of the table not helping, and she did not write it down, because there is no column.

**The oil is bought on a Saturday by the district's man, because that is when the shops are open and because he goes up on the Saturday for the pump. It is three and six.**

"Three and six," said Neris Kade. "And if it were four and one in a wet week I could not buy four and one in a wet week. We shall have it on Saturday and the can will do us to the Tuesday, and after the Tuesday it is nobody's lamp."

"It's been raining nine days," the man agreed, from the doorway, and said nothing else, because he has carried a tin up that ladder twice since the eighteenth and has not once been thanked and does not expect to be and is not going to be told a thing he already knows.

**So on the Saturday the twenty-fourth the tin goes down to fourpence, and the oil goes up the ladder in a can, and the lamp is lit on the Saturday night, and it is a plain lamp behind a glass that cannot show a state, and from four streets away a man who has never seen it lit would say a lamp is up, and a man who has seen it every week of his working life would say nothing, and both of those are correct.**

And on the Tuesday the twenty-seventh the shilling comes and is not spent, because the keeper's evening hours for that week cannot be paid out of a shilling and a can, and the tin stands at a shilling and fourpence, and the can is done.

**And she knew it on the Saturday, the moment the can was carried up the ladder, and she did not buy a second one, and she could not have bought a second one, and oil is three and six and the tin had fourpence in it on the Monday, and that is the end of the arithmetic and there is nobody to send it to.**

---

**And then the form came, and it came on the Saturday, and it is the only thing in four weeks that anybody has sent the ninth ward that was actually a form.**

It is one sheet, printed, with a minute number on it, and it has been four weeks in coming and it came from the assessor's office by the ordinary post with a covering line that is fifteen words long and entirely courteous: *That your district state in writing what it is able to do about its water.*

The box is four inches deep. She measured it with a thumbnail, twice, and it is four inches, and the four inches are ruled into two columns, and the head of the left-hand column says *resources* and the head of the right-hand column says *personnel*, and there is nothing else on the sheet, and no schedule is asked for, and no sum is asked for, and there is no box for a figure of any kind on either side.

**It is the first form this Council has ever sent the ninth ward that is not a return, and it is a quarter the size of a return, and it can be answered in an evening, and it is going to take her the best part of a week, and she knew that on the Saturday afternoon and sat down and did the left-hand column before she had her coat off.**

*Clearing rota. The low side, the two rain gardens and the back lanes. Two hours, Tuesdays and Thursdays, in the wet. Six men and a water steward. Marda Loon keeps it in her own hand at the pump house and has kept it since her father's time, and it is in a book, and nobody has asked for it.*

*Pump order. Written by me, on the eighth of the Bellweather month, at about ten at night, off a notice with a number and an hour and no place in it, and the district has never once been told by anybody that it cannot check it.*

*Reach. The ward has a gauge in a stilling well on the freight apron with a glass datum in it cut by M. Vale, in a hand-bedded brass surround, at the ward's cost, and it is checked four times a day against the firm's free sheet by the tower keeper in the fifth column of a book that is not a record, and the two of them agree and disagree and the difference is written down every time.*

*Personnel. Six with a water steward. Nine who can stand in a doorway for four hours.*

**And then she came to the ninth name and stopped, and sat with the pen off the paper for about a quarter of an hour, and then wrote this, in the right-hand column, in her own working hand, and it is the first refusal the ninth ward has ever entered in anything:**

*The district can put six men on a pump and a steward with a lantern. The district has nine people who can stand in a doorway for four hours. The district holds no list of who those nine are and is not going to send one, and the reason is that a list of nine names is not a schedule, it is a set of nine people we are fond of, and the day it is in a form it is a set of nine people we are answerable to, and there is no tenth person in the ninth ward and no form in this city that knows there is no tenth person.*

**And she signed it, and dated it, and it went in the post on the Monday, and it cost twopence, and the district book has no column for a form that has been sent either, so that is not written down either.**

---

**The lamp went out on the evening of the twenty-seventh at about the half hour, the way it had been going out every evening until the nineteenth, and the way a man puts a lamp out when the can is done. The man who put it out was sixty-three or sixty-four and had been paid an evening's hours twice since the ledger money and not for the week that began on the twenty-seventh.**

He stood in the dark tower in the rain and did the thing that has no record in it at all, which is to stand where the light would be, for the length of a smoke. The plate in the feed seat was warm to within a degree of his palm and he has never once opened a holder in his life and never once looked at what is under the plate, and there is a spare boxed on the shelf under the seat with the word *spare* on it in his own hand, and neither of those is going to be looked at by anybody this month.

**Down on the low ground, on the same evening, four hundred and forty people had their notice in four places, and all four of them were hand-copied and all four of them were made by a woman of fifty-eight who brings tea up a ladder at eight and has never been thanked, and one of them is in the plate glass case at the school gate where two hundred and forty children are on the roll and forty of them walk up from these blocks, and the lamp in the tower above them had shown a light for eight days and then showed nothing at all, and the two facts are not connected and there is no form anywhere that would say they were.**


**What she did on the Monday instead was write a line in the district book in the pump hours column, because it is the only column that takes a man's hours, and it is not a lamp column and the day book does not have one.**

*27th. Lamp out at half past six. No oil. Keeper paid to the 27th.*

And that is the entire record of a district's public notice being switched off in the ninth ward, and it is in the wrong column, and a man a hundred and forty years from now reading the district book for a history of the ward's flood season will find it between two pump entries and will not know what it is, and there is nothing anybody can do about that and nobody in this basin is going to try.

She did not go and say so. She went and stood at the school gate at about twenty to eight on the Wednesday morning with her hands in her pockets and looked at a lit plate glass case with a shutterful of hours in it, which is where the notice has lived since the twenty-ninth of Drownmonth, and the children came up out of the low blocks in the dark, forty of them, and forty-one was the number counted outside those doors at eighteen hundred on the eighth of the Bellweather month and it is a different number and it is right too.
