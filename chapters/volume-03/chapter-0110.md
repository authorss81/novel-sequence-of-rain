# Chapter 110 — The Line In The File

The young man came in on the Monday morning of the third of the month at about ten past ten, and he was nineteen, and he had a card, and Verna Sly put him in the column before she put down the card.

*Third. A young man off a firm's second floor, wants to see the ward file for a date. Card, four questions, no hurry about it, and no hurry is the thing.*

She is thirty-eight and has been the fourth ward's clerk for eleven years and keeps the column because in eleven years she has learned that the only difference between a district office and a queue at a counter is the column. The column has been the same ruled page in the same book since her second year and it has three headings and no fourth, and the third heading is *what he did not say*, and she added that one herself.

"Ward file," he said. "Eleventh of Sleetmonth, two winters back."

"There's a file."

"May I see it?"

"You may. It's not mine and it's not the district's, it belongs to a clerk who has been dead eleven years, and anybody may come to this office and look at it. That is in our minute, in my own words, and I voted for it." She said it exactly the way she had said it to a man with a card and a shilling a week about eleven days before, and she heard herself, and she let him hear it. "What are you after it for."

He was ready for it. That was the thing she noted afterwards. He had an answer ready, and the answer was the wrong shape, because it was the shape of a reason rather than the shape of a man.

"Our firm has a request outstanding on a district paper of that date. We would like to establish whether the Council holds a copy."

"Now that," said Verna Sly, "is the wrong way round."

---

**She made him wait, and she made him wait by doing the ordinary work, and while she did it she went back over the fortnight in which this ward was the only thing in the basin that anybody had come to look at.**

A firm had offered a shilling a week and been refused in writing. A reader had written the ward's words out on her own shutter with four lines under them and her name at the top of the lot. A wall in a goods yard had carried the ward's refusal since ten past eight on the thirtieth and had a queue on the step by half past nine. Twenty-odd people had come to the counter in nine days and she had seen every one of them, and in nine days not one person had come to look at a file.

"Third," she said, "eleventh of Sleetmonth, and the hand is not your man's."

"No, ma'am."

"It's a dead man's and it's eleven years old, and there's nine years of them in that drawer, and the nine years are nothing but the day and the week, because that is all that clerk wrote. If you want a reading you'll not find one. If you want a form you'll find a hundred and they'll be the same form." She took the drawer out. "You'll have to stand there and read it, because I'm not reading it out to a man off a second floor and I'm not copying it either, and that's the terms of this office and not a mood."

He read it standing. It took him about four minutes and he read it the way a clerk reads, all the way down each line.

---

*11th Sleetmonth. Low side to the ninth ward, three trips, one boat, a man and a lad, lamp. Eleven. Sheet to follow.*

*25th Sleetmonth. Ninth ward's sheet not come. Wrote. No reply.*

**And that is the whole of it, and it is two lines out of nine years in a dead clerk's hand, and Verna Sly had read them once, in her third year, in a drawer, on a wet afternoon, and had thought nothing of them, and had gone on thinking nothing of them every day since, which is a thing she is now going to think about for some time.**

Eleven people. Off the low side. To the ninth ward, which is four miles and a lock downriver and has four hundred and forty people on it. Three trips in one boat with a lamp at night, in Sleetmonth, in a freeze, because something was going to happen on that low side, and nobody in the fourth ward wrote down what it was, because nobody in the fourth ward was told what it was, because the notice that came named a corridor and hours and no place.

*Sheet to follow.* The sheet did not follow. The ward wrote about it on the twenty-fifth and there was no reply, and a clerk wrote *no reply* in a file, and that is where it stopped, and the file has been in a drawer in a goods yard off the north road for eleven years and three weeks and nobody has ever come and asked.

"He said it would follow," said the young man.

"Twenty-five lines down the file there is a man of my office's predecessor writing to somebody about it, so it did not, and he wrote to them, and they did not write back." She put her hand on the drawer. "That's a piece of paper, that is, in this drawer, about eleven people who are alive somewhere or were, and I could not tell you the name of one of them, and I have been the clerk of this ward for eleven years."

**Which is the sentence that put it on a list, and it went on the list because she said it out loud to a stranger, and she has thought about that since and cannot decide whether it was the shop talking or the office.**

---

He asked her to copy the two lines out. She said no. He asked again, and then he said the thing that is the whole of him, and he said it pleasantly.

"Our firm pays for copying."

"No," said Verna Sly. "This ward does, and it is twopence a sheet, and the terms on the board say any person may come to the office and copy it at the ward's cost, and it may not be printed for sale. So you may copy it, and I shall charge this office for it, and you may tell your firm that is what happened, and I should like you to tell your firm that."

He copied it himself, standing at her counter, in about four minutes, and he did it well, which she noticed and did not like.

"Who sent you?" she said, while he was writing.

"Our firm."

"Which is not a person. Which firm, and who inside it, and is the firm doing this for itself or for a customer or for a district?"

He did not look up. "The firm is doing it for me."

"Then that is the first answer anybody has given me in nine days that I believe, and I believe it because you said it too quickly, and I am not going to say so, and you may write that down as an insult." She put her hand on the column. "And you did not tell me who you were working for, and I did not ask you, and that is on me and it goes in the book this afternoon, and the day somebody comes in here with a better reason I am going to be a woman who let a boy look at a dead clerk's handwriting without asking, and there is nobody in this ward I can ask about it because the district will not be told."

---

**She wrote it up afterwards, in the column, with the hour, and then read back what she had written, which is a thing she has never done in eleven years and did that afternoon because a file nobody reads back is not a record, and a thing that is not a record is a thing a district cannot complain with.**

*Third, 11 a.m. to 12.20. A young man of nineteen off a firm's second floor, card of the firm, four questions. Asked for the ward file at the 11th of Sleetmonth. Gave him the file. He stood and read it nine years. He asked me to copy two lines; I said the ward would charge him and he copied it himself at the counter.*

*What I did not say: I did not ask who he was working for, and I did not ask whether the firm had a district, and he did not offer either, and I let a stranger read a dead clerk's hand in this office because I wanted somebody to want to see it. That is not a refusal and it is not a decision and I have put it in the column because there is nowhere else in this ward to put it.*

**And then, at the foot of the page, under the ruling, in the same hand and smaller, the thing she has thought about every day since and would not have written if the week had been a quieter one:**

*I have been the clerk of this ward eleven years and there are eleven people in the ninth ward that this ward put there, and I could not tell you the name of one of them, and there are two lines in a dead man's hand that say they went, and that is the only paper in the world that says so.*

She did not put the file back in the drawer. She left it out on the counter, in a busy office, on the Monday, where a queue could see the edge of it, which is not a policy and is not an act and is the first thing she has done in eleven years that she cannot explain to her council.

**The council sits at eight on the Wednesday and item three is the board, and she has never in eleven years taken a thing to the council that was not on the board already, and that is not modesty, it is the reason a district like this one can still say no at all.**

She drafted the notice in her head twice on the Tuesday. The first draft said *the office has been asked by a person about a transfer of the eleventh of Sleetmonth and the paper is not in the district's possession.* That was four lines and every one of them was true and it would have taken the council half an hour and produced a minute in which this ward had asked the council whether it still existed.

The second draft said *nothing*, and it was one line, and it was the one she sent to the meeting.

"There's a dead clerk's file," she said, "and a stranger came and read it, and I let him, and I have written it in my column with the hour. I am not asking the district about it. If I put eleven people in front of this council then in six months there will be a form about eleven people, and I have spent a fortnight watching this ward become the only thing in the basin that anybody can produce, and I am not doing the other half of it out of a good heart."

A man at the back asked whether the eleven were alive.

"I don't know," said Verna Sly. "That is the exact truth and I have had two days with it and I have not slept much and I have not written it anywhere except in a book in a drawer where a queue could see the edge of it, and if the ward's council wants to know whether eleven people are alive then the ward's council can come and look at the file, and there is one of us and it is me, and I am not going to be a woman who says I don't know to a room."

Nobody argued. It is the only unanimous minute in four years in the fourth ward, and it is a minute against doing anything, and it is the second of those in three weeks, and the eighth of those in twenty years, and every one of them was hers.

The four-foot-by-three board on the outside wall of that office still carries one page of foolscap in four clips and a heading in a clerk's hand, and it says what the fourth ward declines and why, and it does not say this, and she did not add to it, and there was a queue on the step by half past nine that morning with a man from a firm on it who had been told no once already in writing.
