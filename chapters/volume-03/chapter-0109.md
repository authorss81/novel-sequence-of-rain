# Chapter 109 — A Decision Every Friday

The ninth ward had to decide by noon on the Friday and it decided at ten past eleven, on the thirtieth day of Miremonth, which was the last day of a month this basin has not finished in since the Bellweather, in the district office, with four people in it, and the deciding took nine minutes, and the reason for it is a sentence Neris Kade has said about four times since and does not enjoy saying.

"A crew can stand in one place once. There are two answers on that shutter and they are about nine minutes apart at eight o'clock and three and a half hours apart by the time anybody's boots are on. So you take the one that lets the men be standing still, and it goes in the book, and you write why."

**They took Sable Orin's. It turned at the first hour and the flat was clear by half past six. The flat was in fact clear at about ten past seven, and Marek Vale's reading — the release holds to two o'clock — was also right, and had been all along, and the district did not know it for another day and a half, and nobody ever told the district, and that is the ordinary condition of a district that reads two shutters and one of them is painted by a man who cannot read his own glass.**

The gang went down at one. Six men and the water steward, which is the gang the ninth ward has and the only gang it has, and the water steward is Marda Loon and has been since her father's time. On that Friday morning she was down there with a lantern before the others and she was back before the light, and she did not write it up afterwards, because the thing she wrote it up in is the book and the book is a rota and a rota does not have a column for a day a man happened to be out.

The market's low yard is clear for the first time since the autumn. That is the visible good and it is not the district's to spend. It is four men's week taken out of a yard because the reach happened to be off, and the yard's foreman said what yard's foremen say, which is *that's your luck and not your doing*, and he was not unkind about it and he was not grateful either.

**The gang came back at about ten past four, wet to the knee, and one of the six, a man who has been on this rota since before the flood year, put his hand flat on the door frame of the pump house and said the thing the district book will not have.**

"We'd have been here Friday at six if anybody had told us Thursday."

"I know," said Marda Loon.

"And nobody could tell us Thursday, and that's not a complaint, because there was nothing to tell us Thursday. That's what I mean." He took his hand off the door frame. "There's no such thing as a Thursday. That's all."

She wrote the days in the book, because the days are what the book is for.

She walked up to the pump house on the Saturday morning with the lantern still in her hand because it wanted trimming and there is nobody else who will do it. It was the first day of Frostmonth, and the month had begun on a Friday, and the district has been getting used to saying the name for two days and has not settled into it. Marda Loon was in there with the book open on the sill, and the two of them looked at it for a while without saying anything much.

"You have it all in there," said Neris Kade.

"I have the days in it."

"Not the men."

"**I have never had the men in it.**" Marda Loon turned a page over and shut it again. "There is a day and there is a thing to be done and there is a lamp, and that is three things, and I have kept it since my father had this room and he kept it the same, and if you want a man's name on a Tuesday you will have to go and find a man, and he is on the low side at half past one and he will be there till three, and he does not want to be written down any more than I do."

"Was anybody ever asked for that book?"

Marda Loon looked up. "By who?"

That is the whole of it. Nobody had asked for it. The assessor had asked a glass foreman whether the Office had ever sent a district a book and had been told no, and the answer had been given by a woman with a rack key in her apron, and it had been about a shop. Nobody has asked Marda Loon for the book at the pump house, and the ninth ward's form described it in a line and a half as *in a book, and nobody has asked for it*, and that sentence has been in a form that went in the post two days before the men were standing on the flat.

And then the arithmetic of it, which she did on the flat with the lantern in her hand, which took her about four minutes, which nobody in the district office had thought of, and which she has not written down anywhere.

The clearing is two hours, Tuesdays and Thursdays, in the wet, on the low side and the two rain gardens and the back lanes. She has it in a book. Six men, twice a week, since before her father had the pump house, and a man with a lantern.

"If a crew can be told the water is off for a known number of hours," she said, "then the clearing is done in one go instead of four, and the four become one, and one is a morning."

"So we'd do it in one morning," said the district clerk, who is a young man with a fair hand and has never once been asked to keep a rota.

"Once. If we ever got a known morning. And we won't, and that is the whole of it, and don't write any of that down in a column, because there is no column and there is not going to be a column."

---

**The cost of the shutter came on the Tuesday, and it was not weather and it was not a reading and it was nobody's fault, and it was a woman's time.**

Ivy Tranter is fifty-eight, brings tea up a ladder at eight, and has copied the ninth ward's notice by hand since the twenty-ninth of Drownmonth because the tower lamp is out and the frame is a bubbled temporary that cannot show a state. The notice lives in four hand-copied shutters and a plate glass case at the school gate.

There were four places on Friday. There are five on Tuesday, and the fifth is on the same shutter as the fourth, in a different hand, underneath it, and it says the district is to decide by noon every Friday, and it does not say until when.

"That is the same to read," said Ivy Tranter, to the fourth shutter, which cannot answer. "Same two answers, same noon. But there is no last one on it, and I want to know whether there is a last one, because if there is not then I am a woman of fifty-eight doing a copy every Tuesday for the rest of my life, and nobody has asked me to, and I would go on doing it."

She went on doing it. Nobody has asked her. There is no column in the ninth ward's district book that takes a woman's Tuesdays.

The shilling came in on the Tuesday from the firm, and the tin stands at two and fourpence, and the oil is three and six and is bought on a Saturday by a man who has carried a can up that ladder twice since the eighteenth and has never been thanked. The lamp is still out. The keeper's evening hours for that week are a shilling and are unpaid. The only line any of it has ever got is in the pump hours column, and it says *27th. Lamp out at half past six. No oil. Keeper paid to the 27th.*

---

And on the Wednesday afternoon at about four o'clock, in an office with the door shut, Neris Kade asked for a pen and was given one and then said the thing she had come in to say.

"I have put a wall up with two answers on it and a Friday in it. I would like somebody to write down what I am about to say, because I am going to say it once and then I am not going to say it again, and there is nobody else in this basin I want to say it to."

The clerk put his pen down flat.

"A disagreement is not a schedule. A disagreement is two people being honest on a wall, and a wall is a place where a thing can be true without anybody having had to act on it. And now this ward is going to decide, every Friday, for as long as that shutter is up, whether a crew goes on a flat, and a district cannot run a rota on a thing two readers are allowed to change on a Wednesday night."

"Then what do we do?"

"We decide on the Friday, and the decision and the reason go in the book, and we do not pretend the other man was wrong, because he was not, and if he ever comes to this office we put his reading in front of the decision in the same column and let it stand. That is a rota. It has a Friday in it and a reason in it and two readings in it, and neither reading has been asked to agree with anything." She put her hand flat on the counter, the way people do. "And it is the first thing this ward has ever made that another person could be produced out of, and I have not got a word for what that is and I do not want one this month."

He wrote it down. One line in the district book, in the pump hours column, which is the only column that takes a man's hours, and it is the second time in a month that a thing which is not a man's hours has gone into it.

*The ward decides the flat each Friday off a wall, on two readings, both standing, neither withdrawn. Entered at the warden's own hand because there is nowhere else to put it.*

The page in her own notebook — the one headed *NINTH WARD — WHAT THE NOTICE DID NOT SAY* — is still seven lines and the eighth is still unwritten. She did not open it, and that was the second time this season she has not opened it, and the first time was about a lamp.

---

**She sent the same four words up the ridge road again at about half past four, with a runner and no signature, and it was the second time in a fortnight and the first time had not been answered either.**

***WHO DECIDES. N. K.***

He read it standing at the bench with the north window behind him and the lamp on. He did not answer it. The answer had been on a shutter in a ward's own hand four days earlier, and it was not his, and he read that too, and the two of them together are the whole of the answer, and neither of them is worth a shilling and neither of them is being paid for.

**There is a drawer on this bench with a brown paper envelope in it that is not tied and one sheet of district paper in it in a hand that copied nine hundred entries out of other people's hands for twenty-two years, and he has not written on it, and there are three blocks of pencil in the inside of his left cuff and no room in that cuff for a fourth.**
