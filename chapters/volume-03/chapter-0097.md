# Chapter 97 — A Minute Wants A Name

The Council's minutes come to the Hearthward district office on a Tuesday in a brown envelope with a printed head on it, and Kell Bray has read the same eleven pages every month for twenty years and can find the ninth ward's business with her thumb.

Today the ninth ward's business is item four, and item four is in a different level of language from the rest of the paper, and it is the only item in nine years of these minutes that asks a question instead of reporting one.

*Item four. That a district be asked in writing to state what it is able to do about its water, and be unable to answer, is a defect in the question and not in the district. The Board requires: (a) the office to which a district's statement is addressed; (b) the date of the last statement received from each ward; and (c) whether the date standing against a ward's line in the Standing Response Register is the date of a statement received or the date of a stub issued.*

She read (c) four times and then went and got the district's own copy of the direction of the twenty-third of Drownmonth, which is a printed sheet with a minute number on it and which she had read on the day it came and once since, and it says in paragraph one:

*That the stub be issued to each of the nine wards, in an envelope, addressed, with the season stated.*

**There is no envelope.** She checked, because she is the sort of clerk who checks, and the direction was issued on a Wednesday from a building four miles away, and the thing a direction like that needs in order to work is a piece of paper with a stamp on it that can be posted, and there is no such paper in the building, and there has never been one, and the direction is the first thing this Council has sent out of that building in a generation and it cannot be posted.

So the Register's dates are the dates the Register wrote them.

**Which means the second-writings box is empty for a reason, and the reason is not that nine districts declined.**

She wrote it out in the back of a form because that is what she does, in the fair hand of a woman who has been in a ledger since she was eleven years old, and it took four lines:

*Stub issued: Register writes the date.*
*Stub returned: none. Box empty.*
*Statement received: never, from anybody, because there is no addressed envelope and no form on which to state anything.*
*Therefore the eight dates on eight lines are the day the Register sent a piece of paper to a place that is not a building.*

And then, on a fifth line, because she had been a clerk for twenty years and had learned that the fifth line is the one anybody reads:

*Therefore the question in the minute cannot be answered out of a book. It is not a book question. Somebody's job was this.*

---

**Nobody at the Council can tell her, and she goes and finds that out in person, at about half past ten, in a corridor.**

The basin house has a press room on the ground floor and a hearing room off it and a corridor with four doors in it, and the Register is on a shelf in that corridor where a person may take it down without asking, and Bray has taken it down without asking four times this quarter.

"Mr. Skell. Item four. The office to which a district's statement is addressed."

Skell is a young man with a fair head and a manner that has been polished by about six years of a job in which he is mostly right. "The technical cell," he says. "The Register is a technical-cell instrument. The cell maintains it."

"Right. And who in the cell receives a district's statement?"

"Nobody," says Skell, and then hears it, and does the thing that a good clerk does, which is to say the next part out loud before the other person can. "Nobody receives it, because a district does not send one. A district sends nothing. The direction is a direction to a district. It goes out. It is the district's business what it does with it, and the cell does not hold district papers."

"Then the cell has no idea what eight of the nine wards did with it."

"No," says Skell. "The cell has an empty box, which is the same fact as that and in a better hand."

**And that is the whole of the answer and it is correct and it is a wall, and it is the same wall that has been in the same place since the twenty-third of Drownmonth, and it is made of one true sentence.**

Bray stood in the corridor for a moment with the Register in her hand and worked out what she would have to do to get an answer at all, and the answer was that she would have to go and ask nine district clerks, by hand, one at a time, because there is no schedule in this Council for receiving a list of anything and there is no office that holds a district's answer to a question about its own water.

**Twenty years of ledgers, and the thing that was missing was never a book. It was a person, and the person was not anybody's employee.**

---

**She wrote the letters at her own desk from about one o'clock, and she wrote nine of them, and they are the first form she has ever composed for this Council that is going out in the ordinary post.**

*Hearthward district office, [date].*

*To the clerk of the [x] ward.*

*The Weather Council has asked at item four of its minute of the twentieth of this month for the office to which a district's statement of what it is able to do about its water is addressed, and for the date of the last statement received from each ward, and for the ruling of the Board as to whether the date against a ward's line in the Standing Response Register is the date of a statement received or the date of a stub issued.*

*The Council's direction of the twenty-third day of Drownmonth asked each ward to state that, in a form, by the end of the term. The form is a stub. No ward has returned a stub, for want of an envelope, and no statement has been received by anybody, and the Register's dates are the day the Register wrote them.*

*So that a minute may be answered from a record and not from a memory, I ask one question of your district: when a reader or a keeper or any person at all brings a reading into the district, whose hand copies it into a book, what is that person's title in the district's own words, and on what date did they last do it.*

*The district may refuse to answer, or may answer in part. A refusal will be entered as a refusal and will not be held against the ward in any transaction. If your district keeps no such book, the answer is that it keeps no such book, and that is a complete answer and I would rather have it than none.*

*K. Bray, clerk, Hearthward district office.*

**She wrote it nine times and addressed eight envelopes before the light went, and she addressed the ninth one, and she put it in with the other eight, and then she took it out again.**

Because the ninth is Low Spindle, and Low Spindle is a district with four hundred and forty people on a low ground and a tower whose glass was lit yesterday for the first time since the middle of the Bellweather month and carries no legible state, and a warden who has been doing the work of a Council, a firm and a weather office on her own since the Bellweather month, and **there is not one thing in this letter that is the Council's business, and all nine letters are the same letter, and eight of them are asking a district about its own past and the ninth one would be asking a district about its own past by a different route.**

The whole difference is that the first eight go to a clerk who is answerable to a council and will be minuted whatever she says, and the ninth goes to a warden's office with a warden in it and a page in a drawer and eight lines on a page of which she cannot write the ninth.

*You cannot be compensated for a thing you have not got written down, and you cannot get it written down afterwards, and the only time anybody will let you write it down is before you need it.* She heard that sentence in a room on the fifteenth of Drownmonth, in somebody else's voice, and she has never written it down herself, and she thought about it now in a district office with eight envelopes and a ninth.

**A district is a person. A clerk is a person. One of those two can be asked a question and the other one has to be asked to her face.**

She put the eight envelopes in her satchel and the ninth in her coat, and signed the book, and put the book back, and walked down the hill in the rain at four o'clock because the post goes at half four and because she had decided to walk down there and hand it over rather than post it, and walking down there took fifty minutes and standing outside took eleven of them.

---

**The district office in the ninth ward is a room over a locker's yard with a notice board on the outside of it and a bell in it, and at twenty past five on a wet Tuesday in this city the lamps inside are lit and you can see them from the low lane, and the woman at the counter had a pen behind her ear and would have taken a letter, and Bray stood on the pavement opposite for eleven minutes and did not cross the road.**

Not out of politeness. Because the door is nine minutes' walk each way for a woman with a child in this weather, which is a thing she has written about in other districts, and because she had come down a hill with a letter in her coat and she had not decided what she was going to say if the warden said *what is it*, and she had been in a room four days ago and watched a woman with a good hand refuse a district's own figure for a reason that was correct, and she had understood at the time, and had not understood until the pavement.

**The letter asks a district to say the name of a person who used to be its employee.** That is not the same question as *what can you do about your water*, and a warden who has spent a season being asked the first by a Council, a firm, a market and a printing press, in that order, is entitled to hear a clerk of another district say why she has come down a hill on her own time before she says anything else.

So she went home. It rained. The eight letters went in the post at half past four from a box on the corner, one for each of the other wards, and the ninth is in a coat on a hook by a door in Hearthward, and the post takes days, and it took three days for the first answer and it was not the one she had hoped for and it was the one that mattered.

**And the thing she noticed on the walk back up the hill, at about ten to six, with her coat closed over it, was that she has just composed a form, put it in the post, and expected nine districts to fill it in, and that nobody has refused it, and that the reason nobody has refused it is that nobody has seen it yet.**

It went out at half past four. Somebody sold a district's own reach hours to a printing firm for a shilling a week in ninety seconds in a room with a clerk in it, and three weeks later a clerk of another district posted nine letters to the ordinary post without a vote, a council, a minute, or a second reading of anything, and both of those happened in the same basin inside three weeks, and one of them is going to be on a wall in a fortnight, and the other one is going to be in nine letter trays, and neither of them has anything to do with the other, and there is no column in the world that says a thing done for nothing is the same as a thing done because it was asked.

She did not write that down either. She got in at about ten past seven and hung the coat up and took the ninth letter out of the pocket and put it in the drawer with the other eight stubs that nobody will ever return, which are not the Council's and are hers, and which she keeps because a clerk keeps what comes to her desk.
