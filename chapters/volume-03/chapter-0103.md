# Chapter 103 — An Office Not Re-established

Perdie Alvay is sixty-four, has chaired the sixth ward for nineteen years, has never once used a gavel she was given in her second year, and carries a book to every meeting of everything she has ever chaired.

The book is about two inches thick. It is a minute book and it is not the ward's minute book; the ward's minute book is in the ward office and has a spine on it. Hers has no spine, because she had it rebound herself in her first year because a chair who loses her own book has lost the only thing that was hers.

**It is the only complete record of what the sixth ward has ever said out loud about its water, and it was never sent to anybody, and it is not going to be.**

The letter came on the Thursday from a clerk of Hearthward with a minute number on it and nine words of covering politeness, and it asked one question, and Perdie Alvay read the question four times and then got a sheet of ward paper and answered it herself, in her own hand, which is a broad slanted hand that has got worse over nineteen years and is perfectly legible.

*To the clerk of the Hearthward district office.*

*Sir or Madam. The sixth ward employed a sheet clerk for twenty-two years. Her name is Hester Skeat. She was engaged in the spring of the year of the first floods and her duty was to copy into the ward's book every reading brought to the district by a reader, a keeper or any other person, together with the hour, and to keep the book, and to answer any question a ward meeting put about the water of this ward.*

*That office was discharged from the ward's budget at the end of the season that ended in Miremonth, two years ago. It was not abolished by a vote and no person was dismissed. The line was simply not in the estimate when the estimate was made.*

*The last entry in her hand is the twenty-second of that Miremonth. The book after that is in my hand and in the district clerk's and it is not a copy.*

*I do not know where the book is and I will not say that she has it, because I have not asked her, and because a ward that has put a woman out of a job for twenty-two years and then written to her and asked where her book is has asked her for two things at once.*

— P. Alvay, chair, sixth ward.*

**She read it twice when it was written and then she went and looked in the drawer where the ward's papers that nobody can produce go, and the drawer had a name on it that she had put there herself in her first year, and the envelope in it said SEASON RETURNS, QUERY, nothing more.**

---

**Here is the whole of the fourth year, and she had it out on the table in about four minutes, because she is a woman who knows where things are.**

Four years of the sixth ward's readings, in six or seven hands, in a folder, on district paper, in no particular order, with the folds of a ward office's drawer across them: about nine hundred sheets.

*3rd M., yr 4. A reader of the south side. Corridor, off and on, third to sixth hour. Cannot say where. Entered.*
*9th M., yr 4. Marda Loon, water steward. Reach low at 06:10, clear by 09:00. Entered.*
*19th M., yr 4. Nothing brought.*

**Four hundred sheets and some of this. Four years of a ward's own water, kept by a wage of about thirty-one pounds a year, in a wage book and a drawer, and then, when the estimate was made, the wage was not in it and the four hundred sheets went into an envelope in a drawer with a label on it that Alvay wrote herself.**

The line before that, on the same day, two years ago, is a letter from the district clerk to the Council's technical cell, and it is four lines, and it is correct, and it is the single most frightening document in the ward's file:

*The ward offers to the Council its readings for the past four years, being about nine hundred sheets, and asks that they be held with the ward's line in the Standing Response Register. The ward has no facility for their keeping.*

And the answer, four lines, in a different hand, dated eleven days later:

*This office does not hold district papers. A district's local record is a local record. No arrangement is available.*

**Nothing was wrong with that answer. It is the same sentence a clerk of the Council wrote in a corridor this month, about a minute item, in another building, four years later, and it is the answer that this building gives to every question of this shape, and the ward asked eleven days before the estimate for its own memory to be taken into a place, and was told that it could not.**

The ward also offered them to a firm and got a reply in nine days which thanked the ward and said the firm did not hold district records. The ward also offered them to a newspaper, and a reporter came and took notes for forty minutes and wrote two hundred words about a ward with a good heart, and did not want the sheets.

**The sheets are in the drawer. That is where they have been for two years. Nobody has asked for them. There is no form.**

---

**The district meeting on the Monday night was eleven people and a gavel that was not used, and item four was in it because Alvay put it in it.**

"Mrs. Hesk," she said, "you will want to know what the sixth ward is going to put in the Council's form, and I am going to tell you now so that nobody has to be surprised by it on Thursday."

The room waited.

"The ward is going to write one page and send it to the Council, and the page is going to be true, and it is going to be the only thing about this ward's water that has left this room in two years." She put her hand flat on the book. "And the ward is not going to send this."

**"Ma'am—"**

"Listen to the reason, because the reason is the only interesting thing I have got and I have been sitting on it for a week." She turned the book round and did not open it. "Everything I know about the water of this ward for nineteen years is in that book, and the whole of it was written down by me, in this room, at this table, at a meeting, for a meeting, and by a man who came to a meeting. **And if this room had been cancelled, we would have had no record at all, and the record would have been complete.**"

The room took a moment.

"That is what it is for," said Alvay. "It is not a return. It is not a schedule and it is not a resource. It is a thing I have to bring, and I bring it because if I do not bring it then nobody in this ward can say in ten years what we actually said, and a thing nobody can say is a thing that was never said. And that is *precisely* why it cannot be sent to a Council."

**"Because the moment this ward puts a written list of what it can do in front of a public body, that list has a price on it by the following quarter, and it will not be our price.** Watch what has happened to a ward in this basin that did exactly that, in the winter, and did nothing wrong at any point in the doing of it. It was asked a question in a form. It answered the form in a fair hand. A councillor put it in his coat, and he carried it down to a counting floor in daylight and sold it for eleven shillings, and that is the whole of the crime, and there was no villain in it and there was a vote. **And that ward has a wall in its third season and a tower that is dark and a tin with a lamp in it, and a Council paid it seven and six for a morning it gave up at somebody else's price and refused it its own price of one and four for want of a record, and then a firm printed that figure with the receipt for the eleven shillings underneath it and the ward's name at the top, in a print shop, on a public wall.** This ward has a wall and a market and a gauge in a master's shed. **So the book stays on this table, and the page goes to the Council, and the page is a page, and a page can be read and a book can be sold.**"

Nobody argued with her, and nobody applauded, and a man of about sixty at the back said, "Then what is the point of us keeping it, ma'am, if it can't be used."

**"The point," she said, "is that it is ours. A thing that is ours can be used by us, on a Tuesday, when we want to. You have all sat in a room where a thing that was everybody's was settled by one man and there was nothing anybody could bring to the next meeting. And if it is handed over now, it is a thing somebody has, and in a year somebody will have taken a page out of it, and it will have been for a good reason, and the good reason will be in a minute.**"

Nobody had an answer to that, and the chair of the council, who had come to be against it, said the one thing that moved her, and he said it in about four seconds and then sat down again.

"There's a lady in the back row who's been on this council nineteen years and hasn't spoken in two of them," he said, "and she wants the book offered. On request. To a district. Not to a Council and not to a firm and not to a newspaper."

**And the minute of the sixth ward, which is an ordinary minute book with a spine on it, records that a person asked, and the minute of the sixth ward chair, which is her own, records in the middle of a page, which is not where minutes are written, the following, and it was read out before it was initialled:**

*Book retained. Offered for the use of a district, on request. Not sent.*

**And Perdie Alvay picked up the gavel, for the only time in nineteen years, and put it down again without striking anything, and that is the only time it has come out of the case.**

**The page that went to the Council on the Wednesday is four lines long, and it names one person, and the four lines say only that this ward cannot answer item four and that its book is retained. It is the only page in this basin in a hundred and forty years that says a district could not tell a Council what it could do, and it says it in the form the Council sent. The book went nowhere, and never will, except on request, to a district.**

It went in the post with a minute number on the covering line and it cost twopence, and the sixth ward's minute book says *Item 4. Page sent. Book retained. Offered on request. Unanimous*, and the unanimous is true, and it is the only unanimous minute in four years, and nobody thanked the chair.
