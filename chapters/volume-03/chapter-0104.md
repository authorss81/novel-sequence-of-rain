# Chapter 104 — The Book In The Cupboard

Hester Skeat is sixty-one and has never in her life been anybody's idea of anything, and the first thing she says when a stranger on the ridge in the wet asks her name is the second thing, which is that she is not on a list and there is not a list, and she says it in a voice that closes about four inches of the conversation.

The second thing is: "You'll want the book. I'm not having the book."

"You've come about a book."

"I've come about a woman who used to copy readings into a book for the sixth ward, and I think you're her. The letter is four lines and it's signed by a chair, and I've met the chair once, at a meeting, in 'sixty-four, and I was the copy clerk at that meeting and I said nothing, which is what copy clerks do." She puts the bag down on the step. "And the book isn't in this house any more in the sense that anybody means by a book, and if that's the end of it I'll go and be telephoned at a different hour."

She lets him in, and he is about thirty and he has a strip of plate in a case that he keeps touching, and she does not ask him his name and he does not offer it, which she will think about afterwards and decide was a courtesy. It is two rooms and a stair and a yard with a line across it, and there is a book on the dresser under a cloth, and she does not look at it while she is talking, which takes about nine minutes and is the most direct thing anybody has done to him in four months.

"Twenty-two years," she says. "Spring they hired me off a list at an office in the west end, I'd done a season's copying for the water office for twelve shillings, and they wanted somebody who could read a hand. Twenty-two. And the last one was the twenty-second of Miremonth, two years back, and the wage stopped at the end of that season, and there was no conversation."

"You were let go."

"**There was no wage in the estimate.** That's the whole of it and it's not soft, it's a budget. They put my line in for twenty-two years and then in the year they made the estimate there was not a paragraph for me in it, and nobody rang, because there's no procedure for ringing. There's a procedure for a man who's in a dispute and a procedure for a woman who's pregnant and I don't think there's a procedure for a line that simply isn't there any more."

"And the book."

---

**"I took it home, and I'll tell you exactly why, and it is not because I thought it was important."**

She takes the cloth off it. It is a foolscap book with a printed head and a spine, and the head says *Ward of the Sixth — Notices and Readings Received*, and there is a ruled column for the day, the hour, the person bringing it, what was brought, and where it was put.

"Three months after the wage stopped I went down to the ward office to see whether anybody had picked the book up, and they hadn't, and the district clerk said nobody knew whose it was, and that was true, and about six weeks after that the drawer started having a good many things in it that nobody could find, because nobody could find them, because there was no book to find them in."

"So you kept it."

"I kept it because after about four months of that I noticed the ward had stopped writing anything down at all, and I'm the one who wrote it down, and there's nobody in that ward now who could tell you what the water did on a Tuesday in the year the budget came round." She squares the book on the dresser. "**There are nine hundred entries in that book and nobody has ever seen one of them, and every one of them is right, because I wrote every one of them out myself off the sheet they brought me.**"

---

**And then she says the thing that the whole of this month is about, and she says it without any weight at all, the way you say a thing you have said to yourself many times.**

"You'll be after what the ward could do, and I'll tell you now that you'll not find it in here, and I'd say the same to the Council, the firm, the shop and the vicar. **I only ever wrote down what somebody brought me.**"

She put her hand flat on the cover.

"Eight or nine people, all told. The steward. Two or three men of the ward's own who could read a glass well enough. A reader out of Hearthward for about two years, twice a week, whose name I never had and did not ask for. A keeper off the north wall in the winter, when he was on the wall, about six times. A man from a mill with a level he wanted compared."

"And if nobody brought anything that day?"

"Then the line says *nothing brought this day*, and I wrote that at the time, in the column, and I want you to have that in front of you before you go anywhere, because **there is not a single day in twenty-two years in that book that says we had nothing. It says nobody brought anything.** Those are not the same and only one of them is true and I never knew which."

---

**"You've read it since," he said. "Two years. Why would you read it?"**

"Because it's mine and there's nobody else," said Hester Skeat. "And because a man of my age with a book he has never looked at finds out what's in it at the wrong time."

She did not read it every night. She read it perhaps twice a year, and she has three things, and she gives them in the order she thinks of them and not in the order of how much they are worth.

"One. There's three hundred and about sixty days in twenty-two years with *nothing brought this day* in them, and a hundred and sixty of those are in the last four years of my time and they're not scattered, they're in the last **eleven months**, near enough every week. **So the ward went quiet. Not the water. The ward.** And I can tell you which eleven months and I cannot tell you why, and I have sat here with the book open on my knee trying to work out whether they stopped because there was nothing to say or because there was nobody left to say it, and I have not got to the bottom of that in two years and I have not told a soul in this house that it bothers me."

"Two."

"Two. A reader out of Hearthward. Twice a week, two years, and never once a place, and never once a name, and the entries are the best work I have ever done and the least useful. A young fellow with a glass of his own he could not read and would not be told how to hold, and he was in twice a week whatever the weather was, and he never once waited to be asked. **And when he stopped, there is no line. There's a Tuesday in the spring and then the Wednesday, and the Wednesday says *nothing brought this day*, and that's the whole of the end of it, and I could not tell you to within a season when he stopped or why.**"

"And three."

"Three's the one you won't believe," said Hester Skeat, "and it's the one I've thought about most."

---

**"The ward's own men used to bring me a place. Every time, for twenty-two years. *The low side of the quay, from the second bridge. The third hour.* A place, because if a warden has to act on it at four in the morning he can't go out and find a corridor, and a ward knows its own streets, and a man of the ward will say the street because the street is his business."**

She opened the book at a page near the front and turned it round and put her finger on it, and it was a sheet in a hand she knew, and it said a corridor and hours and *below the north wall*, and no place at all.

"And then somebody would come in from outside the ward — the Hearthward woman, the keeper, the man off the mill — and they would give me a corridor and hours and nothing else. **And I wrote it down. Twenty-two years, about nine hundred sheets, and there is not one line in that book in my hand where I asked anybody where they meant.**"

The room was very quiet.

"**That was the whole of it. I was the place where a corridor became a street, and I never asked, because a ward clerk asking a reader to be more particular is a ward clerk telling a reader it's not good enough, and nobody ever told me that was what I was doing, and I would have asked. I'd have asked the first week.**" She shut the book. "And I'm not going to sit here and be sorry about it in front of a stranger, because if I was doing it then I was doing it every day of the twenty-two years and I did it well, and the ward got nine hundred sheets out of it and the ward could not use a single one of them without a Council's form and an addressed envelope that nobody in this city has ever had made."

---

**"The chair says she hasn't asked you where it is."**

"She's right not to have. And if I was her I'd not." Hester Skeat turned the book over and put the cloth back on it. "A ward that puts a woman out of a job at the end of a season and then writes and asks where her book is has asked her for two things at once, and I should think nothing of the second one and there's a man of my age who would have gone straight down there and handed it over the counter inside a week, and I have thought about that and I am not him and I have not been him since the day the wage stopped, and I would rather say that out loud than have it be found out in a minute."

"So it isn't in this house."

"**It isn't in this house in the sense anybody means. There's the dry one in the dresser and there's a wet one under the stairs, because this is a rainward city and the dry one is for reading in and the wet one is for knowing, and if you'd looked in the dresser you'd have seen one of the two, and the one in the dresser is not the one you want.**" She said it flatly. "You'll not have the wet one. And I'll tell you the reason now so it's written down that I said it, because reasons that are said afterwards are a different animal from reasons that are said first."

**"There is one of these in the world. If it goes into a press there is one of it and no second, and a thing with one of it is a thing that can be altered, and if it's altered it will be altered by somebody who is not on a table, and there would be exactly one person alive who could say so, and she would be sixty-one and she would not be on a wage. And you cannot ask a district for that and a Council cannot buy it and a firm certainly could if they ever knew it existed.**"

"So it stays."

"It stays in a house, in a town, in a family, and it is read about twice a year by a woman who was very good at it, and there is no form for it and no copy of it and no bell for it and nobody has ever asked me for it, and that is the only condition under which it is any use to anybody at all."

---

**"There is one more thing and then you can go and do whatever it is you're going to do," said Hester Skeat, "and I want it said in a room and not in a minute, because a minute is a thing they can unminute.**

"**You are going to go away from here with an idea that a person held a pen and stopped, and that is true, and I am that person, and the ward discharged my wage, and the wage was thirty-one pounds and my own hands, and none of it is a mystery and none of it is a conspiracy and there is no villain in it anywhere. I was a woman who was good at a job. The job went. The book came home with me.**

"And if you were to write that down somewhere, and if you were to do it in a way that made it sound like a thing that had been hidden — **I have not hidden anything, and the ward hasn't, and there is nothing in this house but two books and a chair and a woman who cannot read a glass, and the books were never locked because there was never any reason to lock them.**"

"Then what is the reason for all of this," he said, "if nobody hid it?"

"**Because nobody asked.**" She said it without any weight at all. "That's all. Twenty-two years and eleven months and nine hundred sheets, and nobody ever asked, not one time, not a soul, not the ward, not the Council, not the firm, not the man from the mill. **And that is not a conspiracy, it's a Tuesday.**"

**And she took the book off the dresser again, and put it into a brown paper parcel, and did not tie it, and said:**

"Now go on. You've got a wet morning and I'm not doing this for the sake of a minute and if you come back at six I'll be here, and if you come at eight I'll not."
