# Chapter 115 — Four Lines A Week

*Daily, district notices received.* Book eleven, the fourth year of it in his hand and the eleventh in hers, and the head is printed, and the head does not say what office either of them was in.

Four lines a week. That is the whole of the book, and it is the whole of what this Council has ever received from a district in eleven years.

*Monday. From the third ward, one sheet, no enclosure. Replied.*
*Wednesday. From the second ward, nothing this week.*
*Friday. From the fifth ward, one request for a form. None held. Replied.*
*Monday. From the fourth ward, one request for a form. None held. Replied.*

The requests are the only lines in eleven years that anybody has ever sent this Council. They are all the same request. The district in them is always the same district. The book is a record of things received by a Council, and nobody has ever lodged anything with this Council.

The columns are four. Day, from, what was received, and where it was put.

He had been reading the copy for nine minutes and had got to the eleventh of Sleetmonth, and he read the line and read it again and then said a word his mother would not have liked.

*11th Sleetmonth. From the fourth ward, one sheet, no enclosure.*

"It's here," said Wend Lyle.

"It's here," said Tovan Iles. "That's what here is. That's the line."

"Where did it go?"

"The fourth column."

He looked at the fourth column. The fourth column was empty.

Not empty everywhere. She had told him on Friday that there were entries that carried it and entries that did not, and she had been right. Perhaps one line in eight had a place in it. *West end, press 1. Loose, string shelf. Press 2, upper tray.* The hand changed across the four years the way a hand does when two men take turns, and the later of the two wrote in it more.

The eleventh of Sleetmonth, two winters back, has a line in the first three columns and nothing at all in the fourth. It is the only line in the four years of the man before her with the column left empty, and she has read it every working day for four years and has never once known which day it is.

"There's your paper," she said. "That's the only thing in this building that has ever said it existed."

---

She sat down on the end of her own desk, which she does not do, and told him the rest of it in about ninety seconds, and it is the only time in four years she has said any of it aloud.

"Two days after that line there is a Friday. On the thirteenth, from the Council, going out: four lines, replied. The book does not say what the four lines were, because the book is a book of what this office received and not a book of what it said, and there is no column for that either, and I have never thought about that in four years and I have thought about nothing else this week."

He sat down on the third stool.

"So the Council received a district's paper," he said. "And then the Council wrote back and said it does not hold district papers."

"Four lines. I have never seen them. They are in the post room in a tied bundle by the year, and they are not indexed, and they are not a record, and I would not be permitted to look in the bundle and I would not want to. And the fortnight after that the fourth ward wrote asking about a form, and this office answered it in four lines, and the district clerk wrote the day and the week in his own file, and then, on the Friday, he put it in the basket."

"The basket in the corridor outside the press room."

"The basket in the corridor outside the press room. It is emptied on a Friday. There is a return in the rule book. A line for each Friday and a man's signature against it. The return does not require anybody to look at the basket, or to know what was in it, and I had the rule book in my hand on Tuesday with a boy who wanted to know if anybody reads it."

"No," said Wend Lyle.

"No." She got up and went back to the third desk and picked up her pen. "It does not require anybody to look. That is the sentence, and it is the whole of it, and it is printed, and it is not anybody's fault, and it has been true for longer than either of us."

---

He did the arithmetic in the basin house corridor at about ten past two in the afternoon, standing up, with a sheet of the firm's paper on his knee, and it took him about eleven minutes and nobody watched him do it.

Eleven years. Four lines a week. A book this Council maintains, prints and locks, and has never once read, and which records in the plainest language in the world every single thing that has ever crossed that counter from a district.

A ward asked for a form eleven times in four years and was told there was none.

A ward sent one sheet, once, on the eleventh of Sleetmonth, and the Council received it, and the Council has never held it, and the Council does not hold district papers, and the Council is right, and all of it is true, and the only object in this basin that has ever said a paper existed is a book in the west end with a printed head and an empty column in it.

And the eleven people went off the low side of the fourth ward in a boat with a lamp, because of something nobody could name. There are two pieces of paper in the whole of Veyra that say so. One is a line in a dead clerk's file in a goods yard, which a boy of nineteen read standing up. The other is on the sheet in his hand, in a printed column with a gap in it, and it has been there two winters and nobody has ever read it.

He copied the eleven days out himself before he left, in his own hand, and initialled it, because a firm's copy is made in the office that made it.

Then he wrote, at the foot of it, in a hand that was not his best:

*Held by us: nothing. Not held by us. Not held by anybody. Held in a book in the west end of another building, under a printed head, in a column left empty once in four years, by a man who signs the first of the month four times a year and is not anybody in that building now.*

**And the eleven words after *not held by anybody* are a lie, and he did not know it was a lie for a day and a half, and it is the only thing he wrote that month that was not true, and he did not take it out, because taking it out would have turned it into a firm's opinion of this Council's records, and a firm's clerk is not allowed an opinion of this Council's records.**

---

He came back on the Thursday with a card from his own firm that said *verifying, reference*, and he read his eleven days over again, and then he asked her for something he had not asked for on the first day and had not had the nerve to ask for on the first day.

"I want a page of the fourth column."

"That is not on your slip."

"I know it is not on my slip."

"Then it is a page of a Council book and this is a Council book and it is not for a firm." She said it without heat. "What is it for?"

"To see whether the column is full or whether it is mostly empty. That is all. Whether a man would have to go through it."

**Tovan Iles took a clean sheet off the pad, which took her about a second, and copied out a page of the fourth column in her fair hand, and it is the only time in four years she has copied a column out of that book that nobody had asked her to copy, and it took her about four minutes, and the four minutes are not on anybody's slip, including her own.**

He read it standing at the end of her counter. Press one, press two, press three, the string shelf, the upper tray, a string shelf, a press, press two — seven lines out of eight, in one hand, in the same four years, done by a man who cared about it and did it well and signed nothing.

"Seven in eight," he said.

"Seven in eight," said Tovan Iles, "and I have counted them, and I have been counting them since the second year of this book, and I have never told a managing clerk a single word about it because I am a copy clerk and that is a copy clerk's business. **And there is one blank. One cell, in four years, in one column, in a book that this Council prints, keeps, and has never once read.**"

"Which line?"

"**I have never known which line it is,**" she said. "I have never been able to find it, and I have looked, and I have never told anybody, and I am telling a boy off a firm's second floor about it on a Thursday afternoon because he asked me whether the column was full instead of telling me what he wanted in it."

---

**And the rest of it he worked out on the Friday in a corridor of the basin house, standing up, with a sheet of the firm's paper on his knee, in about eleven minutes, and nobody watched him do it.**

The first is that the date is no use at all. The book has no month heads and no year heads. It has four lines a week, the days of the week, and the number of the month, and it runs on continuously from a year before his own time, so that the eleventh of a month called Sleetmonth occurs eleven times in the eleven years, and there is nothing whatever to tell one from another, and the sheet is in one of them and he has no way on earth to say which.

"That is the worst of it," he said, to nobody, on the Friday. "The paper's date is worth nothing. Anybody who went in there with a date and a good pair of eyes would come out with eleven answers."

Then, at about half past three, on the same corridor: "It does not need a date, and it does not need a name, and it does not need anybody's permission. It needs a man with a week and a steady eye and a reason to go through a column of blanks, and there is no reason in the world for anybody to go through a column of blanks, and that is the only reason it is still there."

"So the Council has printed, in its own hand, under its own head, the one thing in this basin that can be found without asking a person, and it is an empty space, and nobody filled it in because there was nothing to fill it in with, and that is the whole of how eleven people got lost."

---

**And he did not write the fourth thing down anywhere, which was that the blank and the eleventh of Sleetmonth are the same line, and that he has proved it by a thing he has not got, which is the year; and that a man who leaves one gap in four years is a man who was in a hurry; and that a man in a hurry in a records room in a year when nobody came is a man who did not have a press in his hand.**

He did not write it down because he is nineteen, and because it was an opinion, and because on the Friday at about half past four his foreman came up the stairs and asked him where the fourth ward's file copy had gone, and he said it was in the drawer with the other two, and his foreman said, "Good. That's where a record goes," and went down again.

"It isn't a record," said Wend Lyle, to nobody, in an office with four desks in it.

She kept the slip. It went in the press with the others, and the page of the fourth column went in with it, in her own hand, undated and uninitialled and not on anybody's slip, and there is a box in the west end of that room, and nobody in Veyra could say how many of them are in it, including her.
